The Marketing Mix

The marketing mix is the set of controllable tools a business combines to produce the response it wants from its target market. The traditional framework groups these tools into four elements, often called the 4Ps: product, price, place and promotion.

Product refers to the goods or services the firm offers, including features, quality, design, branding and packaging. A product is more than its physical form: customers buy the benefits it delivers. For example, a buyer of a power bank is really buying uninterrupted phone use during a power outage.

Price is the amount customers pay to obtain the product. It is the only element of the mix that produces revenue; the other elements represent costs. Common pricing approaches include cost-plus pricing, where a fixed margin is added to unit cost, and value-based pricing, where the price reflects the customer's perception of value.

Place covers the activities that make the product available to target customers. Decisions about place include the choice of distribution channels, coverage, locations, inventory and transport. A short channel moves goods directly from producer to consumer, while a long channel passes through wholesalers and retailers.

Promotion means the activities that communicate the product's merits and persuade target customers to buy it. The promotion mix includes advertising, personal selling, sales promotion and public relations. Sales promotion offers short-term incentives such as discounts, coupons or free samples to encourage purchase.

Extending the Mix for Services

Services differ from goods because they are intangible, cannot be stored, and are often produced and consumed at the same time. For this reason, the services marketing mix adds three further elements: people, process and physical evidence, giving 7Ps in total.

People are the employees who deliver the service and interact with customers; their training and attitude shape the customer's experience. Process describes the procedures and flow of activities by which the service is delivered. Physical evidence is the tangible environment and cues, such as a clean waiting area or a printed receipt, that help customers judge an intangible service.

Using the Mix Consistently

The elements of the mix must support one another. A premium price, for instance, needs a high-quality product, selective distribution and promotion that emphasises exclusivity. If one element contradicts the others, customers receive a confused message and the positioning strategy fails.
