## Topic 10: Standard Costing

**What you must know:** what standard costing and a variance are; the four types of standards; how standards are set and recorded on a standard cost card; behavioural aspects; standard costing versus budgets; direct material variances (price, usage, cost) and direct labour variances (rate, idle time, efficiency, cost); when to investigate a variance; reconciling standard cost with actual cost.

### Lesson 10.1: Nature of standard costing and variances

**Must know**

- **Standard cost:** a predetermined (planned) cost of one unit of product or service, worked out in advance from expected quantities and prices of materials, labour and overheads under stated working conditions.
- **Standard costing:** a control technique that uses predetermined standards for costs and revenues, compares them with actual results, and analyses the differences (variances) by cause so that management can act.
- **Variance:** the difference between a planned, budgeted or standard cost and the actual cost (and similarly for revenue).
    - **Favourable (F):** actual cost is **less** than standard (or actual revenue more than standard). Raises profit.
    - **Adverse (A):** actual cost is **more** than standard (or actual revenue less). Reduces profit.
- **Variance analysis:** breaking a total variance into its parts (for example price and usage) to find causes and responsibility.
- **Management by exception:** managers give attention only to significant variances (the exceptions); results that match standard need no action.
- **Standard hour:** the quantity of work that should be done in one hour at standard performance. Output is often measured in standard hours.

**Steps in a standard costing system**

1. Set standards for each element of cost per unit.
2. Record actual costs and actual output.
3. Compare actual cost with the standard cost of actual output.
4. Calculate and analyse variances by cause.
5. Report variances to the responsible managers.
6. Investigate significant variances and take corrective action (or revise the standard).

**Advantages of standard costing**

1. Provides a yardstick for measuring performance and efficiency.
2. Enables control through variance analysis and management by exception.
3. Fixes responsibility on named managers for named variances.
4. Simplifies stock valuation and bookkeeping (stock is held at standard cost).
5. Helps in budgeting, pricing and quotations.
6. Makes staff cost-conscious and encourages efficiency.

**Limitations of standard costing**

1. Setting standards is costly and time-consuming.
2. Standards go out of date quickly when prices change (for example under high inflation) and need regular revision.
3. Best suited to standardised, repetitive production; hard to use for one-off jobs.
4. Variances can be interdependent, so blame may fall on the wrong manager.
5. Unrealistic standards demotivate staff.
6. It focuses on cost and may ignore quality, delivery and customer satisfaction.

**Explain more**

- Standard costing uses predetermined standards for costs and revenues so that control is exercised through variance analysis. Do not confuse it with **marginal costing** (charges only variable costs to units), **job costing** (costs each customer order separately) or **historical costing** (records costs after they happen, with no target to compare against).
- A standard is a **unit** figure; a budget is a **total** figure. Standard cost per unit × budgeted output = budgeted cost.
- Favourable does not always mean good. A favourable material price variance from buying cheap leather can cause an adverse usage variance through extra wastage (see Lesson 10.4).

### Lesson 10.2: Types of standards, setting standards and the standard cost card

**Must know**

**Four types of standards**

1. **Ideal standard:** assumes perfect conditions: no wastage, no machine breakdown, no idle time, maximum efficiency. Almost never achieved; always gives adverse variances.
2. **Attainable (expected) standard:** can be achieved under efficient operating conditions, with allowances for normal wastage, normal idle time and normal breakdowns. Most widely used; best for motivation and control.
3. **Basic standard:** set once and left unaltered over a long period. Useful for showing trends over years; not useful for current control, because it becomes out of date.
4. **Current standard:** based on current working conditions (current wastage, current efficiency, current prices). Set for a short period; realistic but may build current inefficiency into the target.

| Standard | Achievable? | Effect on staff | Main use |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ideal | Rarely | Demotivates, constant adverse variances | Shows the target of perfection |
| Attainable | Yes, with effort | Motivates best | Control, budgeting, stock valuation |
| Basic | Changes with time | Meaningless for control | Long-run trend analysis |
| Current | Yes | Little challenge if conditions are poor | Short-term control |

**Setting standards (principles)**

- **Material quantity:** from engineering or technical specifications of the product, with an allowance for normal loss (wastage, evaporation, scrap). Set by engineers and production staff.
- **Material price:** from the purchasing department, using expected market prices, quantity discounts, delivery costs and expected inflation for the period.
- **Labour time:** from work study (time and motion study) of each operation, with allowance for rest, fatigue and normal idle time. Expressed in standard hours.
- **Labour rate:** from the personnel (human resources) department, using agreed wage rates, union agreements and the grade of labour needed.
- **Overheads:** from overhead budgets: standard rate = budgeted overhead ÷ budgeted standard hours (or units).

**Generation of standard cost information:** standards are drawn from technical specifications, work study, purchasing records and price forecasts, personnel records and wage agreements, past cost records, and overhead budgets. A **standards committee** (production, purchasing, personnel, engineering and the cost accountant) usually agrees them. They are recorded on a standard cost card and reviewed regularly, at least yearly.

**Standard cost card:** a document showing, for one unit, the standard quantity and standard price of each element of cost and the total standard cost. Format: element; quantity; price or rate; ₦ cost.

**Explain more**

**Worked example: standard cost card.** An Aba shoe factory makes a standard leather sandal ("Model A").

| Element | Standard quantity | Standard price/rate | ₦ per pair |
|---|---|---|---|
| Direct material: leather | 2 sq m | ₦1,500 per sq m | 3,000 |
| Direct labour | 3 hours | ₦800 per hour | 2,400 |
| **Standard prime cost** | | | **5,400** |
| Variable production overhead | 3 hours | ₦200 per hour | 600 |
| Fixed production overhead | 3 hours | ₦400 per hour | 1,200 |
| **Standard production cost** | | | **7,200** |

Each line is quantity × price: 2 × ₦1,500 = ₦3,000; 3 × ₦800 = ₦2,400; 3 × ₦200 = ₦600; 3 × ₦400 = ₦1,200. Total = ₦3,000 + ₦2,400 + ₦600 + ₦1,200 = ₦7,200. This card is used in Lessons 10.4 to 10.6.

- If the 2 sq m includes a normal-loss allowance (say 1.8 sq m goes into the shoe and 0.2 sq m is normal off-cut), the standard is attainable. An ideal standard would allow only 1.8 sq m.
- **Traps:** "unaltered over a long period" = basic; "perfect conditions" = ideal; "efficient conditions with allowances" = attainable; "present conditions, short period" = current.

### Lesson 10.3: Behavioural aspects; standard costing and budgets

**Must know**

**Behavioural aspects of standard costing**

1. **Participation:** staff who help set standards accept them more readily and are more motivated to achieve them.
2. **Level of difficulty:** attainable standards motivate best; ideal standards discourage staff because they can never be met; loose standards give no challenge.
3. **Slack:** managers asked to set their own standards may pad them (add slack) to look good.
4. **Blame culture:** using variances to punish people breeds fear, hiding of information and manipulation of records. Variances should be used to help, not to blame.
5. **Controllability:** managers should be judged only on variances they can control.
6. **Interdependence:** one manager's action (cheap materials) can cause another's adverse variance (wastage, slow working), causing conflict.
7. **Timely feedback:** variance reports must be prompt and clear to influence behaviour.

**Standard costing and budgets**

| Point | Standard costing | Budget |
|---|---|---|
| Unit of expression | Per unit of product or operation | Totals for a department or the whole business |
| Scope | Mainly production costs | All functions: sales, production, cash, administration |
| Basis | Technical assessment of what cost should be | Plan of what is expected to happen |
| Use | Control and variance analysis, stock valuation | Planning, coordination and control |
| Can exist without the other? | Needs budgets to be fully useful | Budgets can exist without standards |

- **Link:** Budgeted cost = Standard cost per unit × Budgeted output. Standards are the building blocks of budgets; both are used for control by comparing actual with plan.

**Explain more**

- If the factory budgets 1,200 pairs a month, budgeted direct material cost = 1,200 × ₦3,000 = ₦3,600,000. The standard (₦3,000 a pair) is the unit figure; the budget (₦3,600,000) is the total.
- **Flexing:** variances compare actual cost with the standard cost of **actual** output, not with the original budget. Comparing actual cost for 1,000 pairs with a budget for 1,200 pairs would mix up volume with efficiency.

### Lesson 10.4: Direct material variances

**Must know**

Let SQ = standard quantity for actual output, AQ = actual quantity, SP = standard price, AP = actual price.

- **Material cost variance (total):** the difference between the standard material cost of the actual production and the actual cost of direct materials used.
    - Material cost variance = (SQ × SP) − (AQ × AP)
- **Material price variance:** the part of the cost variance caused by paying a price different from standard.
    - Material price variance = (SP − AP) × AQ = (AQ × SP) − (AQ × AP)
- **Material usage variance:** the part caused by using a quantity different from the standard for actual output.
    - Material usage variance = (SQ − AQ) × SP = (SQ × SP) − (AQ × SP)
- **Check:** Material cost variance = Price variance + Usage variance.
- SQ = Actual output × Standard quantity per unit.
- A positive answer is favourable; a negative answer is adverse.
- When several materials are mixed, the usage variance can be split further into a **mix variance** and a **yield variance**.

**Causes**

| Variance | Adverse causes | Favourable causes |
|---|---|---|
| Price | Market price rise, inflation, loss of trade discounts, emergency (rush) buying, buying higher quality, poor negotiation, higher transport cost | Bulk discounts, cheaper supplier, lower quality material, market price fall |
| Usage | Wastage, lower quality material, theft, poor workmanship, faulty machines, unskilled labour | Better quality material, more skilled workers, better supervision, less waste |

**Explain more**

**Worked example 1.** The Aba factory (standard: 2 sq m at ₦1,500) made 1,000 pairs. It bought and used 2,100 sq m of leather at ₦1,450 per sq m.

1. SQ = 1,000 × 2 = 2,000 sq m. Standard cost of actual output = 2,000 × ₦1,500 = ₦3,000,000.
2. Actual cost = 2,100 × ₦1,450 = ₦3,045,000.
3. Price variance = (₦1,500 − ₦1,450) × 2,100 = ₦50 × 2,100 = **₦105,000 F**.
4. Usage variance = (2,000 − 2,100) × ₦1,500 = −100 × ₦1,500 = **₦150,000 A**.
5. Cost variance = ₦3,000,000 − ₦3,045,000 = **₦45,000 A**. Check: ₦105,000 F − ₦150,000 A = ₦45,000 A.

Interpretation: the buyer saved ₦50 a sq m by buying cheaper leather, but the cheaper leather tore more easily and 100 extra sq m were used. The two variances are interdependent: the purchasing manager's "favourable" variance caused the production manager's adverse one.

**Worked example 2: price variance at purchase or at usage.** A Kano flour mill's standard price for wheat is ₦200 per kg. It bought 5,000 kg at ₦210 and used 4,600 kg.

- If stock is held at **standard** cost, the price variance is calculated on **quantity purchased**: (₦200 − ₦210) × 5,000 = **₦50,000 A**. This is the usual approach because the variance is reported as soon as the buying happens.
- If the price variance is calculated on **quantity used**: (₦200 − ₦210) × 4,600 = **₦46,000 A**. The remaining ₦4,000 sits in closing stock of 400 kg.
- The usage variance is always based on quantity **used**.

**Worked example 3: working backwards.** Standard price ₦90 per kg; standard quantity for actual output 10,000 kg; usage variance ₦36,000 F. Extra or saved quantity = ₦36,000 ÷ ₦90 = 400 kg saved, so AQ = 10,000 − 400 = **9,600 kg**.

- **Traps:** price variance uses **actual** quantity; usage variance uses **standard** price. The "standard cost of actual production" uses SQ for actual output, never the budgeted output. The **cost** (total) variance compares standard cost of actual production with actual cost; the price and usage variances are its two parts.

### Lesson 10.5: Direct labour variances

**Must know**

Let SH = standard hours for actual output, AH paid = actual hours paid, AH worked = actual hours worked, SR = standard rate, AR = actual rate.

- **Labour cost variance (total):** the difference between the standard labour cost of actual production and the actual labour cost.
    - Labour cost variance = (SH × SR) − (AH paid × AR)
- **Labour rate variance:** caused by paying a wage rate different from standard.
    - Labour rate variance = (SR − AR) × AH paid
- **Idle time variance:** the cost of hours paid for but not worked, valued at standard rate. Always adverse.
    - Idle time variance = (AH paid − AH worked) × SR
- **Labour efficiency variance:** caused by working more or fewer hours than standard for the actual output.
    - Labour efficiency variance = (SH − AH worked) × SR
- **Check:** Labour cost variance = Rate + Idle time + Efficiency.
- SH = Actual output × Standard hours per unit. If there is no idle time, AH worked = AH paid.

**Causes**

| Variance | Causes |
|---|---|
| Rate | Wage increase or new union agreement, overtime or shift premium, using a higher (or lower) grade of worker than planned, bonus payments |
| Idle time | Machine breakdown, shortage of materials, power failure, strikes, illness or injury to workers, waiting for instructions or work, poor production planning |
| Efficiency | Skill and experience of workers, motivation and morale, quality of materials, condition of machines, quality of supervision, learning effect, wrong grade of labour |

**Explain more**

**Worked example 1.** The Aba factory (standard: 3 hours at ₦800) made 1,000 pairs. Workers were paid for 3,100 hours at ₦850 an hour. A machine breakdown caused 150 idle hours.

1. SH = 1,000 × 3 = 3,000 hours. Standard cost of actual output = 3,000 × ₦800 = ₦2,400,000.
2. Actual cost = 3,100 × ₦850 = ₦2,635,000. AH worked = 3,100 − 150 = 2,950.
3. Rate variance = (₦800 − ₦850) × 3,100 = **₦155,000 A**.
4. Idle time variance = 150 × ₦800 = **₦120,000 A**.
5. Efficiency variance = (3,000 − 2,950) × ₦800 = **₦40,000 F**.
6. Cost variance = ₦2,400,000 − ₦2,635,000 = **₦235,000 A**. Check: ₦155,000 A + ₦120,000 A − ₦40,000 F = ₦235,000 A.

**Worked example 2: finding standard hours from the efficiency variance.** 17,500 direct labour hours were worked at a standard rate of ₦6.50 an hour. The efficiency variance was ₦7,800 favourable.

1. Hours saved = ₦7,800 ÷ ₦6.50 = 1,200 hours.
2. Favourable means fewer hours were worked than allowed, so SH = 17,500 + 1,200 = **18,700 standard hours**.
3. Check: (18,700 − 17,500) × ₦6.50 = ₦7,800 F. (If the variance had been adverse, SH = 17,500 − 1,200 = 16,300.)

**Worked example 3: finding the actual rate.** 6,200 hours were paid; standard rate ₦500; rate variance ₦31,000 A. Excess per hour = ₦31,000 ÷ 6,200 = ₦5, so AR = ₦500 + ₦5 = **₦505**.

- **Traps:** rate variance uses hours **paid**; efficiency variance uses hours **worked**; idle time uses the **standard** rate. An increase in the wage rate causes a **rate** variance, not an idle time variance. Illness or injury to workers, machine breakdown and shortage of materials all cause idle time.

### Lesson 10.6: Investigating variances and reconciling standard and actual cost

**Must know**

**When to investigate a variance (factors)**

1. **Size (materiality):** investigate if the variance exceeds a set amount or percentage of standard (for example over ₦50,000 or over 5%).
2. **Controllability:** investigate variances management can act on; uncontrollable ones (a national price rise) may only need the standard revised.
3. **Cost versus benefit:** investigate only if the expected saving from correcting the cause exceeds the cost of investigating.
4. **Trend:** a small variance that grows month after month needs investigating even if each one is small.
5. **Type of standard:** under an ideal standard, adverse variances are expected and less alarming.
6. **Reliability of measurement:** the variance may come from recording errors or a wrong standard, not real inefficiency.
7. **Interdependence:** a variance may be caused by another department.
8. **Favourable variances too:** a large favourable variance may signal a loose standard or a quality cut.

**Possible causes found on investigation:** measurement or recording error; out-of-date or wrong standard; random (chance) fluctuation; genuine operational inefficiency or efficiency.

**Reconciliation of standard cost with actual cost**

- Actual cost = Standard cost of actual output + Adverse variances − Favourable variances.
- The reconciliation (operating) statement starts with the standard cost of actual output, lists each variance (F or A) and ends at actual cost.

**Explain more**

**Worked example: reconciliation of prime cost for 1,000 pairs (Aba factory).**

| | F (₦) | A (₦) | ₦ |
|---|---|---|---|
| Standard prime cost of actual output (1,000 × ₦5,400) | | | 5,400,000 |
| Material price variance | 105,000 | | |
| Material usage variance | | 150,000 | |
| Labour rate variance | | 155,000 | |
| Labour idle time variance | | 120,000 | |
| Labour efficiency variance | 40,000 | | |
| **Totals** | 145,000 | 425,000 | |
| Net adverse variance (₦425,000 − ₦145,000) | | | 280,000 |
| **Actual prime cost** | | | **5,680,000** |

Check: actual materials ₦3,045,000 + actual labour ₦2,635,000 = ₦5,680,000. The figures agree, so the variances are correct. Adverse variances are added because they make actual cost higher than standard; favourable ones are deducted.


### One-minute recap

- Standard cost = predetermined unit cost; standard costing = control through comparing standards with actual and analysing variances.
- Variance = standard (or budget) minus actual; favourable if actual cost is lower, adverse if higher.
- Four standards: ideal (perfect), attainable (efficient with allowances, motivates best), basic (long-run, unaltered), current (present conditions).
- Standard cost card: quantity × price for each element per unit.
- Budgeted cost = standard cost per unit × budgeted output; variances compare actual with the standard cost of **actual** output.
- Material: price = (SP − AP) × AQ; usage = (SQ − AQ) × SP; cost = SQ × SP − AQ × AP.
- Labour: rate = (SR − AR) × hours paid; idle time = idle hours × SR (always adverse); efficiency = (SH − hours worked) × SR.
- Investigate by size, controllability, cost versus benefit, trend and reliability.
- Actual cost = standard cost of actual output + adverse − favourable variances.

### Theory practice

**Question:** A Lagos paint factory's standard for one tin is 5 kg of material at ₦120 per kg and 2 labour hours at ₦600 an hour. In March it made 800 tins, buying and using 4,200 kg of material for ₦483,000. Labour was paid for 1,700 hours costing ₦1,054,000, of which 60 hours were idle because of a power failure. Calculate the material and labour variances and reconcile standard prime cost with actual prime cost.

*Solution:*
1. AP = ₦483,000 ÷ 4,200 = ₦115. SQ = 800 × 5 = 4,000 kg.
2. Material price = (₦120 − ₦115) × 4,200 = **₦21,000 F**. Usage = (4,000 − 4,200) × ₦120 = **₦24,000 A**. Cost = 4,000 × ₦120 − ₦483,000 = ₦480,000 − ₦483,000 = **₦3,000 A**.
3. AR = ₦1,054,000 ÷ 1,700 = ₦620. SH = 800 × 2 = 1,600. Hours worked = 1,700 − 60 = 1,640.
4. Rate = (₦600 − ₦620) × 1,700 = **₦34,000 A**. Idle time = 60 × ₦600 = **₦36,000 A**. Efficiency = (1,600 − 1,640) × ₦600 = **₦24,000 A**. Cost = 1,600 × ₦600 − ₦1,054,000 = ₦960,000 − ₦1,054,000 = **₦94,000 A**.
5. Reconciliation: standard prime cost 800 × (₦600 + ₦1,200) = ₦1,440,000; favourable ₦21,000; adverse ₦24,000 + ₦34,000 + ₦36,000 + ₦24,000 = ₦118,000; net adverse ₦97,000; actual = ₦1,440,000 + ₦97,000 = **₦1,537,000** (= ₦483,000 + ₦1,054,000).
