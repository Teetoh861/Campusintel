## Topic 4: Materials Costing and Control

**What you must know:** what materials and inventory control are; the purchasing, receiving, inspection, storage and issue procedures and their documents; bin card vs stores ledger; perpetual inventory, continuous stocktaking and discrepancies; pricing issues by FIFO, LIFO and average methods and their effect on profit; maximum, minimum, re-order and average stock levels; the EOQ model and its costs.

### Lesson 4.1: Materials, inventory control and purchasing

**Must know**

- **Materials:** all the tangible material assets of an organisation **other than its fixed assets**: raw materials, components, consumable stores, work-in-progress and finished goods.
- **Inventory (stock) control:** the system used to control the investment in stock items, with the overall objective of **minimising the total associated costs** (purchase, ordering and holding costs) while avoiding stock-outs.
- **3 motives for holding stock:**
  1. **Transaction:** to meet normal day-to-day production and sales needs.
  2. **Precautionary:** a buffer against unexpected demand or late deliveries.
  3. **Speculative:** holding stock to profit from an expected **increase in its price** in the future.
- **Purchasing procedure (in order):**
  1. **Purchase requisition:** a **written request to initiate purchases**, sent by the storekeeper or user department to the purchasing department.
  2. Purchasing department finds sources of supply, compares quotations and selects a supplier.
  3. **Purchase order:** the formal order sent to the supplier, signed by the purchasing officer (in a tertiary institution, the **Procurement Director** issues purchase orders).
  4. **Receiving:** goods arrive with the supplier's **delivery note** (waybill); the receiving section counts them.
  5. **Inspection:** quality and specification are checked; rejected items go back with a returns note.
  6. **Goods received note (GRN):** records the details of materials received from suppliers: date, supplier's name, purchase order number, description and quantity.
  7. Accounts matches **purchase order, GRN and invoice** before paying the supplier.
- **Functions of the purchasing department:** receiving purchase requisitions; finding sources of supply; negotiating prices and terms; preparing and following up purchase orders; dealing with rejects and returns. **Accounting for materials received is NOT a purchasing function** (it belongs to the cost/stores accounts section).

**Explain more**

- Trap: purchase **requisition** = internal request (stores to purchasing). Purchase **order** = external order (purchasing to supplier). An **invoice** is the supplier's bill; a **proforma invoice** is a quotation-style invoice sent before goods are supplied; a **waybill** travels with the goods; a **stores requisition note** draws goods out of stores, not in.
- A Kano flour mill's storekeeper sees wheat falling to the re-order level, raises a purchase requisition; the purchasing manager sends a purchase order to a supplier in Funtua; the lorry arrives with a waybill; the quality lab inspects the grain; the receiving clerk prepares a GRN; accounts pays only when order, GRN and invoice agree.
- **ABC analysis** groups stock by value so control effort goes where the money is:
  - **A items:** few in quantity (roughly 10% of items) but **high in value** (roughly 70% of value). Tightest control.
  - **B items:** middle group (roughly 20% of items, 20% of value).
  - **C items:** many in quantity (roughly 70% of items) but low in value (roughly 10%). Simple control.

### Lesson 4.2: Storage, issue, stores records and discrepancies

**Must know**

- **Duties of the storekeeper:** **receiving**, storing safely, **recording** (bin cards) and **issuing** stock; keeping stock within its levels; raising purchase requisitions. **Selling stock items is NOT a storekeeper's duty.**
- **Materials (stores) requisition note:** authorises the storekeeper to issue materials to a department or job. Also: **materials returned note** (unused materials back to stores) and **materials transfer note** (from one job to another).
- **Bin card:** a **quantity-only** record of materials received, issued and in balance, kept by the storekeeper at the bin.
- **Stores ledger:** a record of **both quantity and value** of materials received, issued and in balance, kept by the cost/stores accounts office.

| Point | Bin card | Stores ledger |
|---|---|---|
| Kept by | Storekeeper | Cost (stores) accountant |
| Kept where | In the store, at the bin | In the accounts office |
| Shows | Quantity only | Quantity and value |
| Posted | As each movement happens | From the documents, after the movement |

- **Perpetual inventory:** a system of recording stores balances **after every receipt and issue**, so the balance of every item is always known from the records (bin cards and stores ledger).
- **Continuous stocktaking:** **physically counting** a few items every day on a rota (and at random, without warning) so every item is checked several times a year. Perpetual inventory is the **records**; continuous stocktaking is the **physical check** of those records. (Periodic stocktaking counts everything once or twice a year, usually at year end.)
- **Treatment of discrepancies** (physical count differs from records):
  1. Investigate the cause (clerical error, theft, evaporation, breakage, wrong issue).
  2. Correct clerical errors in the records.
  3. **Normal** (unavoidable) losses: written off to **production overhead**, so they are absorbed into the cost of good output.
  4. **Abnormal** losses (beyond the normal level): **written off to the costing profit and loss account**, never added to product cost.
  5. Adjust the bin card and stores ledger to the physical count and tighten controls.

**Explain more**

- Advantages of perpetual inventory with continuous stocktaking: errors and fraud found early; production is not stopped for an annual count; interim accounts can be prepared without a full count; stock is kept between its maximum and minimum levels; correct data for insurance claims; a moral check on stores staff.
- Worked example: the bin card at a Lagos bakery shows 520 bags of sugar; the physical count finds 505. Shortage = 520 − 505 = 15 bags. At ₦2,000 a bag, value = 15 × ₦2,000 = ₦30,000. If normal loss is 5 bags (₦10,000), that goes to production overhead; the other 10 bags (₦20,000) are abnormal and go to the costing profit and loss account. Both records are reduced to 505.
- Trap: abnormal wastage is **not** added to product cost and **not** shared; it is written off to the costing profit and loss account.

### Lesson 4.3: Pricing issues: FIFO, LIFO and average methods

**Must know**

- **Pricing issues:** deciding the cost charged to production for materials issued; the same choice fixes the value of closing stock.
- **FIFO (First In, First Out):** issues are priced at the prices of the **first (earliest) batches received** into the store. Closing stock is at the **latest** prices.
- **LIFO (Last In, First Out):** assumes stocks are issued in the **reverse order of receipt**: issues are priced at the **latest** batches. Closing stock is at the **earliest** prices.
- **Simple average:** issue price = sum of the unit prices of the lots in stock ÷ number of lots (ignores quantities).
- **Weighted average (perpetual):** after **each receipt**, Average price = Total value in stock ÷ Total units in stock. Issues are priced at that average until the next receipt.
- **EOQ is NOT a pricing method**; it decides how much to order.
- **Check for any method:** Value of issues + Value of closing stock = Opening stock + Purchases.

**Effect on profit** (cost of sales is lower when issues are cheaper, so profit is higher):

| Prices rising | Cost of issues | Closing stock | Profit |
|---|---|---|---|
| FIFO | Lowest | **Highest** | **Highest** |
| Weighted average | Middle | Middle | Middle |
| LIFO | Highest | **Lowest** | **Lowest** |

When prices are falling, the order reverses (FIFO gives the lowest profit). IAS 2 does not allow LIFO in published financial statements, but it is still used and examined for cost records.

**Explain more**

**Worked example 1 (all three methods, perpetual).** An Aba shoe factory's leather record: Mar 1 received 1,000 units for ₦3,000 in total (unit price = ₦3,000 ÷ 1,000 = ₦3.00); Mar 8 received 3,000 units at ₦3.50; Mar 12 issued 2,500 units; Mar 20 issued 1,000 units.

Mar 1 and Mar 8 are the same under every method: Mar 1 balance ₦3,000; Mar 8 amount received = 3,000 × ₦3.50 = ₦10,500; balance = ₦3,000 + ₦10,500 = ₦13,500 (4,000 units).

*LIFO:*
- Mar 12 issue 2,500 from the latest lot: 2,500 × ₦3.50 = ₦8,750. Balance = ₦13,500 − ₦8,750 = ₦4,750 (500 at ₦3.50 + 1,000 at ₦3.00).
- Mar 20 issue 1,000: 500 × ₦3.50 = ₦1,750 plus 500 × ₦3.00 = ₦1,500; amount ₦3,250 (two prices used). Balance = 500 × ₦3.00 = ₦1,500.

*FIFO:*
- Mar 12 issue 2,500: 1,000 × ₦3.00 = ₦3,000 plus 1,500 × ₦3.50 = ₦5,250; amount ₦8,250. Balance = 1,500 × ₦3.50 = ₦5,250.
- Mar 20 issue 1,000 × ₦3.50 = ₦3,500. Balance = 500 × ₦3.50 = ₦1,750.

*Weighted average:*
- After Mar 8: ₦13,500 ÷ 4,000 = ₦3.375.
- Mar 12 issue 2,500 × ₦3.375 = ₦8,437.50; balance 1,500 × ₦3.375 = ₦5,062.50.
- Mar 20 issue 1,000 × ₦3.375 = ₦3,375; balance 500 × ₦3.375 = ₦1,687.50.

| | Total issues | Closing stock | Check |
|---|---|---|---|
| FIFO | ₦11,750 | ₦1,750 | ₦13,500 |
| Weighted average | ₦11,812.50 | ₦1,687.50 | ₦13,500 |
| LIFO | ₦12,000 | ₦1,500 | ₦13,500 |

Prices rose (₦3.00 to ₦3.50), so FIFO has the highest closing stock and profit, LIFO the lowest.

**Worked example 2 (weighted average with rounding, receipt after issues).** A Kano flour mill: Jun 3 received 600 bags at ₦40 (₦24,000); Jun 10 received 800 bags at ₦46 (₦36,800); Jun 14 issued 500 bags; Jun 22 received 700 bags at ₦50 (₦35,000).
1. After Jun 10: 1,400 bags, ₦60,800. Average = ₦60,800 ÷ 1,400 = ₦43.428571..., rounded to 2 decimal places ₦43.43.
2. Jun 14 issue (using the rounded price, nearest naira): 500 × ₦43.43 = ₦21,715.
3. Balance 900 bags; carrying the average unrounded, value = 900 × ₦43.428571 = ₦39,085.71.
4. After Jun 22: 1,600 bags, ₦39,085.71 + ₦35,000 = ₦74,085.71. New average = ₦74,085.71 ÷ 1,600 = ₦46.30 (2 decimal places).

Always follow the rounding the question states; it changes the last digits.

**Worked example 3 (simple average).** If 600 bags at ₦40 and 800 at ₦46 are in stock, simple average price = (₦40 + ₦46) ÷ 2 = ₦43, even though there are more ₦46 bags. Weighted average gives ₦43.43.

**Worked example 4 (FIFO for finished goods).** Opening stock 400 units valued at ₦1,800; 8,000 units produced at ₦48,000; closing stock 900 units. Under FIFO the oldest units are sold first, so closing stock comes from this period's production: unit cost = ₦48,000 ÷ 8,000 = ₦6; closing stock = 900 × ₦6 = ₦5,400.

- Method for any long question: draw a stores ledger with columns Date | Receipts (qty, price, ₦) | Issues (qty, price, ₦) | Balance (qty, ₦). Under FIFO and LIFO, list the balance as separate lots so you can see which prices are left. If the business is new, there is no opening stock.

### Lesson 4.4: Stock control levels

**Must know**

- **Re-order level (ROL):** the stock level at which a new order is placed.
  - **ROL = Maximum consumption × Maximum re-order period (lead time)**
  - With a safety stock: **ROL = (Average usage × Lead time) + Safety stock**
- **Minimum stock level:** the level below which stock should not normally fall, to prevent running out; it is the allowance (buffer) that covers errors in forecasting the lead time or demand during the lead time.
  - **Minimum level = ROL − (Average consumption × Average re-order period)**
- **Maximum stock level:** the level above which stock should not normally rise.
  - **Maximum level = ROL + Re-order quantity − (Minimum consumption × Minimum re-order period)**
- **Average stock level = Minimum level + ½ Re-order quantity** (alternative: (Maximum level + Minimum level) ÷ 2).
- **Average re-order period = (Minimum lead time + Maximum lead time) ÷ 2.** Average consumption, if not given = (Minimum + Maximum consumption) ÷ 2.
- **Danger level = Average consumption × Maximum re-order period for emergency purchases**; at this level emergency buying starts.
- **Re-order quantity (ROQ):** the amount ordered each time, normally the **EOQ**.

**Explain more**

**Worked example (Lagos bakery, flour bags).** Usage: minimum 30, average 50, maximum 70 bags a day. Lead time 10 to 16 days. EOQ 600 bags.
1. ROL = 70 × 16 = **1,120 bags**.
2. Average lead time = (10 + 16) ÷ 2 = 13 days. Minimum level = 1,120 − (50 × 13) = 1,120 − 650 = **470 bags**.
3. Maximum level = 1,120 + 600 − (30 × 10) = 1,720 − 300 = **1,420 bags**.
4. Re-order quantity = EOQ = **600 bags**.
5. Average stock = 470 + ½ × 600 = **770 bags** (the alternative formula gives (1,420 + 470) ÷ 2 = 945 bags; use the formula the question signals, and show it).

**Safety stock version:** usage 80 units a day, lead time 7 days, safety stock 200 units. ROL = (80 × 7) + 200 = 560 + 200 = **760 units**.

**Mixed units:** consumption 150 to 300 units per **week**, delivery 21 to 35 **days**. Convert days to weeks: 35 ÷ 7 = 5 weeks. ROL = 300 × 5 = **1,500 units**. Always put usage and lead time in the same unit first.

- Traps: maximum consumption × maximum period is the **ROL**, not the maximum level. The maximum level formula **adds** ROQ and **subtracts** minimum × minimum. The re-order quantity is the EOQ, not the ROL.

### Lesson 4.5: Economic order quantity (EOQ)

**Must know**

- **EOQ (Economic Order Quantity):** the order size that **minimises the total of ordering and carrying (holding) costs**. At the EOQ, annual ordering cost and annual carrying cost are **equal**, and their total is at its **minimum**.
- **Ordering costs:** cost of placing and receiving an order: clerical and stationery costs, telephone, transport, inspection of goods received. They **fall** as order size rises (fewer orders).
- **Carrying (holding) costs:** cost of keeping stock: warehousing/storage, insurance, interest on capital tied up, deterioration, obsolescence, theft. They **rise** as order size rises.
- Symbols: D = annual demand (units); Co = cost per order; Cc (or Ch) = carrying cost per unit per year; Q = order quantity; P = purchase price per unit.
- **EOQ = √(2 × D × Co ÷ Cc)**
- **Annual ordering cost = D × Co ÷ Q** (number of orders D ÷ Q, times Co).
- **Annual carrying cost = Q × Cc ÷ 2** (average stock Q ÷ 2, times Cc).
- **Number of orders a year = D ÷ EOQ.** **Interval between orders = Days in the year ÷ Number of orders.**
- **Total annual cost = (D × P) + (D ÷ Q × Co) + (Q ÷ 2 × Cc).**
- **Rearranged for demand: D = EOQ² × Cc ÷ (2 × Co).**
- **Assumptions:** demand and lead time known and constant; Co and Cc constant; price constant (no discounts); no stock-outs; whole order delivered at once.

**Explain more**

**Worked example 1 (carrying cost as a %).** A paint dealer in Ikorodu sells 24,000 tins a year. Cost per order ₦300; price ₦400 a tin; carrying cost 10% of price a year.
1. Cc = 10% × ₦400 = ₦40 per tin per year.
2. EOQ = √(2 × 24,000 × 300 ÷ 40) = √360,000 = **600 tins**.
3. Orders a year = 24,000 ÷ 600 = **40**.
4. With 360 working days: interval = 360 ÷ 40 = **every 9 days**.
5. Ordering cost = 40 × ₦300 = ₦12,000; carrying cost = 600 ÷ 2 × ₦40 = ₦12,000 (equal, as expected). Total ordering and carrying cost = **₦24,000**.

**Convert demand to a year first.** Demand given per month: × 12 (1,500 a month = 18,000 a year). Per quarter: × 4. Per six months: × 2. Carrying cost must be **per unit per year**. Round EOQ to whole units (state "approximately").

**Worked example 2 (finding demand).** EOQ is 400 units, Co = ₦50, Cc = ₦5. D = 400² × 5 ÷ (2 × 50) = 160,000 × 5 ÷ 100 = **8,000 units**; orders = 8,000 ÷ 400 = 20.

**Worked example 3 (bulk discount).** A Kano mill uses 12,000 units a year at ₦20; Co = ₦40; Cc = ₦6 per unit per year (constant). The supplier offers a 2% discount for orders of 2,000 units.
- EOQ = √(2 × 12,000 × 40 ÷ 6) = √160,000 = 400 units.
- At EOQ: purchases 12,000 × ₦20 = ₦240,000; ordering (12,000 ÷ 400) × ₦40 = 30 × ₦40 = ₦1,200; carrying 400 ÷ 2 × ₦6 = ₦1,200. Total **₦242,400**.
- At 2,000 units: price ₦20 × 98% = ₦19.60; purchases 12,000 × ₦19.60 = ₦235,200; ordering 6 × ₦40 = ₦240; carrying 2,000 ÷ 2 × ₦6 = ₦6,000. Total **₦241,440**.
- Difference = ₦242,400 − ₦241,440 = ₦960, so the discount is cheaper here. Always compare **total** cost including purchases; EOQ alone ignores discounts, so it does not always give the lowest total cost. Buying the whole year in one order means average stock = D ÷ 2 and one order cost.

- Traps: the EOQ formula has **2** in the numerator and **Cc** in the denominator; D × Co ÷ Q is ordering cost, Q × Cc ÷ 2 is carrying cost. Warehousing cost is a **carrying** cost, not an ordering cost.

### One-minute recap

- Materials = tangible assets other than fixed assets; inventory control minimises total stock costs.
- Motives: transaction, precautionary, speculative (price rise).
- Purchase requisition (internal) → purchase order (to supplier) → receiving and inspection → GRN → pay on order, GRN and invoice agreeing.
- Bin card = quantity only (storekeeper); stores ledger = quantity and value (accounts).
- Perpetual inventory = records after every movement; continuous stocktaking = daily physical checks. Abnormal losses go to costing profit and loss.
- FIFO prices issues at earliest prices; LIFO at latest; weighted average recalculated after each receipt. Rising prices: FIFO highest stock and profit, LIFO lowest.
- ROL = Max usage × Max lead time (or average usage × lead time + safety stock).
- Min = ROL − Avg usage × Avg lead time; Max = ROL + ROQ − Min usage × Min lead time; Average stock = Min + ½ ROQ.
- EOQ = √(2DCo ÷ Cc); ordering cost = DCo ÷ Q; carrying cost = QCc ÷ 2; equal at EOQ.

### Theory practice

**Question:** A Lagos printing firm uses 8,000 reams of paper a year, bought at ₦500 a ream. Each order costs ₦250 and carrying cost is 20% of the purchase price a year. Calculate (a) the EOQ, (b) the number of orders a year and (c) the total annual ordering and carrying cost at the EOQ.

*Solution:*
1. Cc = 20% × ₦500 = ₦100 per ream per year.
2. (a) EOQ = √(2 × 8,000 × 250 ÷ 100) = √40,000 = **200 reams**.
3. (b) Orders = 8,000 ÷ 200 = **40 orders**.
4. (c) Ordering cost = 40 × ₦250 = ₦10,000; carrying cost = 200 ÷ 2 × ₦100 = ₦10,000; total = **₦20,000**.
