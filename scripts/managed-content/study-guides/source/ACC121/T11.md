## Topic 11: Contemporary Issues in Cost Accounting

**What you must know:** activity-based costing and cost drivers; just-in-time and backflush costing; target costing and life-cycle costing; total quality management, the four costs of quality and the characteristics of quality cost information; throughput accounting; how technology changes costing.

### Lesson 11.1: Activity-based costing (ABC)

**Must know**

- **Activity-based costing:** a method that charges overheads to products according to the **activities** each product uses, instead of one blanket rate based on labour or machine hours.
- **Activity:** a task that consumes resources, e.g. machine set-ups, ordering materials, inspection, despatch.
- **Cost pool:** the total overhead collected for one activity.
- **Cost driver:** the factor that causes the cost of an activity to rise or fall, e.g. number of set-ups, number of purchase orders, number of inspections.

**5 steps in ABC:**

1. Identify the main activities.
2. Collect the overhead of each activity into a cost pool.
3. Identify the cost driver for each activity.
4. Calculate a cost driver rate: **Cost driver rate = Cost pool ÷ Total number of cost drivers**.
5. Charge each product: **Overhead charged = Cost driver rate × Drivers used by the product**.

| Traditional absorption | ABC |
|---|---|
| One or a few rates (labour or machine hours) | One rate per activity |
| Assumes overhead varies with volume | Recognises costs driven by complexity and batches |
| Low-volume products often under-costed | More accurate product costs |
| Simple and cheap | Costly and time-consuming to set up |

**Explain more**

*Worked example.* An Ikeja factory makes Standard (10,000 units, 8,000 machine hours) and Deluxe (2,000 units, 2,000 machine hours). Overhead is ₦1,000,000: set-ups ₦600,000 (Standard 10 set-ups, Deluxe 20) and order handling ₦400,000 (Standard 30 orders, Deluxe 50).

1. **Traditional:** rate = ₦1,000,000 ÷ 10,000 machine hours = ₦100 an hour. Standard: 8,000 × ₦100 = ₦800,000, so **₦80 a unit**. Deluxe: 2,000 × ₦100 = ₦200,000, so **₦100 a unit**.
2. **ABC rates:** set-ups ₦600,000 ÷ 30 = ₦20,000 per set-up; orders ₦400,000 ÷ 80 = ₦5,000 per order.
3. Standard: (10 × ₦20,000) + (30 × ₦5,000) = ₦350,000 ÷ 10,000 = **₦35 a unit**.
4. Deluxe: (20 × ₦20,000) + (50 × ₦5,000) = ₦650,000 ÷ 2,000 = **₦325 a unit**.

The small-batch Deluxe uses far more set-ups and orders per unit, so ABC charges it more. Under the traditional rate the high-volume Standard was subsidising Deluxe.

- **Advantages:** more accurate costs, better pricing and product-mix decisions, shows what drives overhead so it can be managed.
- **Limitations:** expensive to run, choosing drivers involves judgement, some costs (e.g. the chief executive's salary) have no clear driver.
- Trap: the cost driver is the **cause** of the cost (number of set-ups), not the cost itself (set-up cost).

### Lesson 11.2: Just-in-time (JIT) and backflush costing

**Must know**

- **Just-in-time:** a system in which materials are bought and goods are produced only when needed, so that stock is kept close to zero. It is a **pull** system: customer demand pulls production through the factory.
- **JIT purchasing:** small, frequent deliveries arrive just as they are needed.
- **JIT production:** each stage produces only what the next stage calls for.

**Features of JIT:**

1. Very low or zero stocks.
2. Close, long-term relationships with a few reliable suppliers.
3. Small, frequent deliveries.
4. High quality (zero defects), since there is no buffer stock to cover faults.
5. Reliable machines (preventive maintenance) and short set-up times.
6. A flexible, multi-skilled workforce, often in production cells.

- **Benefits:** lower storage and holding costs, less waste and obsolete stock, faster response to customers, better quality.
- **Problems:** a late supplier stops production; little protection against sudden demand rises; depends on reliable transport.
- **Backflush costing:** a simplified method, used with JIT, that records costs mainly at the **end** of production (on completion or on sale) and works **backwards**, using standard costs, to charge materials and conversion costs to output.

**Explain more**

- Backflush suits JIT because there is little work in progress to track; detailed entries for every stage add cost without adding much information.
- *Example:* an Aba shoe factory using JIT completes 1,000 pairs. Standard material cost is ₦800 a pair. Instead of recording each issue to work in progress, it records one entry at completion: 1,000 × ₦800 = **₦800,000** of material charged to finished goods.
- In Nigeria, port delays, poor roads and Lagos traffic make strict JIT risky; firms often keep a small safety stock.
- Trap: JIT is a **pull** system, not push. Traditional factories "push" goods into stock regardless of demand.

### Lesson 11.3: Target costing and life-cycle costing

**Must know**

- **Target costing:** start from the price customers will pay, deduct the profit the firm requires, and the remainder is the cost the product must be made for.
- **Target cost = Target (market) selling price − Required profit**
- **Cost gap = Estimated cost − Target cost.** The gap is closed by redesign, value engineering, cheaper materials or better methods before production begins.
- **Life-cycle costing:** tracking and accumulating **all** the costs of a product over its whole life, from research and design through production and marketing to withdrawal.

**Stages of the product life cycle:** introduction, growth, maturity, decline.

**Explain more**

*Target costing example.* A Lagos firm plans a blender that will sell for ₦5,000. It wants a profit of 20% of the selling price. Estimated cost is ₦4,600.

1. Required profit = 20% × ₦5,000 = ₦1,000.
2. Target cost = ₦5,000 − ₦1,000 = **₦4,000**.
3. Cost gap = ₦4,600 − ₦4,000 = **₦600** a unit to be removed.

- Trap: target costing is **price-led** (price − profit = cost); traditional cost-plus pricing is cost-led (cost + mark-up = price).

*Life-cycle costing example.* A phone-charger project expects 5,000 units over its life. Research ₦2,000,000; design ₦1,000,000; production ₦3,000 a unit; marketing ₦1,500,000; withdrawal (decommissioning) ₦500,000.

1. Production = 5,000 × ₦3,000 = ₦15,000,000.
2. Life-cycle cost = ₦2,000,000 + ₦1,000,000 + ₦15,000,000 + ₦1,500,000 + ₦500,000 = **₦20,000,000**.
3. Life-cycle cost per unit = ₦20,000,000 ÷ 5,000 = **₦4,000**.

- Production cost alone (₦3,000) would understate cost by ₦1,000 a unit. Most life-cycle costs are committed at the design stage, so that is where control matters most.

### Lesson 11.4: Quality, throughput accounting and technology

**Must know**

- **Total quality management (TQM):** an approach in which everyone in the organisation works continuously to improve quality and meet customer needs, aiming at **zero defects** and **getting it right first time**.
- **Cost of quality:** the total cost of preventing, finding and correcting defects.

**4 costs of quality:**

1. **Prevention costs:** stop defects happening, e.g. quality training, supplier vetting, preventive maintenance.
2. **Appraisal costs:** check quality, e.g. inspection, testing.
3. **Internal failure costs:** defects found **before** the goods reach the customer, e.g. scrap, rework.
4. **External failure costs:** defects found **after** delivery, e.g. warranty repairs, returns, lost goodwill.

- **Cost of conformance = Prevention + Appraisal. Cost of non-conformance = Internal failure + External failure.**
- **Characteristics of quality cost information:** objective, timely, relevant, accurate (reliable) and understandable. It is **not** subjective.
- **Throughput accounting:** treats only direct material as variable; all other factory costs are fixed. It aims to maximise throughput through the **bottleneck** (the scarcest resource).
- **Throughput = Sales − Direct material cost**
- **Return per factory hour = Throughput per unit ÷ Bottleneck hours per unit**
- **Cost per factory hour = Total factory cost (excluding materials) ÷ Total bottleneck hours available**
- **Throughput accounting ratio (TAR) = Return per factory hour ÷ Cost per factory hour.** A ratio above 1 means the product is worth making.

**Explain more**

*Cost of quality example.* A Kano flour mill spends: training ₦300,000; inspection ₦200,000; scrap and rework ₦250,000; warranty refunds ₦400,000. Prevention ₦300,000 + appraisal ₦200,000 = conformance **₦500,000**; internal ₦250,000 + external ₦400,000 = non-conformance **₦650,000**; total **₦1,150,000**. Spending more on prevention usually cuts failure costs.

- Trap: scrap found in the factory is **internal** failure; a customer return is **external**. Inspection is **appraisal**, not prevention.

*Throughput example.* A product sells for ₦2,500 with direct material of ₦1,000 and needs 0.5 bottleneck hours. Factory costs are ₦4,800,000 for 2,000 bottleneck hours.

1. Throughput per unit = ₦2,500 − ₦1,000 = ₦1,500.
2. Return per factory hour = ₦1,500 ÷ 0.5 = ₦3,000.
3. Cost per factory hour = ₦4,800,000 ÷ 2,000 = ₦2,400.
4. TAR = ₦3,000 ÷ ₦2,400 = **1.25**, above 1, so the product is worth making.

**Effect of technology:**

- Advanced manufacturing technology (computer-aided design and manufacture, robots, flexible manufacturing systems) and enterprise resource planning (ERP) software are now common.
- Direct labour falls and overheads rise, so labour-hour absorption becomes less meaningful and ABC more useful.
- Data are captured in real time, so cost reports are faster and more detailed; automation also supports JIT and backflush.

### One-minute recap

- ABC: activities, cost pools, cost drivers; rate = cost pool ÷ number of drivers.
- ABC gives more accurate costs and stops high-volume products subsidising low-volume ones.
- JIT: pull system, near-zero stock, reliable suppliers, zero defects.
- Backflush costing records costs at the end of production, working backwards with standard costs.
- Target cost = selling price − required profit; cost gap = estimated cost − target cost.
- Life-cycle costing gathers all costs over a product's life; most are committed at design.
- 4 costs of quality: prevention, appraisal (conformance); internal, external failure (non-conformance).
- Quality cost information is objective, timely and relevant, never subjective.
- Throughput = sales − direct material; TAR above 1 is good.
- Technology lowers direct labour, raises overheads and favours ABC.

### Theory practice

**Question:** A Lagos bottled-water company spends: staff quality training ₦180,000; testing samples ₦120,000; re-bottling rejected bottles in the factory ₦90,000; replacing bottles returned by customers ₦210,000; vetting new suppliers ₦60,000. Classify these costs and calculate the costs of conformance, non-conformance and total quality.

*Solution:*
1. Prevention: training ₦180,000 + supplier vetting ₦60,000 = ₦240,000.
2. Appraisal: testing ₦120,000.
3. Internal failure: re-bottling ₦90,000.
4. External failure: customer returns ₦210,000.
5. Conformance = ₦240,000 + ₦120,000 = **₦360,000**. Non-conformance = ₦90,000 + ₦210,000 = **₦300,000**. Total cost of quality = **₦660,000**.
