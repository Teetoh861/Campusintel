## Topic 3: Cost Estimation and the High-Low Method

**What you must know:** what cost estimation is; the cost function Y = a + bx; the five estimation methods with their advantages and disadvantages; the five high-low steps; high-low with inflation, stepped fixed costs and percentage activity levels; regression (least squares); limitations.

### Lesson 3.1: Cost estimation and the cost function

**Must know**

- **Cost estimation:** the measurement of historical cost with a view to helping in the prediction of future cost for management decision making. In short, historical information is analysed to provide estimates on which to base future expectations.
- **Semi-variable (mixed) cost:** a cost with a fixed part and a variable part, e.g. electricity (standing charge plus a rate per unit used), telephone, machine maintenance.
- **Cost separation:** breaking a mixed cost down into its variable and fixed elements. You must do this before you can estimate, because the two parts behave differently when activity changes.
- **The cost function:** Y = a + bx, where:
  - **Y** = total cost (the dependent variable)
  - **a** = total fixed cost
  - **b** = variable cost per unit (of output, machine hour, square metre or whatever drives the cost)
  - **x** = number of units (level of activity)
- On a graph (cost on the Y axis, activity on the X axis): **a** is where the line cuts the cost axis (the intercept); **b** is the slope of the line.

**Explain more**

- Once you know a and b you can predict total cost at any activity level by putting the new x into Y = a + bx. Every computation in this topic ends this way.
- Example: an Aba shoe factory finds its factory running cost is Y = ₦250,000 + ₦1,200x, where x is pairs made. For 900 pairs next month: Y = 250,000 + (1,200 × 900) = 250,000 + 1,080,000 = **₦1,330,000**.
- **Stepped fixed cost:** a fixed cost that stays flat over a range of activity, then jumps to a new level, e.g. hiring an extra supervisor once output passes a limit. In the cost function you simply add the jump to a when activity crosses the trigger point (see Lesson 3.4).
- **Trap:** a is **total** fixed cost (not per unit); b is variable cost **per unit** (not total). Total variable cost = b × x.

### Lesson 3.2: The five cost estimation methods

**Must know**

There are **five main methods** of predicting future figures from the analysis of past data:

1. **The engineering method** (also called the **work measurement** method)
2. **The account analysis method** (accounting method)
3. **The scatter diagram approach** (scattergraph, visual fit)
4. **The high and low method** (high-low or **range** method)
5. **The regression analysis technique** (also called the **least squares** technique)

| Method | How it works | Main strength | Main weakness |
|---|---|---|---|
| Engineering | Engineers estimate physical inputs (materials, labour hours, machine hours) per operation; prices and rates are then applied | Good for repetitive processes with a clear input-output relationship | Time consuming; hard with many products, joint costs or unknown mix |
| Account analysis | Ledger accounts are classified as fixed, variable or semi-variable from the accountant's experience | Fast, cheap, simple | Subjective, arbitrary separation |
| Scatter diagram | Plot total cost against activity for each period; fit a straight line by eye | Simple, convenient, uses all points | Line of best fit is subjective |
| High-low | Uses the highest and lowest activity points in Y = a + bx | Simple, objective, quick | Uses only two extreme points |
| Regression | Statistical line of best fit through all points by formula | Most accurate; gives confidence in goodness of fit | Needs more calculation |

**Account analysis: advantages (3)**
1. It is fast and inexpensive.
2. It is simple and easy to understand.
3. It is flexible, as it can be revised regularly.

**Account analysis: disadvantages (4)**
1. It relies on a single observation to determine the cost function.
2. It is based on past cost.
3. Separation of mixed cost is arbitrary.
4. The initial classification is subjective.

**Explain more**

- **Engineering method in practice:** a Kano flour mill engineer works out that one 50kg bag of flour needs 52kg of wheat, 0.1 labour hours and 0.05 machine hours. The accountant then prices each: wheat at ₦900 per kg, labour at ₦2,000 per hour, machine time at ₦6,000 per hour. Cost per bag = (52 × 900) + (0.1 × 2,000) + (0.05 × 6,000) = 46,800 + 200 + 300 = **₦47,300**. It looks forward from physical facts, not from past ledger figures.
- **Scatter diagram:** total cost on the Y axis, activity on the X axis, one dot per period. You draw the straight line that seems to pass closest to the dots. Where it cuts the cost axis is the fixed cost. Accuracy is doubtful because two people may draw different lines, and the data are past costs.
- **High-low** is described as an **objective** way of separating mixed costs; account analysis and the scatter diagram are **subjective**.
- **Exam trap:** options such as "highest square method" are invented. The real list is engineering, account analysis (accounting), scatter diagram, high-low (range), regression (least squares). Do not confuse "least squares" with "highest square". Break-even analysis, marginal costing and weighted average are **not** estimation methods either.

### Lesson 3.3: The high-low method step by step

**Must know**

**High-low method:** a technique which uses the highest and lowest activity levels to separate a mixed cost into its variable and fixed parts, using extreme data points in Y = a + bx.

**The 5 steps**
1. Select the **highest** activity level and the **lowest** activity level.
2. Determine the cost associated with each. These costs may **not** necessarily be the highest and lowest costs.
3. Variable cost per unit: b = (Total cost at high activity − Total cost at low activity) ÷ (Units at high activity − Units at low activity)
4. Fixed cost from the cost function: a = Y − bx (fixed cost = total cost − (activity level × variable cost per unit)), using either the high or the low point.
5. Predict future cost using Y = a + bx.

**Advantages (3):** simple to operate; not subjective; quick and not expensive.
**Disadvantages (2):** not fully representative, as it uses only the extreme values; gives an abnormal cost function when the extreme points are not typical.

**Explain more**

**Example A (two points).** Bojuboju Limited's maintenance cost at its highest and lowest machine hours (7,400 and 5,000 hours) is ₦2,040 and ₦1,680.
- b = (2,040 − 1,680) ÷ (7,400 − 5,000) = 360 ÷ 2,400 = **₦0.15 per machine hour**
- a = 2,040 − (0.15 × 7,400) = 2,040 − 1,110 = **₦930**
- Check with the low point: 930 + (0.15 × 5,000) = 930 + 750 = ₦1,680. Correct.
- Cost function: **MC = ₦930 + ₦0.15x**

**Example B (predict).** Lonpe Plc: total cost ₦50,000 at 5,000 units and ₦70,000 at 10,000 units. Total cost at 15,000 units?
- b = (70,000 − 50,000) ÷ (10,000 − 5,000) = 20,000 ÷ 5,000 = ₦4
- a = 70,000 − (4 × 10,000) = ₦30,000
- Y = 30,000 + (4 × 15,000) = **₦90,000**

**Example C.** Product XYZ costs ₦240,000 for 5,000 units and ₦296,000 for 7,000 units. Budgeted cost of 9,500 units?
- b = 56,000 ÷ 2,000 = ₦28; a = 296,000 − (28 × 7,000) = 296,000 − 196,000 = ₦100,000
- Y = 100,000 + (28 × 9,500) = 100,000 + 266,000 = **₦366,000**
- Trap: ₦100,000 is only the fixed cost and ₦266,000 only the variable cost. The answer is the total.

**Example D.** A department's total cost was ₦82,200 at 420 units and ₦90,275 at 515 units.
- b = 8,075 ÷ 95 = ₦85; a = 82,200 − (85 × 420) = 82,200 − 35,700 = ₦46,500
- Model: **TC = ₦46,500 + ₦85V**. (₦8,075 is the cost difference, not the fixed cost.)

**Example E (pick by activity, not cost).** Six months' output and total cost: 7,000 units ₦250,000; 8,000 ₦230,000; 6,200 ₦194,000; 7,700 ₦222,000; 8,200 ₦229,000; 7,800 ₦212,000.
- Highest **activity** is 8,200 units (₦229,000), not month 1 with the highest cost of ₦250,000. Lowest activity is 6,200 units (₦194,000).
- b = (229,000 − 194,000) ÷ (8,200 − 6,200) = 35,000 ÷ 2,000 = ₦17.50
- a = 229,000 − (17.50 × 8,200) = 229,000 − 143,500 = ₦85,500
- **Y = ₦85,500 + ₦17.50x**

**Example F.** DG's output and cost: 65,000 units ₦145,000; 80,000 ₦162,000; 90,000 ₦170,000; 60,000 ₦140,000; 75,000 ₦160,000. Expected cost at 85,000 units?
- High 90,000 (₦170,000); low 60,000 (₦140,000). b = 30,000 ÷ 30,000 = ₦1
- a = 170,000 − 90,000 = ₦80,000. Y = 80,000 + 85,000 = **₦165,000**

**Example G (power cost, machine hours).** Eight weeks: 8,000 hrs ₦3,600; 9,000 ₦3,950; 6,000 ₦3,050; 7,400 ₦3,380; 8,600 ₦3,870; 9,200 ₦4,020; 3,700 ₦2,095; 7,800 ₦3,530. Week 9 at 7,200 hours?
- High 9,200 hrs (₦4,020); low 3,700 hrs (₦2,095)
- b = 1,925 ÷ 5,500 = ₦0.35 per machine hour; a = 4,020 − (0.35 × 9,200) = 4,020 − 3,220 = ₦800
- Y = 800 + (0.35 × 7,200) = 800 + 2,520 = **₦3,320** (₦2,520 alone forgets the fixed cost)
- The same pattern with costs a thousand times larger and four times the hours (high 36,800 hrs ₦4,020,000; low 14,800 hrs ₦2,095,000): b = 1,925,000 ÷ 22,000 = ₦87.50 per hour; a = 4,020,000 − 3,220,000 = ₦800,000; at 28,800 hours, Y = 800,000 + 2,520,000 = **₦3,320,000**.

### Lesson 3.4: High-low with inflation, stepped costs and percentage activity

**Must know**

- **Inflation:** where inflation makes costs in each period impossible to compare, adjust all costs to the same price level using a price level index before applying high-low.
  - Cost at base prices = Actual cost × (Base index ÷ Index of that year)
  - Future cost at future prices = Cost at base prices × (Future index ÷ Base index)
- **Stepped fixed cost:** if a known fixed-cost increase is already inside one observed figure, **remove it first**, apply high-low, then **add it back** only for activity levels above the trigger.
- **Percentage activity levels:** treat each percentage point as x; b = cost change per 1% of activity.

**Explain more**

**Example H (inflation).** Risk Free Associates:

| Year | Output | Total cost ₦ | Index | Cost at index 100 ₦ |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2005 | 97,500 | 362,500 | 100 | 362,500 × 100/100 = 362,500 |
| 2006 | 120,000 | 448,000 | 112 | 448,000 × 100/112 = 400,000 |
| 2007 | 135,000 | 522,750 | 123 | 522,750 × 100/123 = 425,000 |
| 2008 | 90,000 | 504,000 | 144 | 504,000 × 100/144 = 350,000 |
| 2009 | 112,500 | 620,000 | 160 | 620,000 × 100/160 = 387,500 |

- High 135,000 (₦425,000); low 90,000 (₦350,000). b = 75,000 ÷ 45,000 = ₦1.6667 per unit (exactly 5/3)
- a = 425,000 − (5/3 × 135,000) = 425,000 − 225,000 = ₦200,000 (at 2005 prices)
- 2010 at 115,000 units: base-price cost = 200,000 + (5/3 × 115,000) = 200,000 + 191,666.67 = ₦391,666.67. At index 180: 391,666.67 × 180/100 = **₦705,000**
- At 180,000 units: 200,000 + 300,000 = ₦500,000 × 1.8 = **₦900,000**
- Comment: 115,000 units lies inside the observed range (90,000 to 135,000), so ₦705,000 is reasonably reliable. 180,000 units lies far outside it; this is extrapolation, and fixed costs may step up or the variable rate may change, so ₦900,000 is much less reliable.
- Trap: ₦391,667 forgets to re-inflate to the 2010 index.

**Example I (stepped fixed cost, step outside the data).** A cleaning firm in Lekki: overheads ₦73,950 at 12,750 m² and ₦83,585 at 15,100 m². Above 15,500 m² another supervisor is needed and fixed cost rises by ₦21,675. Estimate overheads at 16,000 m².
- Both observations are below 15,500, so neither contains the extra supervisor.
- b = (83,585 − 73,950) ÷ (15,100 − 12,750) = 9,635 ÷ 2,350 = ₦4.10 per m²
- a = 83,585 − (4.10 × 15,100) = 83,585 − 61,910 = ₦21,675
- At 16,000 m²: 21,675 + 21,675 (step) + (4.10 × 16,000 = 65,600) = **₦108,950**
- Trap: ₦87,275 forgets the extra supervisor.

**Example J (step already inside one observation).** Total cost ₦125,250 at 6,500 units and ₦203,500 at 11,000 units. Above 9,500 units an extra ₦40,000 fixed cost is needed, and it is included in the 11,000-unit figure.
- Remove the step: 203,500 − 40,000 = ₦163,500 at 11,000 units
- b = (163,500 − 125,250) ÷ (11,000 − 6,500) = 38,250 ÷ 4,500 = ₦8.50
- a = 125,250 − (8.50 × 6,500) = 125,250 − 55,250 = ₦70,000 (fixed cost up to 9,500 units)
- (i) 10,000 units: 70,000 + 40,000 + 85,000 = **₦195,000**
- (ii) 8,500 units: 70,000 + 72,250 = **₦142,250** (below the trigger, no step)
- (iii) 12,000 units: 70,000 + 40,000 + 102,000 = **₦212,000**
- Trap: using the raw figures gives b = ₦17.39 (rounded) and ₦186,111 at 10,000 units. That is wrong because the step distorts the slope.

**Example K (percentage activity).** A manufacturer's flexible budget:

| Cost | 60% | 70% | 80% |
|---|---|---|---|
| Direct materials | 120,000 | 140,000 | 160,000 |
| Direct labour | 90,000 | 105,000 | 120,000 |
| Production overheads | 54,000 | 58,000 | 62,000 |
| Other overheads | 40,000 | 40,000 | 40,000 |
| Total | 304,000 | 343,000 | 382,000 |

Estimate total cost at 77%.
- b = (382,000 − 304,000) ÷ (80 − 60) = 78,000 ÷ 20 = ₦3,900 per 1%
- a = 382,000 − (3,900 × 80) = 382,000 − 312,000 = ₦70,000
- At 77%: 70,000 + (3,900 × 77) = 70,000 + 300,300 = **₦370,300**
- Check by element: materials 2,000 × 77 = 154,000; labour 1,500 × 77 = 115,500; production overheads are semi-variable: 400 per 1% plus 30,000 fixed = 30,000 + 30,800 = 60,800; other overheads 40,000 (fixed). Total = ₦370,300. Correct.

### Lesson 3.5: Regression analysis and the limits of estimation

**Must know**

- **Regression analysis (least squares technique):** a statistical method of estimating cost using historical data from a number of previous periods, in the form Y = a + bx. It gets a line of best fit and tells the user the confidence level that can be put on the goodness of that line. It shows the nature of the relationship between a dependent variable (cost) and an independent variable (activity).
- Formulas (n = number of observations):
  - b = (nΣxy − ΣxΣy) ÷ (nΣx² − (Σx)²)
  - a = Σy ÷ n − b(Σx ÷ n)
- **Limitations of cost estimation from past data:**
  1. Past costs may not reflect future conditions (new prices, technology, methods).
  2. Inflation distorts comparisons unless costs are indexed.
  3. The straight-line (linear) assumption holds only within the relevant range of activity.
  4. Fixed costs may step up outside the observed range, so extrapolation is risky.
  5. One activity measure may not explain all of the cost.
  6. Abnormal periods (strikes, breakdowns) distort the data, especially for high-low.

**Explain more**

**Example L.** A manufacturer's output and total cost: July 80 units ₦10,200; August 90 ₦10,900; September 100 ₦12,100; October 80 ₦10,800; November 120 ₦13,700; December 110 ₦12,500. Estimate the regression equation.

| x | y | xy | x² |
|---|---|---|---|
| 80 | 10,200 | 816,000 | 6,400 |
| 90 | 10,900 | 981,000 | 8,100 |
| 100 | 12,100 | 1,210,000 | 10,000 |
| 80 | 10,800 | 864,000 | 6,400 |
| 120 | 13,700 | 1,644,000 | 14,400 |
| 110 | 12,500 | 1,375,000 | 12,100 |
| Σx = 580 | Σy = 70,200 | Σxy = 6,890,000 | Σx² = 57,400 |

- b = (6 × 6,890,000 − 580 × 70,200) ÷ (6 × 57,400 − 580²) = (41,340,000 − 40,716,000) ÷ (344,400 − 336,400) = 624,000 ÷ 8,000 = **₦78**
- a = 70,200 ÷ 6 − 78 × (580 ÷ 6) = 11,700 − 7,540 = **₦4,160**
- **Y = ₦4,160 + ₦78x**. At 130 units: 4,160 + 10,140 = ₦14,300.
- Here two months share the lowest output (80 units) at different costs, which shows why two extreme points can mislead; regression uses every month.
- **Choosing a method:** when a question says "using any method you feel is suitable", high-low is acceptable and fastest; regression gives the closest estimate. Always state the method you used.

### One-minute recap

- Cost estimation analyses historical cost to predict future cost for decisions.
- Mixed costs must be separated (cost separation) using Y = a + bx: a = total fixed cost, b = variable cost per unit, x = units, Y = total cost.
- Five methods: engineering (work measurement), account analysis, scatter diagram, high-low (range), regression (least squares).
- Account analysis: 3 advantages (fast/cheap, simple, flexible), 4 disadvantages (single observation, past cost, arbitrary separation, subjective classification).
- High-low: pick highest and lowest **activity** (not cost); b = cost difference ÷ activity difference; a = Y − bx; then predict.
- High-low advantages: simple, not subjective, quick and cheap. Disadvantages: uses only extremes; abnormal if extremes are untypical.
- Inflation: deflate costs to one index, then re-inflate the forecast.
- Stepped fixed costs: strip the step out before high-low, add it back above the trigger.
- Regression: b = (nΣxy − ΣxΣy) ÷ (nΣx² − (Σx)²); a = Σy/n − bΣx/n.
- Forecasts outside the observed range are extrapolations and less reliable.

### Theory practice

**Question:** A Kano flour mill records electricity cost against machine hours: January 1,200 hours ₦96,000; February 1,500 hours ₦112,500; March 1,700 hours ₦129,000; April 1,800 hours ₦126,000. Using the high-low method, find the cost function and estimate the electricity cost for 1,600 machine hours.

**Solution:**
- Highest activity = April, 1,800 hours (₦126,000). March has the highest cost but not the highest activity, so ignore it. Lowest activity = January, 1,200 hours (₦96,000).
- b = (126,000 − 96,000) ÷ (1,800 − 1,200) = 30,000 ÷ 600 = ₦50 per machine hour
- a = 126,000 − (50 × 1,800) = 126,000 − 90,000 = ₦36,000
- Cost function: **Y = ₦36,000 + ₦50x**
- At 1,600 hours: Y = 36,000 + (50 × 1,600) = 36,000 + 80,000 = **₦116,000**
