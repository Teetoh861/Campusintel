## Topic 2: Cost Classification

**What you must know:** what cost classification is and why it is done; the bases of classification (element, function, behaviour, traceability, controllability, relevance, normality, product vs period); fixed, variable, semi-variable and stepped costs; costing methods vs costing techniques; the elements of cost and how to build a cost sheet from prime cost to total cost and profit.

### Lesson 2.1: Nature and purpose of cost classification

**Must know**

- **Cost classification:** the arrangement of cost items in logical groups having regard to their nature or purpose. Costs that share a feature (what they are, where they arise, how they behave) are grouped together.
- **Major objective:** to assist management in various decision areas (planning, control, pricing, stock valuation, decision making). Classification is not mainly for product diversification or choosing a production mix; those are only some of the decisions it supports.
- **Purposes of classification:**
  1. Ascertaining the cost of products and services (cost sheets, stock values).
  2. Planning and budgeting (fixed and variable costs behave differently).
  3. Control and responsibility (who can influence which cost).
  4. Decision making (which costs are relevant to a choice).
  5. Fixing selling prices and preparing quotations.
- **Bases (methods) of classification:**
  1. **Element (nature):** materials, labour, expenses.
  2. **Function:** production, administration, selling and distribution (also research and development).
  3. **Behaviour (activity level):** fixed, variable, semi-variable, stepped.
  4. **Traceability:** direct, indirect.
  5. **Controllability:** controllable, uncontrollable.
  6. **Relevance (decision making):** relevant, irrelevant (sunk, committed), opportunity, notional, incremental.
  7. **Normality:** normal, abnormal.
  8. **Association with the product (inventory):** product cost, period cost.
  9. **Time:** historical, predetermined (estimated, standard).

**Explain more**

- Exam trap: "adaptability of cost", "colour of cost" or "size of cost" are **not** bases. Behaviour, relevance and function **are**.
- The same cost can sit in several groups at once. Flour in a Lagos bakery is a material (element), a production cost (function), a variable cost (behaviour), a direct cost (traceability) and a product cost (inventory).

### Lesson 2.2: Elements of cost, direct vs indirect, and function

**Must know**

- **Elements of cost:** materials, labour and expenses. Each element is either direct or indirect.
- **Direct cost:** can be traced to (identified with) a specific cost unit, e.g. leather in a shoe, wages of plasterers in a construction company.
- **Indirect cost (overhead):** cannot be traced to a single unit or it is uneconomic to do so; shared by many units, e.g. factory rent, the maintenance manager's salary.
- **Direct expenses** (also called **chargeable expenses**): expenses, other than materials and labour, incurred for a specific job or unit. Examples: royalty paid per unit produced, hire of special equipment for a particular order, special design or drawings for one job, subcontract work on one order.

| | Direct | Indirect (overhead) |
|---|---|---|
| Materials | Wood in a table, leather in a shoe | Glue, oil, cleaning materials |
| Labour | Machine operators, plasterers, bakers | Supervisors, storekeepers, cleaners |
| Expenses | Royalty per unit, special tool hire | Rent, electricity, insurance, depreciation |

- **Prime cost** = Direct materials + Direct labour + Direct expenses. Prime cost is a **direct** cost (the total of all direct costs).
- **Overhead** = Indirect materials + Indirect labour + Indirect expenses.
- **Classification by function:**
  1. **Production (factory, manufacturing) overhead:** the total of all indirect costs associated with manufacturing.
  2. **Administration overhead:** costs of formulating policy, directing and controlling the organisation (directors' and chief accountant's salaries, head-office rent).
  3. **Selling and distribution overhead:** selling = creating and stimulating demand (advertising, salesmen); distribution = getting goods to customers (delivery vans, warehousing).
- **Total cost** = Prime cost + all overheads (production, administration, selling and distribution).

**Explain more**

- Royalty **per unit produced** is a direct expense. Royalty based on **sales** is a selling overhead. Read the wording.
- Director's salary, advertising and general electricity are **not** direct expenses: they cannot be traced to one unit.
- In a construction company the plasterers work on the building itself, so their wages are direct labour. A chief accountant at John Holt Plc, a general manager at Zenith Bank and a maintenance manager at Dangote Plc are all indirect: none of them works on a specific unit.
- Prime cost is sometimes defined as direct materials plus direct labour only. When a question lists direct expenses, include them in prime cost.

### Lesson 2.3: Classification by behaviour

**Must know**

- **Cost behaviour:** the way total cost and cost per unit are affected by changes in the level of activity.
- **Relevant range:** the range of activity over which the assumed behaviour holds.

| Cost | Total cost as output rises | Cost per unit as output rises | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Fixed** | Stays the same (within the relevant range) | Falls | Factory rent, insurance, salaried manager, straight-line depreciation |
| **Variable** | Rises in direct proportion | Stays constant | Direct materials, piece-rate wages, royalty per unit |
| **Semi-variable (mixed)** | Rises, but not in proportion | Falls | Electricity, telephone, salesman's salary plus commission |
| **Stepped (step-fixed)** | Fixed over a range, then jumps | Falls within each step | Supervisors' salaries, extra rented warehouse |

- **Fixed cost:** a cost which stays the same in total for a period, even when output changes (within the relevant range). Incurred even when nothing is produced.
- **Variable cost:** a cost incurred only when production takes place, rising in total as output rises. Variable cost **per unit remains constant** as output changes.
- **Semi-variable cost:** partly fixed and partly variable. Example: a phone bill = fixed line rental + charge per call.
- **Stepped cost:** fixed up to a certain activity level, then rises by a fixed amount to a new level.
- Total cost = Total fixed cost + (Variable cost per unit × Units)
- Fixed cost per unit = Total fixed cost ÷ Units

**Explain more**

- Classic swap: a variable cost is **fixed per unit** and variable in total; a fixed cost is **fixed in total** and variable per unit. Options such as "variable cost remains fixed in total" are wrong.
- Factory rent is **fixed**, not semi-variable. Electricity (standing charge + units used), telephone (rental + calls) and a salesman's salary (basic pay + commission) are semi-variable.
- **Worked example (unit cost at a new output).** A Lagos bakery's cost per loaf is ₦200 when it bakes 5,000 loaves a month; 60% of this unit cost is variable. Find the cost per loaf at 8,000 loaves.
  - Variable cost per loaf = 60% × ₦200 = ₦120 (does not change with output).
  - Fixed cost per loaf at 5,000 = ₦200 − ₦120 = ₦80, so total fixed cost = ₦80 × 5,000 = ₦400,000.
  - At 8,000 loaves: fixed cost per loaf = ₦400,000 ÷ 8,000 = ₦50.
  - New cost per loaf = ₦120 + ₦50 = **₦170** (total cost ₦1,360,000).
  - Trap: do not keep ₦200, and do not scale the whole ₦200. Only the fixed part is spread more thinly. If output **falls** to 4,000, fixed cost per loaf rises to ₦100 and unit cost becomes ₦220.
- **Stepped example.** A Kano flour mill needs one supervisor, paid ₦150,000 a month, for every 1,000 bags (or part of 1,000) per month. At 2,500 bags it needs 3 supervisors: ₦450,000. From 2,001 to 3,000 bags the cost is fixed at ₦450,000; at 3,001 bags it steps up to ₦600,000.
- Separating a semi-variable cost into its fixed and variable parts (the high-low method) is taught in Topic 3.

### Lesson 2.4: Controllability, relevance, normality, product and period costs

**Must know**

- **Controllable cost:** can be influenced by the actions of a specified manager (budget holder).
- **Uncontrollable cost:** **cannot** be influenced by the actions of a specified manager (it may be controllable at a higher level).
- **Responsibility centres** (classification by responsibility):

| Centre | Manager is responsible for |
|---|---|
| Cost centre | Costs only |
| Revenue centre | Revenues only |
| Profit centre | Costs **and** revenues (hence profit) |
| Investment centre | Costs, revenues and capital investment (return on investment) |

- **Relevant cost:** the cost considered when taking a decision: a **future, incremental cash** cost that **differs** between the options.
- **Sunk cost:** a cost already incurred that **cannot** be altered by any future decision. Sunk costs are **not relevant** for decision making. Examples: book value of an existing asset, depreciation of machines already bought, cost of resources acquired in the past.
- **Opportunity cost:** the benefit given up by choosing one option instead of the next best alternative.
- **Notional (imputed) cost:** a cost used in cost accounts that involves no actual cash outflow, e.g. interest on the owner's own capital, rent on premises the owner already owns.
- **Incremental (differential) cost:** the difference in total cost between two options or two activity levels.
- **Avoidable cost:** can be saved if an activity stops. **Unavoidable cost:** will be incurred anyway.
- **Normal cost:** normally incurred at a given level of output under normal conditions; included in product cost.
- **Abnormal cost:** **not** normally incurred at a given level of output (e.g. strike, fire, flood); excluded from product cost and written off to profit and loss.
- **Product cost:** cost of making the goods (direct materials, direct labour, direct expenses, production overhead); included in stock value until the goods are sold.
- **Period cost:** charged against profit of the period in which it is incurred (administration, selling and distribution).
- **Unexpired cost:** cost of resources acquired that are expected to contribute to **future** revenue (e.g. closing stock, an asset); shown as an asset. **Expired cost:** already used up in earning revenue; shown as an expense.

**Explain more**

- **Worked example (relevance).** An Aba shoe factory bought a machine three years ago for ₦2,000,000; its book value is ₦800,000. It is deciding whether to buy a new machine for ₦3,500,000. The ₦800,000 book value and past depreciation are **sunk**: they cannot change whatever is chosen. The ₦3,500,000 for the **new** plant is a future cost that depends on the decision, so it is **relevant** and is **not** a sunk cost.
- If the owner could rent the factory floor out for ₦100,000 a month instead of using it, that ₦100,000 is an opportunity cost of using it.
- Sunk costs, committed costs and notional costs are typical irrelevant costs; do not mix up "notional" (no cash flow) with "sunk" (already paid).
- A product cost held in closing stock is an unexpired cost; once the goods are sold it becomes cost of sales, an expired cost.

### Lesson 2.5: Costing methods vs costing techniques

**Must know**

- **Costing method:** the way costs are **collected and ascertained**, chosen to suit the type of product or service.
- **Costing technique:** a way of **presenting and using** costs for control and decision making; it can be applied with any method.

**Costing methods:**
1. **Job costing:** each job or order is costed separately (printing, car repairs, furniture repairs).
2. **Batch costing:** a batch of identical items is costed as one job, then cost per unit = batch cost ÷ units (bread, shoes, medicines).
3. **Contract costing:** large, long jobs at a site, often over a year (building a bridge, roads, shipbuilding).
4. **Process costing:** continuous production through stages; output of one process is input of the next (refineries, cement, soap, beverages).
5. **Unit (single or output) costing:** one uniform product made continuously; cost per unit = total cost ÷ units (brick making, coal mining, flour milling, quarrying).
6. **Operating (service) costing:** costing services rather than goods (hospitals, hotels, transport, electricity, canteen, a factory's boiler house).
7. **Multiple (composite) costing:** a combination of methods for complex assembled products (cars, motorcycles).

**Costing techniques:**
1. Marginal costing
2. Absorption costing
3. Standard costing
4. Historical costing
5. Budgetary control
6. Uniform costing
7. Direct costing

**Explain more**

- Biggest trap: **standard costing is a technique, not a method**; **job costing is a method, not a technique**. "Output costing" is another name for unit costing, so it **is** a method.
- Match industry to method: a private hospital in Lagos, steam from a boiler house, a bus company, a hotel = **operating (service)** costing. Brick making and coal mining = **unit (output)** costing. Building a bridge = **contract** costing. Printing and furniture repairs = **job** costing.

### Lesson 2.6: Elements of cost and the cost sheet build-up

**Must know**

**Cost sheet layout (memorise the order):**

1. Raw materials consumed = Opening stock + Purchases − Closing stock
2. **Prime cost** = Direct materials consumed + Direct labour + Direct expenses
3. Add production (factory) overhead; add opening WIP, less closing WIP
4. = **Factory (works) cost** (also called net works cost)
5. Add administration overhead = **Cost of production**
6. Add opening stock of finished goods, less closing stock of finished goods = **Cost of goods sold**
7. Add selling and distribution overhead = **Total cost (cost of sales)**
8. Add profit = **Selling price**

- **Conversion cost:** the cost of converting raw materials into finished goods = Direct labour + Direct expenses + Production overhead (= Factory cost − Direct materials).
- Profit as a **margin** (percentage of selling price): Profit = Total cost × m ÷ (100 − m), where m is the percentage. Selling price = Total cost ÷ (1 − m/100).
- Profit as a **mark-up** (percentage of cost): Profit = Total cost × mark-up %.
- Items of a purely financial nature are **left out** of the cost sheet: interest paid, dividends, income tax, donations, goodwill written off, profit or loss on sale of assets, cash discounts.

**Explain more**

- **Worked example.** An Aba shoe factory made 4,000 pairs. Opening raw materials ₦40,000; purchases ₦300,000; closing raw materials ₦50,000; direct labour ₦180,000; royalty per pair ₦30,000 in total; factory overhead ₦120,000; opening WIP ₦25,000; closing WIP ₦45,000; administration overhead ₦80,000; opening finished goods ₦34,000; closing finished goods ₦51,000; selling and distribution overhead ₦57,000. Profit is 20% of selling price.

| | ₦ |
|---|---|
| Opening raw materials | 40,000 |
| Add purchases | 300,000 |
| Less closing raw materials | (50,000) |
| Direct materials consumed | 290,000 |
| Direct labour | 180,000 |
| Direct expenses (royalty) | 30,000 |
| **Prime cost** | **500,000** |
| Factory overhead | 120,000 |
| Add opening WIP | 25,000 |
| Less closing WIP | (45,000) |
| **Factory cost** | **600,000** |
| Administration overhead | 80,000 |
| **Cost of production** (₦170 per pair) | **680,000** |
| Add opening finished goods | 34,000 |
| Less closing finished goods | (51,000) |
| **Cost of goods sold** | **663,000** |
| Selling and distribution overhead | 57,000 |
| **Total cost (cost of sales)** | **720,000** |
| Profit (20% of selling price) | 180,000 |
| **Selling price** | **900,000** |

  - Cost of production per pair = ₦680,000 ÷ 4,000 = ₦170.
  - Profit: selling price = ₦720,000 ÷ 0.80 = ₦900,000, so profit = ₦180,000 (that is ₦720,000 × 20 ÷ 80). A 25% mark-up on cost gives the same ₦180,000.
  - Conversion cost = ₦180,000 + ₦30,000 + ₦120,000 = ₦330,000.
- **Margin trap.** Total cost ₦10,000, profit 25% of selling price: profit = ₦10,000 × 25 ÷ 75 = ₦3,333.33, about **₦3,333** (selling price ₦13,333). ₦2,500 would be 25% of **cost**, which is a mark-up, not a margin.
- **Purchased vs consumed trap.** Prime cost uses materials **consumed**, never materials purchased. With materials purchased ₦100,000, consumed ₦90,000, direct labour ₦60,000, direct expenses ₦20,000 and manufacturing overhead ₦30,000, prime cost = ₦90,000 + ₦60,000 + ₦20,000 = **₦170,000** (overhead is not part of prime cost).
- **"Already adjusted" trap.** If a question gives "net works cost after adjusting for WIP", do **not** adjust for WIP again. Example: net works cost ₦200,000; administration ₦100,000 gives cost of production ₦300,000; less closing finished goods ₦30,000 (no opening stock) gives cost of goods sold ₦270,000; add selling overhead ₦10,000 gives cost of sales **₦280,000**.

### One-minute recap

- Cost classification = arranging costs in logical groups by nature or purpose; its main aim is to help management in various decision areas.
- Bases: element, function, behaviour, traceability, controllability, relevance, normality, product vs period, time.
- Elements of cost = materials, labour, expenses; each direct or indirect. Direct expenses = chargeable expenses (royalty per unit, special equipment hire).
- Fixed: constant in total, falls per unit. Variable: constant per unit, rises in total. Semi-variable: electricity, telephone, salesman's pay. Stepped: jumps at set levels.
- Sunk costs are past and irrelevant; relevant costs are future, incremental and differ between options; notional costs have no cash outflow.
- Uncontrollable costs cannot be influenced by the specified manager; a profit centre manager answers for costs and revenues.
- Methods (job, batch, contract, process, unit, operating, multiple) vs techniques (marginal, absorption, standard, historical, budgetary control, uniform).
- Cost sheet: prime cost, factory cost, cost of production, cost of goods sold, total cost, selling price. Conversion cost = direct labour + direct expenses + production overhead.

### Theory practice

**Question:** Ikorodu Furniture Ltd gives you: direct materials consumed ₦250,000; direct labour ₦150,000; direct expenses ₦40,000; factory overhead ₦110,000; administration overhead ₦60,000. Calculate (a) prime cost, (b) conversion cost, (c) factory cost and (d) cost of production.

*Solution:*
- (a) Prime cost = ₦250,000 + ₦150,000 + ₦40,000 = **₦440,000**.
- (b) Conversion cost = ₦150,000 + ₦40,000 + ₦110,000 = **₦300,000**.
- (c) Factory cost = ₦440,000 + ₦110,000 = **₦550,000** (check: materials ₦250,000 + conversion ₦300,000 = ₦550,000).
- (d) Cost of production = ₦550,000 + ₦60,000 = **₦610,000**.
