## Topic 8: Marginal and Absorption Costing

**What you must know:** marginal cost and marginal (variable) costing; absorption (full) costing; contribution and how to compute it per unit and in total; the distinction between the two techniques; how each values stock; preparing marginal and absorption profit statements, including under- and over-absorption; reconciling the two profits.

### Lesson 8.1: Nature of marginal and absorption costing

**Must know**

- **Marginal cost:** the variable cost of one unit of a product or service, which would be avoided if that unit were not produced. In the cost accountant's sense, marginal production cost per unit = direct materials + direct labour + direct expenses + variable production overhead per unit (that is, prime cost + variable overhead); any variable selling cost per unit is added to this to give the full marginal cost used for contribution.
- **Marginal costing:** a costing technique in which only **variable costs** are charged to cost units (products). **Fixed costs** are treated as **period costs** and written off in full against the contribution of the period in which they arise. Marginal costing is also known as **variable costing** (and as **direct costing**).
- **Absorption costing:** a costing technique in which **all production costs, fixed and variable**, are charged to cost units. Fixed production overhead is absorbed into units using a predetermined overhead absorption rate. Absorption costing is also known as **full costing** (or total costing).
- **Contribution:** the difference between sales and the marginal (variable) cost of sales. It "contributes" first to covering fixed costs and then to profit.
- **Basic marginal costing equation:** Sales − Variable cost = Contribution = Fixed cost + Profit (or − Loss).
- **Features of marginal costing:**
  1. Costs are split into fixed and variable (semi-variable costs are separated, for example by the high-low method).
  2. Only variable costs are charged to products and included in stock values.
  3. Fixed costs are period costs, charged in full to the period's profit and loss.
  4. Stock (work-in-progress and finished goods) is valued at marginal (variable) production cost.
  5. Profitability of products is judged by their **contribution**.
  6. Marginal costing is a **technique**, not a method: it can be used alongside job, batch or process costing.
- **Advantages of marginal costing:**
  1. Simple to understand and operate; no arbitrary apportionment of fixed costs to products.
  2. Profit moves with **sales**, not with production, so profit cannot be inflated by building up stock.
  3. No under- or over-absorption of fixed overheads.
  4. Contribution helps decisions: product mix, make or buy, special orders, pricing, shutting down a product, break-even.
  5. Helps cost control, because fixed and variable costs are reported separately.
- **Limitations of marginal costing:**
  1. Splitting costs into fixed and variable is difficult and sometimes arbitrary.
  2. Stock is undervalued, since fixed production overhead is left out; not acceptable for published financial statements (IAS 2 requires production overheads to be included in stock).
  3. Ignores fixed costs in pricing, which can lead to prices that fail to cover total cost in the long run.
  4. Fixed costs are not always fixed, and variable costs do not always vary in a straight line.
  5. Less useful where fixed costs form most of total cost (for example, highly automated factories).
- **Advantages of absorption costing:** complies with IAS 2 for financial reporting; recognises that fixed production costs are part of making a product; avoids showing big losses in seasonal businesses that build stock before the selling season; full cost is a sound base for long-run pricing.
- **Limitations of absorption costing:** apportionment and absorption of fixed overhead are arbitrary; profit can be raised simply by producing more than is sold; under- or over-absorption arises; full cost per unit changes with the level of output, which misleads decisions.

**Explain more**

- The word "marginal" in economics means the cost of one extra unit. In cost accounting, marginal cost per unit is the **variable cost per unit**, because producing one more unit adds only variable cost; fixed cost stays the same.
- Trap: marginal costing is also known as **variable costing**, not absorption costing or full costing (those two are the same thing as each other), and not standard costing (a separate technique, Topic 10).
- Trap: "the variable cost of one unit which would be avoided if that unit were not produced" defines **marginal cost**, not average total cost (which includes fixed cost) and not sunk cost (a past cost that cannot be recovered).
- Both techniques treat **non-production costs** (administration, selling and distribution) as period costs. The only cost that is treated differently is **fixed production overhead**.
- Variable selling costs (for example, sales commission per unit) are part of marginal cost for working out contribution, but they are **never** included in stock values, under either technique, because they arise only when goods are sold.

### Lesson 8.2: Contribution and profit under marginal costing

**Must know**

- **Contribution per unit = Selling price per unit − Variable cost per unit.**
- **Total contribution = Sales − Total variable cost = Contribution per unit × Units sold.**
- **Profit = Total contribution − Fixed costs.**
- **Contribution to sales (C/S) ratio = Contribution ÷ Sales × 100%** (also called the profit-volume or P/V ratio; used fully in Topic 9).
- Contribution per unit stays **constant** at every level of activity (as long as selling price and variable cost per unit do not change); profit per unit does **not**, because fixed cost per unit falls as output rises.
- Fixed costs are **excluded** when computing contribution.

| Item | Marginal costing view |
|---|---|
| Variable cost per unit | Constant |
| Total variable cost | Rises with output |
| Fixed cost in total | Constant (for the period) |
| Fixed cost per unit | Falls as output rises |
| Contribution per unit | Constant |

**Explain more**

**Worked example 1: contribution per unit.** 100,000 units of product JIG were produced and sold. Sales ₦500,000; variable production cost ₦175,000; fixed production cost ₦75,000.

1. Total contribution = Sales − Variable cost = ₦500,000 − ₦175,000 = ₦325,000.
2. Contribution per unit = ₦325,000 ÷ 100,000 = **₦3.25**.
3. Check by unit figures: selling price = ₦500,000 ÷ 100,000 = ₦5.00; variable cost = ₦175,000 ÷ 100,000 = ₦1.75; contribution = ₦5.00 − ₦1.75 = ₦3.25.
4. Profit = ₦325,000 − ₦75,000 = ₦250,000 (₦2.50 per unit).

Traps: ₦5 is the selling price, not the contribution. ₦2.50 is profit per unit (it deducts fixed cost). Never deduct fixed cost when asked for contribution.

**Worked example 2: profit under marginal costing.** Eko Plastics Ltd: fixed cost ₦500,000 for the quarter; variable cost ₦10 per unit; selling price ₦15 per unit; output and sales 150,000 units.

1. Contribution per unit = ₦15 − ₦10 = ₦5.
2. Total contribution = 150,000 × ₦5 = ₦750,000.
3. Profit = ₦750,000 − ₦500,000 = **₦250,000**.

In statement form:

| | ₦ |
|---|---|
| Sales (150,000 × ₦15) | 2,250,000 |
| Less variable cost (150,000 × ₦10) | (1,500,000) |
| Contribution | 750,000 |
| Less fixed cost | (500,000) |
| Profit | 250,000 |

C/S ratio = 750,000 ÷ 2,250,000 × 100% = 33.33% (rounded to two decimal places). Traps: ₦750,000 is contribution, not profit; ₦1,750,000 wrongly adds instead of deducting.

**Why contribution guides decisions.** If Eko Plastics sold one extra unit, profit would rise by exactly ₦5 (its contribution), because fixed cost does not change. Under absorption costing, a "full cost" per unit of ₦10 + (₦500,000 ÷ 150,000) = ₦13.33 (rounded) makes the extra unit seem to earn only ₦1.67, which is wrong for a short-term decision.

### Lesson 8.3: Distinction between the two techniques and stock valuation

**Must know**

| Point | Marginal (variable) costing | Absorption (full) costing |
|---|---|---|
| Costs charged to products | Variable production costs only | All production costs, fixed and variable |
| Fixed production overhead | Period cost: written off in full in the period incurred | Product cost: absorbed into units using a predetermined rate |
| Stock valuation | At variable (marginal) production cost | At full production cost (variable + absorbed fixed production overhead) |
| Key profit measure | Contribution, then net profit | Gross profit, then net profit |
| Under/over-absorption | Does not arise | Arises and must be adjusted in the profit statement |
| Profit depends on | Sales volume only | Sales **and** production volume |
| Use | Internal decision-making and control | Financial reporting (IAS 2), long-run pricing |
| Presentation of costs | By behaviour (variable, then fixed) | By function (production, administration, selling) |

- **Fixed production overhead absorption rate (OAR) = Budgeted fixed production overhead ÷ Budgeted (normal) production volume.**
- **Stock value under marginal costing = Units in stock × Variable production cost per unit.**
- **Stock value under absorption costing = Units in stock × (Variable production cost per unit + Fixed OAR).**
- **Difference in stock values = Units in stock × Fixed OAR.**
- **Fixed overhead absorbed = Actual production × OAR.**
- **Under-absorption:** absorbed < actual fixed overhead; added to cost of sales (reduces profit). **Over-absorption:** absorbed > actual fixed overhead; deducted from cost of sales (increases profit).

**Explain more**

**Worked example 3: stock valuation.** A Kano flour mill makes 50 kg bags of flour. Variable production cost is ₦18,000 a bag (wheat, direct labour, variable milling overhead). Fixed production overhead is budgeted at ₦6,000,000 a month for a normal output of 4,000 bags. At month end, 500 bags are unsold.

1. OAR = ₦6,000,000 ÷ 4,000 = ₦1,500 a bag.
2. Full production cost a bag = ₦18,000 + ₦1,500 = ₦19,500.
3. Closing stock, marginal costing = 500 × ₦18,000 = **₦9,000,000**.
4. Closing stock, absorption costing = 500 × ₦19,500 = **₦9,750,000**.
5. Difference = 500 × ₦1,500 = ₦750,000 of fixed overhead "carried forward" in stock under absorption costing.

**Under- and over-absorption.** Suppose the mill actually produced 4,400 bags and actual fixed overhead was exactly ₦6,000,000.

1. Absorbed = 4,400 × ₦1,500 = ₦6,600,000.
2. Over-absorbed = ₦6,600,000 − ₦6,000,000 = **₦600,000** (deduct from cost of sales).

If actual fixed overhead had been ₦6,300,000 instead, over-absorption = ₦6,600,000 − ₦6,300,000 = ₦300,000. Under- or over-absorption therefore comes from two causes: actual production differing from normal production (volume), and actual fixed overhead differing from budget (expenditure).

Traps: selling and administration costs (fixed or variable) and variable selling costs are **never** included in stock. The only reason stock values differ between the two techniques is fixed production overhead.

### Lesson 8.4: Preparing marginal and absorption profit statements

**Must know**

**Marginal costing statement (layout):**

1. Sales
2. Less variable cost of sales: opening stock (at variable cost) + variable production cost − closing stock (at variable cost)
3. = Production contribution (if you wish to show it)
4. Less variable selling and distribution costs
5. = **Contribution**
6. Less fixed costs (fixed production overhead actually incurred, fixed administration, fixed selling)
7. = **Net profit**

**Absorption costing statement (layout):**

1. Sales
2. Less cost of sales: opening stock (at full cost) + production cost (at full cost) − closing stock (at full cost)
3. Add under-absorbed overhead **or** deduct over-absorbed overhead
4. = **Gross profit**
5. Less non-production costs (administration, selling and distribution, both variable and fixed)
6. = **Net profit**

- **Cost of production** (absorption) = units produced × full production cost per unit; (marginal) = units produced × variable production cost per unit.
- Closing stock units = Opening stock + Production − Sales.

**Explain more**

**Worked example 4: full statements for two months.** An Aba shoe factory makes one style of leather shoe. Data per pair: selling price ₦5,000; direct materials ₦1,200; direct labour ₦500; variable production overhead ₦300; variable selling cost (commission) ₦250 per pair sold. Fixed production overhead ₦3,000,000 a month, budgeted on a normal output of 2,000 pairs. Fixed administration and selling costs ₦1,000,000 a month. Actual fixed costs equal budget. There is no opening stock in Month 1.

| | Month 1 | Month 2 |
|---|---|---|
| Production (pairs) | 2,000 | 1,800 |
| Sales (pairs) | 1,600 | 2,100 |

Preliminary workings:

1. Variable production cost a pair = ₦1,200 + ₦500 + ₦300 = ₦2,000.
2. OAR = ₦3,000,000 ÷ 2,000 = ₦1,500 a pair.
3. Full production cost a pair = ₦2,000 + ₦1,500 = ₦3,500.
4. Stock: Month 1 closing = 0 + 2,000 − 1,600 = 400 pairs. Month 2 closing = 400 + 1,800 − 2,100 = 100 pairs.
5. Absorption: Month 1 absorbed 2,000 × ₦1,500 = ₦3,000,000 (no under/over). Month 2 absorbed 1,800 × ₦1,500 = ₦2,700,000, so **under-absorbed ₦300,000**.

**Marginal costing profit statement**

| | Month 1 ₦ | Month 2 ₦ |
|---|---|---|
| Sales (1,600 / 2,100 × ₦5,000) | 8,000,000 | 10,500,000 |
| Opening stock (× ₦2,000) | 0 | 800,000 |
| Add variable production cost (2,000 / 1,800 × ₦2,000) | 4,000,000 | 3,600,000 |
| Less closing stock (400 / 100 × ₦2,000) | (800,000) | (200,000) |
| Variable production cost of sales | 3,200,000 | 4,200,000 |
| Add variable selling cost (× ₦250) | 400,000 | 525,000 |
| Total variable cost of sales | (3,600,000) | (4,725,000) |
| **Contribution** | 4,400,000 | 5,775,000 |
| Less fixed production overhead | (3,000,000) | (3,000,000) |
| Less fixed administration and selling | (1,000,000) | (1,000,000) |
| **Net profit** | **400,000** | **1,775,000** |

Check: contribution per pair sold = ₦5,000 − ₦2,000 − ₦250 = ₦2,750; 1,600 × ₦2,750 = ₦4,400,000 and 2,100 × ₦2,750 = ₦5,775,000.

**Absorption costing profit statement**

| | Month 1 ₦ | Month 2 ₦ |
|---|---|---|
| Sales | 8,000,000 | 10,500,000 |
| Opening stock (× ₦3,500) | 0 | 1,400,000 |
| Add production cost (2,000 / 1,800 × ₦3,500) | 7,000,000 | 6,300,000 |
| Less closing stock (400 / 100 × ₦3,500) | (1,400,000) | (350,000) |
| Cost of sales at full cost | 5,600,000 | 7,350,000 |
| Add under-absorbed fixed overhead | 0 | 300,000 |
| Adjusted cost of sales | (5,600,000) | (7,650,000) |
| **Gross profit** | 2,400,000 | 2,850,000 |
| Less variable selling cost | (400,000) | (525,000) |
| Less fixed administration and selling | (1,000,000) | (1,000,000) |
| **Net profit** | **1,000,000** | **1,325,000** |

Notice: in Month 1, production exceeded sales and absorption profit was higher. In Month 2, sales exceeded production (stock fell) and marginal profit was higher. Over the two months together, marginal profit = ₦2,175,000 and absorption profit = ₦2,325,000; the gap of ₦150,000 is the fixed overhead in the 100 pairs still in stock (100 × ₦1,500).

Traps: under absorption costing, do **not** deduct fixed production overhead again below gross profit; it is already in cost of sales through the OAR, corrected by the under/over-absorption line. Under marginal costing, deduct the **actual** fixed production overhead incurred, not the amount absorbed.

### Lesson 8.5: Reconciling marginal and absorption profits

**Must know**

- **Difference in profit = Change in stock units × Fixed production OAR per unit.**
- Equivalently: Difference = (Closing stock units − Opening stock units) × OAR.
- Rules:
  1. **Stock increases** (production > sales): **absorption profit is higher**, because some of the period's fixed overhead is carried forward in closing stock.
  2. **Stock decreases** (sales > production): **marginal profit is higher**, because absorption costing releases fixed overhead from earlier periods through opening stock.
  3. **No change in stock** (production = sales): **both profits are the same**.
- Over the long run (when all stock is sold), total profits under both techniques are equal; the difference is only a matter of **timing**.
- **Reconciliation statement:** Marginal costing profit + (increase in stock × OAR) or − (decrease in stock × OAR) = Absorption costing profit.
- If OAR changes between periods, use the fixed overhead **in each stock figure**: Difference = Fixed overhead in closing stock − Fixed overhead in opening stock.

**Explain more**

**Worked example 5: reconciling the Aba shoe factory profits.**

| | Month 1 ₦ | Month 2 ₦ |
|---|---|---|
| Marginal costing profit | 400,000 | 1,775,000 |
| Add fixed overhead in closing stock (400 / 100 × ₦1,500) | 600,000 | 150,000 |
| Less fixed overhead in opening stock (0 / 400 × ₦1,500) | 0 | (600,000) |
| **Absorption costing profit** | **1,000,000** | **1,325,000** |

Short method: Month 1 stock rose by 400 pairs: 400 × ₦1,500 = ₦600,000, absorption higher. Month 2 stock fell by 300 pairs: 300 × ₦1,500 = ₦450,000, marginal higher (₦1,775,000 − ₦1,325,000 = ₦450,000).

Why it happens, in plain words: in Month 1, the shoe factory spent ₦3,000,000 on fixed production overhead. Marginal costing charged all of it to Month 1. Absorption costing charged only the share in the 1,600 pairs sold and parked ₦600,000 in the 400 pairs on the shelf, to be charged in Month 2 when those pairs were sold.

Exam traps:

- Use the **fixed production** OAR only; never include variable cost, selling or administration costs in the reconciliation.
- Use the change in stock **units**, not the units produced or sold.
- The under-absorbed ₦300,000 in Month 2 is **not** part of the reconciliation figure; it appears in the absorption statement only, and the reconciliation still works on stock change alone.
- A manager judged on absorption profit can raise reported profit by over-producing to build stock. This is a key argument for marginal costing in internal reporting.

### One-minute recap

- Marginal cost = variable cost of one unit, avoided if that unit is not produced.
- Marginal costing (variable or direct costing) charges only variable costs to products; fixed costs are period costs.
- Absorption costing (full costing) charges all production costs, fixed and variable, to products.
- Contribution = Sales − Variable cost of sales = Fixed cost + Profit; contribution per unit = Selling price − Variable cost per unit.
- Profit under marginal costing = Total contribution − Fixed costs.
- Stock: marginal at variable production cost; absorption at full production cost. Difference = units × fixed OAR.
- OAR = Budgeted fixed production overhead ÷ Normal output; under-absorption adds to cost of sales, over-absorption reduces it.
- Profit difference = Change in stock units × OAR: stock up, absorption higher; stock down, marginal higher; no change, equal.
- Absorption costing is required for financial reporting (IAS 2); marginal costing is better for decisions.

### Theory practice

**Question:** A Lagos bakery makes a cake that sells for ₦200. Variable production cost is ₦120 a cake. Fixed production overhead is ₦400,000 a month, absorbed on a normal output of 10,000 cakes. Fixed administration cost is ₦200,000 a month. In the month, 10,000 cakes were produced and 8,000 sold, with no opening stock. Calculate (a) closing stock under each technique, (b) profit under each technique and (c) reconcile the two profits.

*Solution:*
1. OAR = ₦400,000 ÷ 10,000 = ₦40; full production cost = ₦120 + ₦40 = ₦160. Closing stock = 10,000 − 8,000 = 2,000 cakes.
2. (a) Marginal: 2,000 × ₦120 = **₦240,000**. Absorption: 2,000 × ₦160 = **₦320,000**.
3. (b) Marginal: contribution = 8,000 × (₦200 − ₦120) = ₦640,000; profit = ₦640,000 − ₦400,000 − ₦200,000 = **₦40,000**.
4. Absorption: sales 8,000 × ₦200 = ₦1,600,000; cost of sales 8,000 × ₦160 = ₦1,280,000 (absorbed 10,000 × ₦40 = ₦400,000, so no under/over-absorption); gross profit ₦320,000; net profit = ₦320,000 − ₦200,000 = **₦120,000**.
5. (c) Stock rose by 2,000 cakes: 2,000 × ₦40 = ₦80,000. Marginal profit ₦40,000 + ₦80,000 = absorption profit ₦120,000.
