## Topic 7: Theory of Firms

**What you must know:** what a firm is; the traditional, managerial and behavioural theories of the firm and their objectives; the types of firms (sole proprietorship, partnership, private and public limited liability companies, public enterprises) with their features, advantages and disadvantages; how companies are formed and financed in Nigeria (CAC, CAMA 2020); privatisation and commercialisation.

### Lesson 7.1: What a firm is and theories of the firm

**Must know**

- **Firm:** a business organisation that brings together the factors of production (land, labour, capital, entrepreneurship) to produce goods or services for sale, usually to make a profit. A group of firms producing the same product is an **industry**; a single factory or production unit is a **plant**.
- **Why firms exist (Ronald Coase, 1937):** using the market for every transaction has costs (searching, bargaining, contracting). A firm exists because organising some activities inside it is cheaper than buying them through the market (**transaction-cost theory**).

**Theories (objectives) of the firm:**

1. **Traditional (neoclassical) theory: profit maximisation.** The firm aims to **maximise profit by producing where marginal revenue equals marginal cost (MR = MC)**. Assumptions:
    - the firm is **managed by its owner-entrepreneur**;
    - it has a **single goal of maximum profit**;
    - it has **full knowledge of its costs and revenues**;
    - it acts rationally.
2. **Managerial theories:** arose because in large modern companies **ownership by shareholders is separated from control by professional managers** (Berle and Means, 1932). Managers pursue their own goals, subject to keeping shareholders satisfied.
    - **Sales revenue maximisation (William Baumol):** managers make **total sales revenue as large as possible, provided profit does not fall below the minimum level that keeps shareholders satisfied**.
    - **Managerial utility maximisation (Oliver Williamson):** managers maximise their own satisfaction by spending on **extra staff, perks (luxury cars, big offices) and discretionary investment**, as long as profits stay acceptable to shareholders.
    - **Growth maximisation (Robin Marris):** managers aim at the **fastest possible expansion** of the firm, **subject to keeping the firm safe from takeover** (job security).
3. **Behavioural theory (Cyert and March; Herbert Simon):** the firm is **a coalition of groups (managers, workers, shareholders, customers, suppliers) with different and often conflicting goals**. Because information is limited (bounded rationality), it **satisfices**: aims at **satisfactory levels of performance rather than the maximum possible**.
4. **Value (wealth) maximisation:** the firm aims to maximise the present value of expected future profits, i.e. the owners' wealth (share price).

**Explain more**

- Trap: "a coalition of groups that settles for satisfactory results" is the **behavioural** view, so it is **NOT** an assumption of the traditional profit-maximisation theory.
- Match the clue to the theory: "as large as possible ... minimum profit" = **Baumol (sales)**; "staff, official cars, offices, status" = **Williamson (managerial utility)**; "fastest expansion ... safe from takeover" = **Marris (growth)**; "satisfactory, not maximum" and "coalition" = **behavioural (satisficing)**; "MR = MC" and "owner-entrepreneur" = **traditional**.
- Why Baumol? Managers' pay, prestige and bank goodwill tend to rise with the size of sales, not profit. A Plc such as MTN Nigeria is run by hired executives, not by the thousands of shareholders who own it: the separation that managerial theories describe.
- The **principal-agent problem**: shareholders (principals) hire managers (agents) who may not act in the owners' interest. Boards, audits and share-based pay are ways to align them.

### Lesson 7.2: Sole proprietorship

**Must know**

- **Sole proprietorship:** a business **owned, financed and controlled by one person**, who **takes all the profit and bears all the risk**. It is the **most common form of business in Nigeria**, mainly because it is **easy and cheap to set up, with few legal formalities**.
- **Main source of capital:** **personal savings and loans from family and friends**; also small loans from banks, microfinance banks, cooperatives and esusu/ajo contribution groups.

**Features:** one owner; owner makes all decisions; **unlimited liability**; no separate legal personality (the owner and the business are the same in law); few legal formalities (only registration of a business name with the **Corporate Affairs Commission (CAC)** if it trades under a name other than the owner's).

**Advantages:**

1. **Easy and cheap to set up, with few legal formalities.**
2. **Quick decision-making** because the owner does not need to consult anyone.
3. **The owner keeps all the profits.**
4. Close personal contact with customers and workers.
5. Privacy: accounts need not be published.
6. Flexibility: easy to change line of business.

**Disadvantages:**

1. **Unlimited liability:** the owner's personal property can be taken to pay business debts.
2. **Limited capital** for expansion.
3. **Lack of continuity:** the business may die or close with the owner.
4. Heavy workload; the owner may lack some skills.
5. All losses are borne by one person.

**Explain more**

- Mama Bisi runs a provision shop alone. It fails owing suppliers ₦2 million, more than the shop's assets. Creditors take her **personal car**: this shows **unlimited liability**.
- Trap: "the owner keeps all the profits" is an **advantage**, so it is NOT a disadvantage. "Limited liability", "easy access to large capital" and "continuity after death" are NOT advantages of a sole proprietorship.
- Examples: barbing salons, POS agents, provision shops, tailors, roadside food sellers.

### Lesson 7.3: Partnership

**Must know**

- **Partnership:** the relationship between persons **carrying on a business in common with a view of profit**, who share capital, management and profits. In Nigeria, an ordinary partnership generally has **2 to 20 partners**; a larger association must register as a company (CAMA 2020 exempts partnerships of lawyers or of accountants from the 20 limit).
- **Deed of partnership:** the **written agreement that sets out the rights, duties and profit-sharing arrangements of the partners** (capital contributed, profit and loss ratio, salaries, interest on capital, admission, retirement and dissolution).

**Types of partners:**

1. **Active (managing, general) partner:** contributes capital and takes part in running the business.
2. **Sleeping (dormant) partner:** contributes capital and shares profit but **takes no part in running the business**.
3. **Nominal partner:** **allows his name to be used** to give the firm credibility, but **contributes no capital and takes no part in management**.
4. **Limited partner:** **liable only up to the capital contributed and does not take part in managing the business**. A limited partnership must have at least one general partner with unlimited liability.
5. **Minor partner:** a minor (below the age of majority) admitted to share in the benefits.

**Types of partnership:** ordinary (general) partnership; limited partnership; limited liability partnership (LLP), which CAMA 2020 allows to be registered with the CAC.

**Liability:** in an **ordinary (general) partnership**, liability is **unlimited**, so partners' private assets can be used to pay the firm's debts.

**Advantages (over a sole proprietorship):**

1. **More capital** can be raised from several owners.
2. **Partners share skills and can specialise.**
3. **Losses and responsibilities are shared.**
4. Better decisions from pooled ideas; easy to form.

**Disadvantages:**

1. **Unlimited liability** (for general partners).
2. **Each partner can be bound by the actions of the other partners** (mutual agency).
3. **Lack of continuity:** death, retirement or bankruptcy of a partner generally **dissolves** the partnership.
4. Disagreements between partners; slower decisions.
5. Capital still limited compared with a company; shares cannot be transferred without the others' consent.

**Explain more**

- Chidi puts ₦5 million into a partnership but takes no part in running it: he is a **sleeping (dormant) partner**. A famous retired judge lending only his name to a law firm, with no capital, is a **nominal partner**.
- Trap: "each partner can be bound by the actions of the other partners" is a **disadvantage**, NOT an advantage.
- If one of three lawyers in a partnership dies and there is no continuation clause, the partnership is generally **dissolved**, though the remaining partners may form a new one. It does not have perpetual succession.
- Partnerships are common among professionals: law, accounting and medical practices.

### Lesson 7.4: Limited liability companies

**Must know**

- **Limited liability:** shareholders can lose **no more than the amount they paid (or agreed to pay) for their shares**. It is a key benefit of **limited liability companies**, both **private (Ltd) and public (Plc)**, not of sole proprietorships or ordinary partnerships.
- **Separate legal entity:** the company **can own property, sue and be sued in its own name, apart from its owners**.
- **Perpetual succession:** the company **continues in existence even when its shareholders die or sell their shares**.
- **Registration:** companies are registered by the **Corporate Affairs Commission (CAC)**, which issues the **certificate of incorporation**, under the **Companies and Allied Matters Act (CAMA) 2020**.
- **Memorandum of association:** sets out the company's **name, objects and relationship with the outside world** (also its liability and share capital).
- **Articles of association:** the **internal rules** for running the company's meetings and management.

| Feature | Private limited company (Ltd) | Public limited company (Plc) |
|---|---|---|
| Sale of shares | **Cannot** invite the public; **restricts transfer** of shares | **Can invite the general public** to buy shares; shares freely transferable (may be listed on the Nigerian Exchange) |
| Members (CAMA 2020) | at least 1, at most 50 (employees not counted) | at least 2, no maximum |
| Name ends in | "Limited" / "Ltd" | "Plc" |
| Disclosure | less | **must publish much financial information** |
| Liability | limited | limited |
| Legal personality | separate | separate |

**Advantages of a Plc:** raises **very large amounts of capital from the public**; limited liability; perpetual succession; shares are easy to sell; easier borrowing; can hire professional managers.

**Disadvantages of a Plc:** **formation is costly with many legal requirements**; **must disclose much of its financial information to the public**; **ownership is separated from control**; slow, bureaucratic decisions; risk of takeover; heavy regulation.

**Advantages of an Ltd:** limited liability; separate legal personality; continuity; more privacy than a Plc; owners keep control. **Disadvantages:** cannot raise capital from the public; shares not freely transferable; more formalities than a sole proprietorship or partnership.

**Company finance:**

1. **Ordinary shares:** holders are **owners** who receive **variable dividends**, have **voting rights**, and are paid **last** when a company is wound up.
2. **Preference shares:** **fixed rate of dividend**, paid **before** ordinary shareholders, usually **limited voting rights**.
3. **Debentures:** loans to the company; holders are **creditors** who receive **fixed interest** whether or not there is profit, and are paid before shareholders.
4. **Internal finance:** **retained earnings (ploughed-back profits)**, sale of assets. **External finance:** new shares, debentures, bank loans and overdrafts.

**Explain more**

- Ade owns 10,000 fully paid shares in a company that goes into liquidation owing large debts. The most he can lose is **the amount he paid for his shares**; his house and car are safe.
- Trap: "shareholders of a Plc have unlimited liability" is false, so it is **NOT** a disadvantage of a Plc. Both Ltd and Plc give limited liability; that is **not** the difference between them. The key difference is that **a Plc can invite the public to buy its shares, while an Ltd cannot**.
- Examples of Plcs: Dangote Cement Plc, MTN Nigeria Communications Plc, Nestlé Nigeria Plc. Many family-owned businesses and start-ups register as Ltd.
- CAMA 2020 also recognises companies **limited by guarantee** (often non-profit, such as associations) and **unlimited companies**.
- Do not confuse the CAC (registers companies) with FIRS (collects federal taxes), the CBN (central bank) or the NPA (ports).

### Lesson 7.5: Public enterprises

**Must know**

- **Public corporation (public enterprise):** a business organisation **established by an Act of the legislature, owned and financed by government, and run by a board appointed by government** to provide essential services.
- **Main source of capital:** **government funding through budget allocations, grants and loans** (plus revenue from its services).

**Features:** created by statute; government ownership and control; separate legal entity; board appointed by government (minister); service motive rather than profit motive; accountable to the public through the legislature; profits go back to government, losses usually covered by subsidy.

**Reasons governments set them up (advantages):**

1. **To provide essential services that private firms may not provide adequately or at affordable prices** (water, power, rail).
2. To control strategic industries (oil, defence, ports).
3. To prevent exploitation by private monopolies.
4. To undertake projects too large or risky for private investors.
5. To create employment and promote balanced regional development.

**Problems (disadvantages) in Nigeria:**

1. **Political interference** in management and appointments.
2. **Inefficiency and dependence on government subsidies.**
3. **Bureaucracy and slow decision-making.**
4. Corruption, overstaffing and poor service.
5. Lack of profit motive and accountability.

- **Privatisation:** **transfers ownership** (fully or partly) of a public enterprise **to private investors**.
- **Commercialisation:** **keeps government ownership** but requires the enterprise to **run on profit-oriented (commercial) lines**, charging economic prices and funding itself.

**Explain more**

- Examples: Nigerian Railway Corporation (NRC), Nigerian Ports Authority (NPA), Nigerian Postal Service (NIPOST). The power-generation and distribution companies of the former PHCN were privatised in 2013. NNPC was converted into NNPC Limited in 2022 under the Petroleum Industry Act 2021 and run on commercial lines.
- Privatisation and commercialisation in Nigeria are handled by the **Bureau of Public Enterprises (BPE)** under the Public Enterprises (Privatisation and Commercialisation) Act 1999.
- Trap: "excessive pressure from private shareholders to pay dividends" is **NOT** a problem of public enterprises: they have no private shareholders. "To maximise dividends to private shareholders" is not a reason for setting them up.
- Trap: exams reverse the two terms. **Privatisation = ownership changes; commercialisation = ownership stays with government, behaviour becomes commercial.**

**Summary comparison:**

| Form | Owners | Liability | Legal personality | Continuity | Main capital source |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Sole proprietorship | 1 | unlimited | none | ends with owner | personal savings, family and friends |
| Ordinary partnership | 2 to 20 | unlimited | none | dissolves on death of a partner | partners' contributions |
| Private company (Ltd) | 1 to 50 | limited | separate | perpetual succession | private shares, retained earnings, loans |
| Public company (Plc) | 2 or more | limited | separate | perpetual succession | public share issues, debentures |
| Public enterprise | government | not applicable (state-owned) | separate (statutory) | as long as the Act stands | government budget, grants, loans |

### One-minute recap

- A firm combines factors of production to produce for sale; Coase: firms exist to save transaction costs.
- Traditional theory: owner-entrepreneur, single goal, full knowledge, profit maximised at MR = MC.
- Managerial theories come from separation of ownership and control: Baumol (sales revenue with minimum profit), Williamson (managerial utility: staff, perks), Marris (growth, safe from takeover).
- Behavioural theory (Cyert and March, Simon): coalition of groups; satisficing.
- Sole proprietor: one owner, all profit, all risk, unlimited liability, quick decisions, limited capital, no continuity.
- Partnership: 2 to 20 partners, deed of partnership; sleeping, nominal, limited partners; unlimited liability and mutual agency.
- Ltd and Plc both give limited liability, separate legal entity and perpetual succession; only a Plc can sell shares to the public.
- CAC registers companies under CAMA 2020; memorandum = external, articles = internal.
- Ordinary shares: owners, variable dividend; preference: fixed dividend, paid first; debentures: creditors, fixed interest; retained earnings = internal finance.
- Public enterprise: statute, government-owned and financed, essential services; privatisation transfers ownership, commercialisation keeps it.

### Theory practice

**Question:** State three differences between a private limited company and a public limited company, and one feature they share.

*Solution:* (1) A Plc can invite the general public to buy its shares; an Ltd cannot. (2) An Ltd restricts the transfer of its shares; a Plc's shares are freely transferable and may be listed on the Nigerian Exchange. (3) Under CAMA 2020 an Ltd has 1 to 50 members, while a Plc needs at least 2 and has no maximum; a Plc must also publish more financial information. Shared feature: both give shareholders limited liability (and both are separate legal entities with perpetual succession, registered by the CAC).
