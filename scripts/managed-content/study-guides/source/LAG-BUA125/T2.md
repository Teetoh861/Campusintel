## Topic 2: Opportunity Cost in Business

**What you must know:** the meaning of opportunity cost and why it exists; how to calculate it (the RMPIC − RICP formula and the next-best-alternative rule); explicit, implicit, sunk, marginal and incremental costs; accounting vs economic profit; the production possibility frontier; opportunity cost in operations and capital structure; implications, limitations and the four decision factors.

### Lesson 2.1: Meaning of opportunity cost

**Must know**

- **Opportunity cost:** the potential benefit that a business, investor or consumer misses out on when choosing one alternative over another. Put simply, the forgone benefit from the option not chosen.
- **Economist's measure:** the value of the **next best alternative** given up (not the total of every alternative, not the price of the item chosen).
- **Second definition:** the cost of time, effort and/or energy used to make Choice A that can no longer be used to make Choice B.
- It can include **money, time and effort**. A choice can have an opportunity cost even when no money is paid.
- **"There is no such thing as a free lunch":** every choice has a cost because time and energy are given up even if no money changes hands.
- **Why it exists:** resources are **scarce** and have **alternative uses**. Scarcity is the central problem of economics.
- In business it is the **trade-off** you accept in exchange for the choice you make. It cannot be predicted with total certainty, but considering it leads to better decisions.

**Explain more**

- Tolu, a sales executive, can attend a product launch or a client meeting, both at 2 pm on Friday. She goes to the launch. Her opportunity cost is the **client meeting**, not "nothing" because the launch was free.
- A bakery spends its ₦3 million budget on a new oven; the next best use was a marketing campaign. Opportunity cost = **the marketing campaign**, not the bread the oven makes or last year's repairs.
- An office manager buys a sofa; the same money could have bought a water cooler and new wallpaper. Opportunity cost = **the water cooler and the wallpaper**.
- More examples: a firm paying ₦1 billion interest on debt gives up upscaling, marketing or product development; a phone maker gives up making computers or keyboards; a holiday gives up a day's work; an employee who quits for a year of study gives up a year's wages.
- Business choices: building a plant in Lagos instead of Accra, upgrading equipment instead of hiring workers, buying shares in MTN instead of GTBank.

### Lesson 2.2: Calculating opportunity cost

**Must know**

- **Formula:** Opportunity cost = RMPIC − RICP
  - RMPIC = return on the most profitable investment choice.
  - RICP = return on the investment chosen to pursue.
  - It is simply the difference between the expected returns of the options. Works in ₦ or in %.
- **Percentage return** = return ÷ amount invested × 100.
- **Next-best rule:** when the question asks for the value of the next best alternative forgone (or when the best option was chosen), the opportunity cost is the return of the **best option not chosen**.
- **Compounding:** return in year n = principal × r × (1 + r)^(n − 1).
- **Production trade-off:** opportunity cost of 1 unit of X = units of Y given up ÷ units of X gained.

**Explain more**

Read the question for the method it names.

Worked example 1 (formula in ₦). Spare ₦5,000,000: treasury bills return ₦600,000; new equipment adds ₦450,000. Firm picks equipment.
Opportunity cost = 600,000 − 450,000 = **₦150,000**.
Likewise: Market A ₦50m, Market B ₦35m, firm picks B: 50 − 35 = **₦15m** (not zero, not ₦85m). Port Harcourt firm: treasury bills ₦1,200,000 vs workshop ₦900,000, picks workshop: **₦300,000**.

Worked example 2 (formula in %). Stock market 10% vs equipment 8%, firm picks equipment: 10% − 8% = **2%**. Treasury bills 14% vs upgrade 9%: **5%**. Three options P 12%, Q 15%, R 9%, firm picks R: RMPIC is the **highest** (15%), so 15% − 9% = **6%** (not 12% − 9%).

Worked example 3 (convert to %). ₦4,000,000 in X returns ₦520,000; in Y returns ₦360,000; firm picks Y.
- X: 520,000 ÷ 4,000,000 × 100 = 13%. Y: 360,000 ÷ 4,000,000 × 100 = 9%.
- Opportunity cost = 13% − 9% = **4%**.

Worked example 4 (next best forgone). An Oyo farmer's plot: maize ₦450,000, cassava ₦380,000, yam ₦520,000. He plants yam (the best). Opportunity cost = next best = **maize, ₦450,000**. Similarly, three projects each costing ₦3,000,000 return A ₦3,540,000, B ₦3,480,000, C ₦3,600,000. Net gains: A ₦540,000, B ₦480,000, C ₦600,000. Choosing C, opportunity cost = next best net gain = **₦540,000**.

Worked example 5 (multi-year). ₦20 million: securities at 10% compounded give ₦2.0m, ₦2.2m, ₦2.42m in years 1-3 (total ₦6.62m). A machine adds ₦0.5m, ₦2.0m, ₦5.0m (total ₦7.5m). Securities win in years 1 and 2, but over three years the machine is better by 7.5 − 6.62 = **₦0.88m**.

Worked example 6 (compounding and cumulative). ₦5,000,000 in securities at 12% compounded:
- Year 1: 5,000,000 × 0.12 = ₦600,000.
- Year 2: 600,000 × 1.12 = ₦672,000.
- Year 3: 672,000 × 1.12 = **₦752,640** (= 5,000,000 × 0.12 × 1.12^2). Total ₦2,024,640.
- A machine adds ₦300,000, ₦800,000, ₦1,200,000 (total ₦2,300,000). Machine better by 2,300,000 − 2,024,640 = **₦275,360**.
- Cumulative: year 1 ₦300,000 vs ₦600,000; year 2 ₦1,100,000 vs ₦1,272,000; year 3 ₦2,300,000 vs ₦2,024,640. The machine first leads in **year 3**.

Worked example 7 (other cases).
- Interest of ₦45,000,000 could instead fund marketing returning 18%: return given up = 45,000,000 × 0.18 = **₦8,100,000**.
- A workshop makes 200 chairs or 50 tables: 1 table costs 200 ÷ 50 = **4 chairs** (1 chair costs 0.25 tables).
- A consultant earns ₦25,000 an hour. Queuing 3 hours costs her 3 × 25,000 = ₦75,000 in lost work; an agent charges ₦5,000. Paying the agent leaves her 75,000 − 5,000 = **₦70,000** better off.

### Lesson 2.3: Opportunity cost and other costs; accounting vs economic profit

**Must know**

- **Explicit cost:** out-of-pocket payment (rent, salaries, raw materials, electricity bills) paid with the firm's assets and recorded in its financial statements.
- **Implicit cost:** no cash exchange; cannot be measured accurately for accounting; it **is** an opportunity cost, also called an **imputed cost**. Examples: rent the owner could earn on her own building, interest forgone on her own savings, salary she gave up.
- **Sunk cost:** money already spent in the past that cannot be recovered whatever you decide (machinery, stock, a feasibility study). It is **irrelevant** to future decisions.
- **Sunk vs opportunity cost:** sunk cost is past spending; opportunity cost is the future return not earned because money was used elsewhere.
- **Marginal cost:** cost of producing **one extra unit**. MC = change in TC.
- **Incremental cost:** cost of raising production by **more than one unit** at a time.

| Profit | Formula |
|---|---|
| Accounting profit | Revenue − explicit costs |
| Economic profit | Revenue − explicit costs − implicit costs |

- Economic profit = 0 means **normal profit** (all resources earn what they could earn elsewhere). Positive = above-normal; negative = economic loss.

**Explain more**

- A Yaba printing shop's explicit cost is the rent paid to the landlord; rent she could collect by leasing her own building is implicit.
- A juice company in Ogun spent ₦2.5m last year on a feasibility study: **sunk**. When replacing an old machine, its **book value** is sunk and irrelevant; only future costs and benefits count.
- Marginal: TC ₦500,000 at 100 crates, ₦503,200 at 101: MC = **₦3,200**. Incremental: 100 to 150 crates, TC ₦500,000 to ₦640,000: incremental cost = **₦140,000** (₦2,800 per extra crate on average).
- Implicit rent: shop rentable at ₦150,000 a month: 150,000 × 12 = **₦1,800,000** a year. Savings of ₦8,000,000 at 11%: 8,000,000 × 0.11 = **₦880,000** a year.

Worked example (Lekki boutique). Revenue ₦12,000,000; explicit costs ₦7,500,000; forgone salary ₦2,400,000; own savings ₦5,000,000 that could earn 10%.
- Accounting profit = 12,000,000 − 7,500,000 = ₦4,500,000.
- Implicit costs = 2,400,000 + (5,000,000 × 0.10 = 500,000) = **₦2,900,000**.
- Economic profit = 4,500,000 − 2,900,000 = **₦1,600,000**.

Worked example (Emeka). Revenue ₦6,000,000; explicit ₦4,200,000; forgone job ₦2,100,000.
- Accounting profit = **₦1,800,000**; economic profit = 1,800,000 − 2,100,000 = **−₦300,000**.
- Meaning: he would earn ₦300,000 more a year by taking the paid job. It is not a cash loss in his accounts.

- Revenue ₦9m, explicit ₦6m, implicit ₦3m: economic profit = **zero**, a normal profit.
- Bisi leaves a ₦1,800,000 job to study; tuition and books ₦600,000; living costs ₦900,000 are the same either way, so they are excluded. Economic cost = 600,000 + 1,800,000 = **₦2,400,000**.

### Lesson 2.4: Opportunity cost in operations and the PPF

**Must know**

**Operations where opportunity cost guides decisions:**

1. **Production:** making one product means not making another (phones vs computers).
2. **Investment:** securities vs new machinery, stock A vs stock B, new plant here vs there.
3. **Pricing:** inputs should be valued at what they could earn elsewhere (current value), not their historical cost.
4. **Resource allocation:** put scarce funds, staff and machine time where they earn the most.
5. **Make-or-buy:** compare the cost of buying with the full cost of making, including what the capacity could earn on other work.
6. **Capital structure:** the combination of debt and equity used to fund operations and growth; expressed as a debt-to-equity or debt-to-capital ratio. It affects value, risk and return.

**Production possibility frontier (PPF/PPC):** shows the maximum combinations of two goods an economy or firm can produce with given resources and technology.

| Point | Meaning |
|---|---|
| On the curve | Full, efficient use of resources |
| Inside the curve | Less than full employment (idle or wasted resources) |
| Outside the curve | Unattainable with current resources |

- Moving along the curve, more of one good means less of the other: the slope shows opportunity cost. A curve bowed outward shows **increasing** opportunity cost. An outward shift means growth (more resources or better technology).

**Explain more**

- **Debt and equity:** each carries an explicit cost (interest to lenders, dividends to shareholders) and an opportunity cost. For debt, money used to repay debt cannot be invested elsewhere. It asks whether the expansion debt makes possible earns more than outside investments. Firms weigh borrowing against issuing shares to find the balance that minimises opportunity costs. This is hard in practice because opportunity cost is **forward-looking**: the actual rate of return on each option is unknown when the choice is made.
- **Pricing example:** a Balogun market trader bought fabric at ₦4,000 a yard; its replacement price is now ₦5,500. Pricing on ₦4,000 ignores the ₦5,500 she could get by reselling it.
- **Make-or-buy example:** making a part costs ₦800 per unit; buying costs ₦950. If the same machine time could make another product earning ₦250 per unit, the true cost of making = 800 + 250 = ₦1,050 > ₦950, so **buy**.

### Lesson 2.5: Implications, limitations and decision factors

**Must know**

**Implications for business operations:**

1. **Better decision-making:** more informed choices that maximise potential profits and minimise missed opportunities.
2. **Resource allocation:** resources are used efficiently, in the most profitable way possible.

**4 limitations of opportunity cost:**

1. **Subjective:** calculations rest on assumptions, not fact, so they are open to error or bias.
2. **Difficult to quantify:** costs are not always financial; they are shaped by preferences, projections, strategy and ethos.
3. **Hard to record:** projected outcomes mix financial and non-financial measures.
4. **Cannot cover all factors:** changes in the economy, public policy and consumer confidence also drive outcomes.

**4 other decision factors (besides opportunity cost):**

1. **Cost:** the expense of deciding and its consequences.
2. **Time:** time is money; judge whether saving time or money matters more.
3. **Utility:** value optimisation, the crux of decision-making.
4. **Effort:** minimise the effort the chosen option requires.

- They are interdependent. **Guiding principle:** maximise utility and value created while minimising cost incurred, time spent and effort required.

**Explain more**

- A manager whose project returns rest on her own guesses about demand is warned the result may be biased: that is **subjectivity**.
- Opportunity cost never produces a precise, certain figure, is not recorded exactly in the financial statements, and is used by all organisations, not only non-profits.
- "Popularity" is **not** one of the four factors.
- Marginal thinking ties in: a student studies one more hour when its marginal benefit exceeds its **marginal** cost (the next best use of that hour).

### One-minute recap

- Opportunity cost = benefit of the next best alternative given up; it exists because resources are scarce with alternative uses.
- It covers money, time and effort; no free lunch.
- Formula: RMPIC − RICP. If the best option was chosen, or the question asks for the next best forgone, use the next best option's return.
- Explicit = cash and recorded; implicit = imputed, no cash; sunk = past and irrelevant; marginal = one more unit; incremental = more than one unit.
- Accounting profit = revenue − explicit costs; economic profit also subtracts implicit costs. Zero economic profit = normal profit.
- PPF: on = efficient, inside = unemployment, outside = unattainable.
- Capital structure = debt and equity mix; debt carries an opportunity cost.
- 4 limitations: subjective, hard to quantify, hard to record, cannot cover all factors. 4 factors: cost, time, utility, effort.

### Theory practice

**Question:** Funke earns revenue of ₦10,000,000 from her catering firm. Explicit costs are ₦6,500,000. She gave up a salary of ₦1,500,000 and put in ₦4,000,000 of her own savings that could earn 12% a year. Find her accounting profit and economic profit.

*Solution:*
- Accounting profit = 10,000,000 − 6,500,000 = **₦3,500,000**.
- Implicit costs = 1,500,000 + (4,000,000 × 0.12 = 480,000) = ₦1,980,000.
- Economic profit = 3,500,000 − 1,980,000 = **₦1,520,000**, an above-normal profit: catering beats her best alternative.
