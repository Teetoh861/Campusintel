## Topic 6: Market Segmentation

**What you must know:** why firms analyse customer needs and segment; the meaning of market segmentation and a segment; the 4 bases for consumer markets and their variables; the 5 requirements for effective segments; targeting (evaluating segments and the targeting strategies); positioning; the steps (segmentation, targeting, positioning); benefits, costs and managerial implications, including segment pricing; and segment-size computations.

### Lesson 6.1: Need analysis and the meaning of segmentation

**Must know**

- **Need analysis:** studying what different customers need and want, how they buy and why, so the firm can see that one market is really many groups with different needs.
- **Market segmentation:** dividing a total market into distinct groups of buyers who have different needs, characteristics or behaviours, and who might require separate products or marketing programmes.
- **Market segment:** a group of consumers who respond in a similar way to a given set of marketing efforts.
- **Mass (undifferentiated) marketing:** treating the whole market as one and offering one product to all; the opposite of segmentation.
- **Origin:** the concept of market segmentation was introduced by **Wendell Smith** (1956).

**Why firms segment (need for segmentation):**

1. Buyers differ in needs, incomes, tastes, locations and buying habits; one offer rarely satisfies all.
2. Resources are limited, so a firm must choose where to compete.
3. It lets the firm match products and the marketing mix more closely to customers' needs.

**Explain more**

- Think of the Nigerian noodle market. Some buyers want the cheapest pack, some want bigger packs for the family, some want new flavours. Indomie offers different pack sizes and flavours because it has analysed these needs.
- Segmentation is not the same as **positioning** (creating a place in consumers' minds) or **market penetration** (selling more of an existing product in an existing market). Exams swap these terms.
- A segment is a **group** responding similarly; it is not one large customer, nor the number of firms in an industry, nor the market share of the largest firm.

### Lesson 6.2: Bases for segmenting consumer markets

**Must know**

The **4 major bases**:

| Basis | Divides buyers by | Typical variables |
|---|---|---|
| Geographic | Where they are | Nations, regions, states, cities, neighbourhoods, city size, population density, climate |
| Demographic | Who they are (measurable population traits) | Age and life-cycle stage, gender, income, family size, occupation, education, religion, ethnicity, generation |
| Psychographic | How they live and think | Lifestyle, personality, values (and social class) |
| Behavioural | How they act towards the product | Occasions, benefits sought, user status, usage rate, loyalty status |

**Behavioural variables (5):**

1. **Occasions:** when buyers get the idea to buy, buy or use (festive gift packs).
2. **Benefits sought:** the benefit the buyer wants from the product (benefit segmentation).
3. **User status:** non-users, ex-users, potential users, first-time users, regular users.
4. **Usage rate:** light, medium and heavy users.
5. **Loyalty status:** how loyal buyers are to a brand.

- **Behavioural segmentation:** dividing buyers by their knowledge, attitudes, uses or responses to a product.
- **Psychographic segmentation:** dividing buyers by lifestyle, personality characteristics or values.
- Production capacity of the selling firm is **not** a basis for segmentation; bases describe **buyers**, not the seller.

**Explain more**

- **Geographic:** a paint company sells different ranges in the rainy south and the dry, hot north of Nigeria because of climate.
- **Demographic:** the most popular basis because the variables are easy to measure and link closely to needs. A bank's savings account for customers aged 18 to 25 in tertiary institutions uses age and life stage. Income and population traits are demographic variables.
- **Psychographic:** a Lekki gym targeting young professionals who treat fitness, healthy eating and an active social life as central to how they live is using lifestyle. Some books place social class under demographic; most introductory marketing lists put it with lifestyle and personality.
- **Behavioural examples:**
    - **Usage rate:** a telecom operator such as MTN offering a special bundle to customers who use more than 20GB a month and a different plan to light users.
    - **Occasion:** a biscuit company making gift packs sold only at Christmas and Sallah. The trigger is the festive occasion, so this is behavioural, not demographic by religion.
    - **Benefits sought:** a toothpaste maker with one brand for whiter teeth, one for fresh breath and one for sensitive teeth.
    - **Loyalty status:** an airline rewarding passengers who fly with it repeatedly and never switch.
- **Trap:** family size is **demographic**, not behavioural. Region, city size and climate are geographic; lifestyle, personality and values are psychographic; usage rate, loyalty and benefits are behavioural.
- Firms often combine bases (multiple segmentation), for example young (demographic), heavy-data (behavioural) users in Lagos (geographic).
- Business markets can be segmented by many of the same variables plus customer size, industry, purchasing approach and situational factors.

### Lesson 6.3: Requirements for effective segmentation

**Must know**

A useful segment must be (5):

1. **Measurable:** its size, purchasing power and profile can be estimated.
2. **Accessible:** the firm can effectively reach and serve it.
3. **Substantial:** large or profitable enough to be worth a separate marketing programme.
4. **Differentiable:** conceptually distinct, and responds differently to marketing mix elements than other segments.
5. **Actionable:** the firm can design effective programmes to attract and serve it.

- A segment does **not** have to contain every consumer in the market; that would be mass marketing.

**Explain more**

- Match the definition to the word. Exams give the definition and ask for the term:
    - "Can be reached and served" = **accessible**, not measurable.
    - "Size, purchasing power and profile can be estimated" = **measurable**.
    - "Large enough to be profitable" = **substantial**.
    - "Responds differently from other segments" = **differentiable**.
    - "Firm can design programmes to attract and serve it" = **actionable**.
- A group with clear needs but only a few dozen buyers, too small to cover a separate programme, fails **substantial**.
- Left-handed people may be a distinct group, but they are hard to identify and reach, so they may fail **measurable** and **accessible**.
- A small firm with resources for only one programme may find two attractive segments but can act on only one: the second fails **actionable** for that firm.

### Lesson 6.4: Targeting and positioning

**Must know**

**Steps in target marketing (STP), in order:**

1. **Segmentation:** divide the market into groups.
2. **Targeting (market targeting):** evaluate the attractiveness of each segment and select one or more to enter.
3. **Positioning:** arrange for the product to occupy a clear, distinctive and desirable place relative to competing products in the minds of target consumers.

**Evaluating segments (3 factors):** (1) segment **size and growth**; (2) segment **structural attractiveness** (competitors, substitutes, power of buyers and suppliers); (3) company **objectives and resources**.

**Targeting strategies:**

| Strategy | What the firm does | Main advantage | Main drawback |
|---|---|---|---|
| Undifferentiated (mass) | Ignores segment differences; one offer for the whole market | Lowest cost | Hard to satisfy everyone; open to focused rivals |
| Differentiated (segmented) | Targets several segments with a separate offer for each | Higher total sales and stronger position in each segment | Raises costs of product development, production and promotion |
| Concentrated (niche) | Goes after a large share of one small segment or niche; suits limited resources | Strong knowledge of customers and a strong niche position | Heavy dependence on one segment; a change there can hurt badly |
| Micromarketing | Tailors offers to local areas or individuals | Very close fit to needs | High cost; lower economies of scale |

**Positioning:**

- **Positioning statement:** to (target segment and need), our (brand) is (concept) that (point of difference).
- Firms differentiate through product, services, channels, people or image, and choose a **value proposition** (the full mix of benefits on which the brand is positioned).

**Explain more**

- A beverage company with a cheap sachet drink for low-income buyers, a premium bottled range for upper-income buyers and a sugar-free version for health-conscious buyers, each with its own advertising, uses **differentiated** marketing.
- A small Lagos firm that serves only premium natural-hair products for one niche uses **concentrated** marketing.
- A fast-food chain advertising itself as "the fastest affordable meal for busy office workers in Lagos" is **positioning**: it names a target (busy office workers) and a point of difference (fast, affordable).
- **Trap:** targeting is choosing segments; positioning is the place in the mind. The order is never positioning first.
- Differentiated marketing does not "ignore differences" or "serve one niche"; it can raise total sales, but at higher cost.

### Lesson 6.5: Benefits, costs and implications for business

**Must know**

**Benefits of segmentation:**

1. Better match of products and marketing mix to customers' needs.
2. More effective competition in chosen segments.
3. Spotting new market opportunities and unserved groups.
4. Efficient use of resources: spending is concentrated on the segments with the best returns.
5. Scope for different prices for groups with different price sensitivity.

**Costs and limits:** more products, research and promotion raise costs; segments can be too small; segmentation does **not** always lower total marketing costs, guarantee no competitors, or remove the need for research.

**Segment pricing rule:** if a firm can separate groups (and stop resale), it charges the **higher price** to the group whose demand is **less price elastic**, and the lower price to the more elastic group.

**Segment-size formulas:**

- Number of buyers in segment = total population x proportion with the trait x proportion who want the product.
- Segment value (per period) = number of buyers x spending per buyer per period.

**Explain more**

- **Managerial economics view:** segmentation is resource allocation. With a limited budget, a manager earns more by spending where the return per naira is highest than by spreading the budget equally over everyone.
- **Segment pricing example:** a cinema charges students less than working adults. Students have lower incomes and more alternatives, so their demand is more elastic; adults' demand is less elastic, so they pay more. Firms must be able to tell the groups apart (student ID) and prevent resale. Airlines, data plans by time of day and senior discounts follow the same logic.

**Worked computation 1:** a campus food delivery start-up estimates 48,000 students; 35% order food online; each spends ₦2,500 a month.

- Buyers = 48,000 x 0.35 = 16,800 students.
- Monthly value = 16,800 x ₦2,500 = **₦42,000,000**.
- Trap: 48,000 x ₦2,500 = ₦120,000,000 ignores the 35%.

**Worked computation 2:** a town has 120,000 households; 40% earn above ₦300,000 a month; 25% of those want a home-cleaning service.

- High-income households = 120,000 x 0.40 = 48,000.
- Target segment = 48,000 x 0.25 = **12,000 households**.
- Trap: 48,000 is the income group only; 30,000 wrongly applies 25% to all households.

- **Implications for managers:** choose bases that link to buying behaviour; check all 5 requirements before committing money; pick a targeting strategy that fits resources (concentrated for small firms, differentiated for large ones such as Nestlé Nigeria or Dangote); position clearly; review segments as incomes, tastes and technology change.

### One-minute recap

- Segmentation = dividing a market into distinct groups with different needs; a segment responds similarly to a set of marketing efforts. Wendell Smith introduced the concept (1956).
- 4 bases: geographic, demographic, psychographic, behavioural.
- Demographic: age, gender, income, family size; psychographic: lifestyle, personality, values.
- Behavioural: occasions, benefits sought, user status, usage rate, loyalty status.
- 5 requirements: measurable, accessible, substantial, differentiable, actionable.
- STP order: segmentation, targeting, positioning.
- Undifferentiated (one offer), differentiated (many offers, higher cost), concentrated (one niche, high dependence), micromarketing.
- Positioning = a clear, distinctive, desirable place in target consumers' minds.
- Charge the higher price to the less price-elastic segment.
- Segment value = population x proportions x spending per buyer.

### Theory practice

**Question:** A snack firm with a small budget compares two campus segments. Segment A: 30,000 students, 20% interested, each spending ₦4,000 a month. Segment B: 8,000 staff, 50% interested, each spending ₦5,000 a month. Find each segment's monthly value and name the targeting strategy if the firm serves only the better one.

*Solution:* A buyers = 30,000 x 0.20 = 6,000; value = 6,000 x ₦4,000 = **₦24,000,000**. B buyers = 8,000 x 0.50 = 4,000; value = 4,000 x ₦5,000 = **₦20,000,000**. Segment A is worth more, so a firm with limited resources serving only A is using **concentrated (niche) marketing**.
