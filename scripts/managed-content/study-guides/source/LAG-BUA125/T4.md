## Topic 4: Business Forecasting

**What you must know:** the meaning and importance of demand forecasting; the factors that affect it; its types (short, medium and long term; passive and active; internal; macro and micro; qualitative and quantitative; demand, sales, economic and technological); the techniques (surveys, statistical methods, econometric models, sales force composite, Delphi, barometric indicators, expert opinion, simulation); the steps; the challenges; and the forecast computations (moving averages, exponential smoothing, trend projection, growth, forecast error and MAD).

### Lesson 4.1: Meaning, significance and factors

**Must know**

- **Demand forecasting:** a technique for anticipating future demand for a product, which tells a business the estimated quantity of a product or service that consumers may want to buy over a period of time.
- **Business forecasting** (wider): predicting future business conditions (demand, sales, costs, prices, the economy) so that managers can plan.
- **Strength of a forecast** depends on 3 things: (1) the **quantity and quality of the data**, (2) the **methods** used to calculate it, (3) the **user's level of competence**.
- **Accuracy** also depends on the validity of the planning data and how far outside circumstances support the plan's assumptions.
- **Forecast errors:** **overestimation** of demand leads to **excess inventory**; **underestimation** leads to **short supply** (stock-outs and lost sales).

**Significance (why forecast):**

1. Foresee customers' wants and plan accordingly.
2. Plan production and inventory.
3. Set prices.
4. Adjust marketing and sales efforts.
5. Meet client demand and manage short- and long-term objectives proactively.
6. Minimise costs of over- or under-stocking.
7. Reduce lost sales due to stock-outs and fulfil orders on time.

**Importance (4 headline benefits):** (1) helps prepare your **budget**; (2) helps determine a **pricing plan**; (3) **reduces backorders**; (4) **saves on restocking** (for example, buying in bulk).

**Factors affecting forecasting (6):**

1. **Economic conditions:** GDP, unemployment rate, inflation rate, consumer confidence.
2. **Competition:** more competition can mean less demand for one firm's product.
3. **Consumer trends:** a shift towards some products affects demand for others.
4. **Price:** demand may fall if price rises.
5. **Availability:** demand may rise if a product is unavailable or in short supply (buyers rush to secure it).
6. **Advertising:** demand may rise with effective advertising.

**Explain more**

- Forecasting **reduces** uncertainty; it never **removes all** uncertainty about future sales. An option claiming it does is wrong.
- Things that do **not** affect forecast strength or demand: the colour scheme of the report, the number of directors on the board. Exams use such distractors.
- Example: a Lagos cosmetics firm overestimates demand for a new body cream and produces far more than customers buy. Direct result: **excess inventory**, tied-up cash and storage cost. If it had underestimated, shops would run out and customers would switch to rival brands: **lost sales**.
- Past demand is a useful starting point, but planning the future needs more inputs than past figures alone.

### Lesson 4.2: Types of forecasting

**Must know**

| Type | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Short-term | Predicts demand for a specific near period (weeks or months, usually up to about a year); methods: statistical analysis, economic models, market research, customer surveys |
| Medium-term | Covers roughly one to a few years; used for budgets, capacity and sales planning |
| Long-term | An analytical procedure that predicts demand over a protracted period, to plan and allocate resources and identify opportunities and risks; uses both qualitative and quantitative methods |
| Passive | Uses **only** previous sales data to project future demand; suits stable firms |
| Active | Uses past demand **plus** current market trends and other data; common in retail and manufacturing |
| Internal | A firm forecasts demand for its own goods using past sales, industry trends, economic conditions and customer feedback, to plan production and inventory |
| Macro | Demand in the **overall economy**, using population, GDP, income and spending |
| Micro | Demand for a **specific product in a given market** or small area, using customer behaviour, seasonality, competition, pricing, demographics, local conditions |

- **Qualitative** forecasting: based on judgement and opinion (expert opinion, Delphi, sales force composite, surveys of buyers' intentions).
- **Quantitative** forecasting: based on numbers and statistical models (time series, moving averages, exponential smoothing, trend projection, regression, econometric models).
- **By subject:** **demand** forecasting (what customers will want); **sales** forecasting (what the firm itself expects to sell); **economic** forecasting (future GDP, inflation, interest rates, employment); **technological** forecasting (the direction and speed of technical change).

**Explain more**

- Time cut-offs vary between books; what matters is the idea: short term = near future, operational plans (staffing, stock); long term = years, strategic plans (new plants, new markets).
- A supermarket in Enugu forecasting the next six weeks for festive staffing and stock is doing **short-term** forecasting.
- Passive vs active trap: **passive = past sales only**; **active = past demand plus current trends and other data**. Neither is defined by time span or by expert opinion.
- Macro vs micro trap: macro is the **whole economy**; micro is **one product in one market**. A soft drink maker forecasting demand in Surulere alone, using local behaviour, seasonality, competition and pricing, is doing **micro** forecasting.
- Dangote Cement uses long-term forecasts before building a new plant; a Shoprite outlet uses short-term forecasts for Christmas stock. The Central Bank of Nigeria and the National Bureau of Statistics publish economic forecasts and data that firms feed into macro forecasts.

### Lesson 4.3: Forecasting techniques

**Must know**

**1. Market research / surveying** (customer surveys; includes the **survey of buyers' intentions**, asking customers what they plan to buy). Three types:

1. **Sample survey:** studies the buying behaviour of a small sample of potential customers.
2. **Complete enumeration survey:** speaks with as many prospective customers as is practical, to collect a larger amount of data.
3. **End-user research:** asks other businesses (your business customers) what their own customers want.

**2. Statistical approach** (reliable, often cost-effective):

1. **Trend projection:** the easiest method; study the past to predict the future.
2. **Regression analysis:** identifies relationships between demand and external factors (price, time of year, advertising).
3. **Time series analysis:** uses historical data; assumes current demand trends will hold true going forward.
4. **Market segmentation:** identifies which segments are most likely to buy.
5. **Decision trees:** analyse how different variables can affect demand.
6. **Simulation modelling:** builds a model of the real system and runs it under different assumptions to see the likely range of outcomes.

**3. Econometric models:** mathematical models that estimate relationships among economic variables such as prices, demand, supply and consumption; also used to examine the effects of economic policies.

**4. Sales force composite ("collective view"):** salespeople estimate demand in their own territories; estimates are combined at branch, region or area level into a company forecast. A **bottom-up** approach.

**5. Delphi method:** a **qualitative** technique; experts are questioned in a **series of rounds** and their opinions are consolidated into a prediction. Developed by the **RAND Corporation**; often used with expert opinion.

**6. Barometric forecasting** uses **3 indicators**:

1. **Leading indicators:** predict future events (a surge in complaints about delivery delays warns that sales may fall).
2. **Lagging indicators:** look back at past outcomes (last month's rise in sales).
3. **Coincident (coincidental) indicators:** gauge what is happening now (real-time inventory turnover).

**7. Expert opinion:** qualitative forecasting that relies on the judgement of experts.

**Explain more**

- **Why econometrics is hard:** forecasters can rarely run controlled trials where only one variable changes, so they must estimate effects from messy real-world data.
- **Sales force composite:** advantage, salespeople are close to the market and often have first-hand knowledge of customers. Caution, price, campaigns, customer affluence and competition differ by territory, and salespeople may be biased (it is not "free from bias"). A beverage firm's reps estimate Lagos 12,500 cartons, Ibadan 8,300, Abeokuta 4,700, Ilorin 3,500: forecast = 12,500 + 8,300 + 4,700 + 3,500 = **29,000 cartons** (add, do not average).
- **Delphi** experts usually answer separately (often anonymously), receive a summary, and revise in the next round until views converge. This avoids one loud expert dominating.
- **End-user research example:** a furniture maker that sells to hotels asks hotel managers what their guests want.
- **Regression example:** a paint company measuring how its sales relate to price, season and advertising.
- **Qualitative vs quantitative trap:** expert opinion, Delphi and sales force composite are qualitative; moving average, exponential smoothing, trend projection, regression and econometric models are quantitative.

### Lesson 4.4: Forecast computations

**Must know**

- **Simple moving average (n periods):** Forecast = (sum of the last n actual values) / n.
- **Weighted moving average:** Forecast = sum of (weight x actual) / sum of weights. If weights add to 1, just add the products.
- **Exponential smoothing:** F(t+1) = F(t) + alpha x (A(t) - F(t)), same as alpha x A(t) + (1 - alpha) x F(t), with 0 < alpha < 1. Higher alpha = faster response to recent demand. alpha = 1 gives F(t+1) = A(t).
- **Constant-change trend:** average change per year = (last value - first value) / number of intervals; forecast = last value + change x years ahead.
- **Trend equation:** Y = a + bX; put in the X for the target year.
- **Least squares with coded years** (sum of X = 0): a = (sum of Y) / n; b = (sum of XY) / (sum of X^2).
- **Compound growth:** Forecast = current value x (1 + g)^n.
- **Forecast error** = actual - forecast. **MAD** (mean absolute deviation) = sum of |actual - forecast| / number of periods.
- **Monitoring:** compare actual outcomes with forecasts; if inaccurate, adjust the method.

**Explain more**

**A. Simple moving average.** A Surulere bakery sold 420, 450, 480, 510, 470, 500 loaves (January to June). 3-month forecast for July = (510 + 470 + 500) / 3 = 1,480 / 3 = 493.33, about **493** loaves. Use only the **latest** 3 months.
A Port Harcourt hotel's quarterly revenue (₦ million): 1.2, 1.5, 1.8, 1.4, 1.7. 4-quarter forecast for quarter 6 = (1.5 + 1.8 + 1.4 + 1.7) / 4 = 6.4 / 4 = **₦1.600 million**.
Forecast error: sales 30, 34, 38, then actual 40 in period 4. Forecast = (30 + 34 + 38) / 3 = 34; error = 40 - 34 = **6**.

**B. Weighted moving average.** A phone accessories shop sold 200 (March), 220 (April), 250 (May); weights 3 (most recent), 2, 1. Forecast = (3 x 250 + 2 x 220 + 1 x 200) / (3 + 2 + 1) = (750 + 440 + 200) / 6 = 1,390 / 6 = **231.67** (2 d.p.). Traps: 1,390 forgets to divide; 215.00 puts the weight 3 on the oldest month.
With weights 0.5, 0.3, 0.2 on ₦90m (latest), ₦80m, ₦70m: 45 + 24 + 14 = **₦83 million** (weights sum to 1, so no division).

**C. Exponential smoothing.** Shoe shop: F(May) = 500, A(May) = 540, alpha = 0.2. F(June) = 500 + 0.2 x (540 - 500) = 500 + 8 = **508**.
Two steps: alpha = 0.4, F1 = 200, A1 = 220, A2 = 210. F2 = 200 + 0.4 x 20 = 208. F3 = 208 + 0.4 x (210 - 208) = 208 + 0.8 = **208.8**.
Finding alpha: F = 1,000, A = 1,200, new F = 1,060. 1,060 - 1,000 = alpha x (1,200 - 1,000), so 60 = 200 x alpha, alpha = **0.3**.
Comparing alphas: start 400, actual 500. alpha 0.1 gives 410; alpha 0.5 gives 450; difference **40**. If alpha = 1, F = 300, A = 340 gives next forecast **340** (the last actual).

**D. Trend projection.** Sales 120, 135, 150, 165 (rising 15 a year): year 5 = 165 + 15 = **180**.
Sales ₦80m (2019) and ₦116m (2025): 2019 to 2025 is 6 intervals, so change = 36 / 6 = ₦6m a year. 2027 = 116 + 2 x 6 = **₦128 million**. Trap: dividing by 7 (the number of years) is wrong.
Equation Y = 250 + 18X with X = 1 for 2021: 2027 is X = 7, so Y = 250 + 126 = **₦376 million**.

**E. Least squares (coded years).** Sales 2021 to 2025: 40, 44, 50, 54, 62; X = -2, -1, 0, 1, 2.

| Year | X | Y | XY | X^2 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2021 | -2 | 40 | -80 | 4 |
| 2022 | -1 | 44 | -44 | 1 |
| 2023 | 0 | 50 | 0 | 0 |
| 2024 | 1 | 54 | 54 | 1 |
| 2025 | 2 | 62 | 124 | 4 |
| Total | 0 | 250 | 54 | 10 |

a = 250 / 5 = 50; b = 54 / 10 = 5.4. 2026 is X = 3: Y = 50 + 5.4 x 3 = **₦66.20 million**.

**F. Compound growth.** ₦50m growing 8% a year: 50 x 1.08^2 = 50 x 1.1664 = **₦58.32 million**. Trap: ₦58.00m adds 8% simple growth twice.

**G. MAD.** Actual 100, 110, 95, 105; forecast 98, 104, 101, 103. Errors: 2, 6, -6, 2. Absolute: 2, 6, 6, 2; total 16. MAD = 16 / 4 = **4.00**. Trap: averaging signed errors gives 1.00, which lets positive and negative errors cancel.

### Lesson 4.5: Steps, challenges and implications

**Must know**

**Steps in forecasting:**

1. Understand the problem and set the objective (what, for whom, how far ahead).
2. Choose the best forecasting method(s).
3. Collect and check the data.
4. Analyse the data and make the forecast.
5. Use the forecast in plans (production, inventory, pricing, budget).
6. Monitor: compare actual results with forecasts and adjust the method if inaccurate.

**Forecasting challenges (7):**

1. Lack of data.
2. Over-reliance on past data.
3. Limited visibility into the future.
4. Inaccurate demand models.
5. Poor forecasting processes.
6. Insufficient resources.
7. Unpredictable external factors.

**Explain more**

- Unpredictable external factors in Nigeria include sudden exchange-rate swings, fuel price changes and policy shifts; these can upset even a sound model.
- Over-reliance on past data fails when conditions change, for example when a new competitor such as a cheaper rival brand enters.
- Implications for managers: use more than one method and compare; update forecasts often; build safety stock where errors are costly; treat a forecast as a planning tool, not a guarantee. "Having too many accurate and complete data sources" is **not** a challenge.

### One-minute recap

- Demand forecasting anticipates future demand; strength depends on data, method and user competence.
- Overestimate gives excess inventory; underestimate gives stock-outs and lost sales.
- 6 factors: economic conditions, competition, consumer trends, price, availability, advertising.
- Passive = past sales only; active adds current trends; macro = whole economy; micro = one product, one market.
- Surveys: sample, complete enumeration, end-user research. Delphi = experts in rounds (RAND).
- Barometric: leading (future), lagging (past), coincident (now).
- Moving average: latest n values / n; weighted: divide by sum of weights.
- Exponential smoothing: F(t+1) = F(t) + alpha(A(t) - F(t)); higher alpha reacts faster.
- MAD = average of absolute errors; error = actual - forecast.
- 7 challenges, from lack of data to unpredictable external factors.

### Theory practice

**Question:** A Kano rice seller sold 60, 64, 70, 66 and 72 bags in weeks 1 to 5. Find the 4-week simple moving average forecast for week 6. Then, using exponential smoothing with alpha = 0.5 and a week-5 forecast of 68 bags, find the week-6 forecast.

*Solution:* Moving average uses weeks 2 to 5: (64 + 70 + 66 + 72) / 4 = 272 / 4 = **68.00 bags**. Exponential smoothing: F6 = 68 + 0.5 x (72 - 68) = 68 + 2 = **70 bags**. The smoothing forecast is higher because it gives half the weight to the latest (high) week.
