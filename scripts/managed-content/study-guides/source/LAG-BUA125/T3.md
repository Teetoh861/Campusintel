## Topic 3: Market Attractiveness, Demand Analysis and the Product Life Cycle

**What you must know:** what a market and market attractiveness are; the factors and frameworks (Porter, PESTEL, SWOT, BCG) and the weighted-score method; demand, the law of demand, its determinants and exceptions; shifts versus movements and equilibrium; price, income and cross elasticity with computations; demand estimation; the four stages of the product life cycle and the strategy for each.

### Lesson 3.1: Markets and market attractiveness

**Must know**

- **Market (3 definitions):** (1) a place where official exchange of goods and services is done using a legal tender or medium of exchange; (2) a place where the factors of production are traded on agreed terms; (3) an intended destination for the sale of a product or service that fulfils the firm's economic objectives and satisfies the needs of society.
- **Market attractiveness:** the potential of a market to generate profits for a firm; the potential of a market to provide **long-term profitability and growth opportunities**. It measures how good a market is for a business: how much money a firm can make selling there. A market that can give a lot of profit is attractive.

**5 reasons it matters to managers:**

1. Helps firms make **informed decisions about entering or investing** in a market (the main reason).
2. Influences efficient **resource allocation**.
3. Useful for **strategic planning**.
4. Foundation for **competitive advantage** and sustainable growth.
5. Useful for effective **risk management**.

It does **not** guarantee profit in any market.

**7 key factors:**

1. **Market size and growth potential:** large and growing beats small and stagnant. Market size = total revenue or number of customers; growth rate = percentage change in market size over time.
2. **Competitive landscape and market structure:** lower competition = more attractive. Shaped by number of competitors, market share distribution, barriers to entry and exit, market concentration, price wars and differentiation strategies.
3. **Customer needs and preferences:** strong needs = more attractive. Assess demographics and psychographics, needs and wants, loyalty and retention, purchasing power.
4. **Barriers to entry and exit:** high barriers make a market **less** attractive to a new entrant. Determinants: economies of scale, capital requirements, regulatory barriers.
5. **Profitability and revenue potential:** higher profitability = more appealing. Determinants: cost structures and pricing power, consumption trends, purchasing power, competitive intensity, entry barriers, economies of scale.
6. **Regulatory environment:** favourable rules attract investment and innovation; unfavourable rules raise cost and risk. Covers tariffs, taxes and trade policies; environmental and labour regulations; registration requirements; ease of doing business; economic freedom.
7. **Technological trends (innovation and disruption):** internet penetration, digital transformation, AI, automation, machine learning, low cost of technology development.

**Other factors:** economic stability, risk factors, corporate governance, purchasing power parity (PPP).

**3 challenges in assessing attractiveness:**

1. **Data limitations:** accurate data may be hard to obtain.
2. **Dynamic factors:** conditions and trends change rapidly.
3. **Subjectivity:** different firms perceive attractiveness differently.

**Explain more**

- Tesla, MTN and Dangote Group assess attractiveness before entering new regions or sectors.
- **Nigerian application:** a population of over 220 million (market size); fintech growing at a CAGR of about 15% with Flutterwave and Paystack (growth); telecoms dominated by MTN, Airtel and Glo, so **low** attractiveness for a new entrant because of high competition and capital needs (competitive intensity); Dangote Cement's high margins (profit margins); tax incentives for agribusiness and renewable energy (regulation); internet penetration driving digital banking and B2B, B2C, C2C and C2B payments (technological trends).
- **Trap on barriers:** high barriers are bad news for a firm trying to **enter** (Kola Foods facing huge capital needs, scale economies and hard-to-get licences finds the market less attractive). For firms already **inside**, high barriers protect profits; long-term profits of existing firms are most threatened when barriers are **low**, because newcomers flood in.
- An **emerging** market is more attractive than a saturated one mainly because of **higher growth potential** (not lower risk or better data).
- Things that are **not** attractiveness factors: the number of shareholders or the age of the chief executive of the firm doing the analysis. Customer psychographics belong to customer needs, **not** to the regulatory environment.

### Lesson 3.2: Attractiveness frameworks and the weighted-score method

**Must know**

**4 frameworks:**

1. **Porter's Five Forces:** analyses competitive forces in a market.
2. **PESTEL:** Political, Economic, Social, Technological, Environmental, Legal factors.
3. **SWOT:** Strengths and Weaknesses (**internal**), Opportunities and Threats (**external**).
4. **BCG matrix:** market growth rate against relative market share.

**Porter's five forces and what drives each:**

| Force | Driven by |
|---|---|
| Rivalry among existing competitors (the centre) | number and diversity of competitors, industry concentration, industry growth, quality differences, brand loyalty, exit barriers, switching costs |
| Threat of new entrants | barriers to entry, economies of scale, brand loyalty, capital requirements, cumulative experience, government policies, access to distribution channels, switching costs |
| Bargaining power of suppliers | number and size of suppliers, uniqueness of each supplier's product, the firm's ability to substitute |
| Bargaining power of buyers | number of customers, size of each order, differences between competitors, price sensitivity, ability to substitute, information available, switching costs |
| Threat of substitute products | number of substitutes, buyers' propensity to substitute, relative price-performance, perceived differentiation, switching costs |

Inflation, corporate governance, PPP and "power of government regulators" are **not** among the five forces.

**BCG matrix:**

| | High relative share | Low relative share |
|---|---|---|
| High growth | Star | Question mark |
| Low growth | Cash cow | Dog |

- **Relative market share (RMS) = own sales / sales of the largest competitor** (if you are not the leader, divide by the leader's sales). Above 1.0 means you lead.

**Weighted-score method:**

- **Weight** = how important the factor is to the business. Weights add to 100%, so a missing weight = 100% minus the others.
- **Score** = how favourable the market is on that factor, rated 1 to 10.
- **Weighted score = weight x score.** **Total** = sum of weighted scores = overall attractiveness.

**Market measures:**

- Growth rate = (new size - old size) / old size x 100
- Market size (revenue) = number of customers x average spend per period
- Future value with CAGR = present value x (1 + g)^n

**Explain more**

- **Five-forces cases:** a cinema in Abuja losing customers to cheap streaming = threat of substitutes. Bottled-water buyers ordering in bulk, comparing prices online and switching at no cost = strong buyer power. A cocoa processor in Ondo with only two suppliers of unique grades = strong supplier power. Inflation and interest rates in a PESTEL = **economic** factors. A well-funded foreign rival about to enter = a **threat** in SWOT.
- **Worked table** (scores out of 10):

| Factor | Weight | Score | Weighted |
|---|---|---|---|
| Market size | 20% | 8 | 1.60 |
| Market growth rate | 15% | 9 | 1.35 |
| Profit margins | 15% | 7 | 1.05 |
| Competitive intensity | 10% | 5 | 0.50 |
| Customer needs | 10% | 8 | 0.80 |
| Barriers to entry | 10% | 6 | 0.60 |
| Technological trends | 10% | 7 | 0.70 |
| Regulatory environment | 5% | 4 | 0.20 |
| Economic stability | 5% | 9 | 0.45 |
| Risk factors | 5% | 5 | 0.25 |
| Total | 100% | | 7.50 |

  A total of 7.5 is **moderately attractive**: strong on size, growth and stability; weak on competition and regulation. Repeat for several markets and pick the highest total; never just average the scores.
- **Comparing markets:** profitability 60%, competition 40%. Market A scores 9 and 3: 0.6 x 9 + 0.4 x 3 = 5.4 + 1.2 = 6.6. Market B scores 6 and 8: 3.6 + 3.2 = 6.8. **B wins** even though A has the higher profit score.
- **Revised score:** a factor weighted 20% moves from 6 to 9. The total changes by 0.20 x (9 - 6) = **+0.60**.
- **Required score:** threshold 7.5; factors weighing 80% already give 5.9. Remaining weight 20%: needed score = (7.5 - 5.9) / 0.20 = **8**.
- **Growth rate:** a market grows from ₦50 billion to ₦60 billion: (60 - 50) / 50 = **20%**. Trap: dividing by the new figure gives 16.67%.
- **Market size:** 1.5 million subscribers x ₦2,000 a month x 12 = **₦36 billion a year**. Trap: forgetting the x 12.
- **CAGR:** ₦100 billion growing at 20% for 2 years = 100 x 1.2^2 = **₦144 billion**, not ₦140 billion (growth compounds).
- **BCG computation:** your sales ₦60 million, largest rival ₦40 million: RMS = 1.5 (high). Market growth 12% (above a 10% cut-off) = **star**. Sales ₦10 million against a leader's ₦40 million: RMS = 0.25 (low); at 15% growth it is a **question mark**, at 5% growth a **dog**.

### Lesson 3.3: Demand, the law of demand and its determinants

**Must know**

- **Demand:** the quantity of a good consumers are **willing and able** to buy at various prices over a period. **Quantity demanded:** the specific amount buyers are willing and able to buy at **one particular price**.
- **Law of demand:** other things being equal (ceteris paribus), as price falls, **quantity demanded** rises; as price rises, quantity demanded falls. Price and quantity demanded are inversely related.
- **Demand curve:** slopes **downward from left to right**. It is drawn holding constant **income, tastes and the prices of other goods** (the good's own price is on the axis, so it is not held constant).
- **Demand function:** Qd = f(P, Y, Pr, T, E, N), where P = own price, Y = income, Pr = prices of related goods, T = tastes, E = expectations, N = number of buyers (population). Linear form: Qd = a - bP.

**Why the curve slopes down (3 reasons):**

1. **Substitution effect:** at a higher price, people switch to (search for) substitutes.
2. **Income effect:** a higher price cuts real income, so people buy less.
3. **Diminishing marginal utility:** **marginal utility** is the extra satisfaction from one more unit; it falls as you consume more, so you buy more only at a lower price.

**Determinants of demand:**

1. **Income:** normal good, demand rises with income; inferior good, demand falls as income rises.
2. **Prices of related goods:** **substitutes** (price of one up, demand for the other up); **complements** (price of one up, demand for the other down).
3. **Tastes and preferences** (advertising, fashion).
4. **Expectations** of future prices and incomes.
5. **Population and number of buyers.**

The **cost of producing** the good is a supply factor, **not** a determinant of demand.

**Kinds of demand and goods:**

- **Individual demand** (one consumer) vs **market demand** (sum of all consumers).
- **Derived (induced) demand:** tied to demand for a parent product (tyres from cars). **Autonomous (direct) demand:** not tied to another product.
- **Consumer (final) goods:** bought by households for own use. **Producer (intermediate) goods:** used in further manufacturing, processing or resale (flour bought by a bakery).

**Exceptions to the law of demand:**

1. **Veblen (prestige) goods:** luxury watches, designer bags; bought more as price rises for status.
2. **Giffen goods:** very inferior staples for poor households; a price rise cuts real income so much that they buy more of the staple.
3. **Expectations of further price rises:** buyers stock up while price is rising (on a diagram, the expectation shifts demand right).
4. **Price-quality illusion (ignorance):** buyers judge quality by price.

**Explain more**

- **Trap:** the law is about **quantity demanded**; "as price falls, demand increases" is wrong.
- Coca-Cola and Pepsi are substitutes; cars and petrol, printers and ink, crackers and cheese are complements. Cheaper cheese means more cheese bought, more demand for crackers and a higher cracker price: **complements**. If a fall in one good's price cuts demand for another, they are **substitutes**.
- If coffee demand falls when income falls, coffee is a **normal** good. Second-hand clothing and cheap staples are often inferior for Nigerian households whose incomes rise.
- Bread, a newspaper or a soft drink with many rivals obeys the law; a luxury designer watch is the classic exception.

### Lesson 3.4: Shifts, movements, equilibrium and demand estimation

**Must know**

| Change in quantity demanded | Change in demand |
|---|---|
| caused only by the good's **own price** | caused by income, related prices, tastes, expectations, population |
| **movement along** the same curve | the **whole curve shifts** |
| price rise = movement up/left; price fall = movement down/right | increase = shift **right**; decrease = shift **left** |

**For a normal good, demand shifts left when:** substitutes get cheaper, complements get dearer, or incomes fall. **It shifts right when:** income rises (normal good) or falls (inferior good), a substitute's price rises, a complement's price falls, or a price rise is expected.

- **Equilibrium:** quantity demanded = quantity supplied at the prevailing price.
- **Excess demand (shortage):** price **rises**. **Excess supply (surplus):** price falls.
- **Price rationing:** in a free market, price increases distribute what is available to those willing and able to pay the most.

**Effect of shifts:**

| Change | Equilibrium price | Equilibrium quantity |
|---|---|---|
| Demand up only | up | up |
| Demand down only | down | down |
| Demand up, supply up | indeterminate | up |
| Demand down, supply down | indeterminate | down |
| Demand up, supply down | up | indeterminate |
| Demand down, supply up | down | indeterminate |

- **Demand estimation (measurement) methods:** (1) consumer surveys and interviews; (2) market experiments (test markets, varying price in selected outlets); (3) statistical methods (trend projection, regression) that fit a demand function to past data.

**Explain more**

- A fall in the quantity of Pepsi bought is best explained by a rise in **Pepsi's own price** (a movement). A wine price rise from ₦15,000 to ₦20,000 a bottle cuts **quantity demanded**, not demand.
- A change in the price of compact discs does **not** shift the CD demand curve; changes in cassette prices, income or wealth do.
- Ibadan incomes rise and people buy more chicken at every price: **rightward shift**. Buyers expect rice to rise sharply next month: today's demand shifts **right**.
- An increase in demand raises **quantity supplied** (a movement along supply). Price and quantity both rising signals an **increase in demand**.
- **Demand function in use:** soap demand Qd = 800 - 5P. At P = ₦60, Qd = 800 - 300 = **500 units**. Such equations are estimated by regression on past sales, prices and incomes.

### Lesson 3.5: Elasticity of demand

**Must know**

- **Price elasticity of demand (PED):** responsiveness of quantity demanded to a change in the good's **own price**.
  - PED = % change in quantity demanded / % change in price (quoted in absolute value; the sign is negative).
- **Income elasticity (YED)** = % change in quantity demanded / % change in income.
- **Cross elasticity (XED)** = % change in quantity demanded of good X / % change in price of good Y. It covers both substitutes and complements.

**Percentage-change methods:**

- Original basis: %change = (new - old) / old x 100.
- Midpoint (arc): %change in Q = (Q2 - Q1) / [(Q1 + Q2)/2] x 100; same for P.
- Point elasticity: PED = (dQ/dP) x (P/Q). For Qd = a - bP, dQ/dP = -b.

**Interpreting values:**

| Value | Name |
|---|---|
| PED = 0 | perfectly inelastic (quantity does not change) |
| PED below 1 | inelastic |
| PED = 1 | unitary elastic |
| PED above 1 | elastic |
| PED infinite | perfectly elastic (tiny price change, infinite quantity change) |
| YED below 0 | inferior good |
| YED 0 to 1 | normal necessity |
| YED above 1 | luxury |
| XED positive | substitutes |
| XED negative | complements |
| XED zero | unrelated |

**Total revenue (TR = P x Q) rule:**

| Demand | Price rise | Price cut |
|---|---|---|
| Elastic | TR falls | TR rises |
| Inelastic | TR rises | TR falls |
| Unitary | TR unchanged | TR unchanged |

**Determinants of PED (demand is more elastic when):**

1. More close **substitutes** exist (a single brand is more elastic than the whole product class).
2. The good is a **luxury** rather than a necessity.
3. A **larger share of income** is spent on it.
4. **More time** has passed since the price change (long run more elastic than short run), because over time **consumers are better able to find substitutes**.

**Explain more**

- **Basic:** price up 10%, quantity down 15%: PED = 1.5, **elastic**. Price up 50%, quantity down 90%: 90/50 = **1.8, elastic**. PED 0.75 = inelastic.
- **Original basis:** price ₦100 to ₦120 (+20%), quantity 500 to 450 (-10%). PED = 10/20 = **0.5, inelastic**.
- **Midpoint:** price ₦200 to ₦150, quantity 40 to 60. %ΔQ = 20 / 50 = 40%; %ΔP = -50 / 175 = -28.57%. PED = 40 / 28.57 = **1.4** (elastic; exactly 1.40, since 0.40 / (50/175) = 1.4). Also, quantity 1,000 to 1,500 when price falls ₦150 to ₦100: 500/1,250 = 40% and 50/125 = 40%, so PED = **1.0**.
- **TR check:** 100 units at ₦10,000 = ₦1,000,000; 75 units at ₦15,000 = ₦1,125,000. A price rise raised TR, so demand is **inelastic**.
- **Predicting quantity:** PED = -2, price up 5%: quantity falls 2 x 5 = 10%. Sales of 3,000 become **2,700**. If PED = 0.5, a 10% price **cut** raises quantity by 5%; if PED = 5, a 10% rise cuts quantity by 50%.
- **Required price change:** PED = -2; to sell 12% more, cut price by 12 / 2 = **6%**.
- **Revenue effect:** 1,000 units at ₦500 (TR ₦500,000), PED = -0.5, price raised to ₦600 (+20%). Quantity falls 0.5 x 20 = 10% to 900. New TR = 900 x ₦600 = ₦540,000, a **rise of ₦40,000**.
- **YED:** income ₦80,000 to ₦100,000 (+25%), Indomie packs 10 to 11 (+10%): YED = 10/25 = **0.4, normal necessity**. Income +20%, quantity -5%: YED = **-0.25, inferior**. YED of 1.5 = luxury.
- **XED:** Coke rises ₦300 to ₦360 (+20%); Pepsi sales 1,000 to 1,100 (+10%): XED = **+0.5, substitutes**. A console's price falls 10%, game sales rise 20%: XED = 20 / -10 = **-2.0, complements**. A rise in good 1's price that cuts quantity of good 2 means XED is **negative**.
- **Point elasticity:** Qd = 1,000 - 4P. At P = ₦100, Q = 600; PED = -4 x 100/600 = **-0.67** (0.67 absolute, rounded). At P = ₦150, Q = 400; PED = -4 x 150/400 = -1.5. The **slope is constant** on a straight line but **elasticity changes** from point to point.
- **Applications:** cigarette tax raises most revenue when demand is **inelastic**. A TV maker cutting price 10% on inelastic demand sees revenue **fall**. If supply rises and price falls but quantity is unchanged, PED is **zero**. Aglets (shoelace tips) take a tiny income share: **inelastic**. Streaming gives cinema tickets more substitutes, so their PED **rises**.

### Lesson 3.6: The product life cycle (PLC)

**Must know**

- **PLC:** the course of a product's sales and profits over its life. **4 stages in order: introduction, growth, maturity, decline.** (Some add a **product development** stage before introduction, with zero sales and rising investment.)

| Feature | Introduction | Growth | Maturity | Decline |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Sales | low | rising rapidly | peak, levelling off | falling |
| Cost per customer | high | average | low | low |
| Profit | negative or little | rising | high, then falling | declining |
| Customers | innovators | early adopters | mass market | laggards |
| Competitors | few | growing number | stable, many, intense | declining number |
| Main aim | create awareness and trial | build market share | defend share, extend life | cut costs, harvest or drop |

**Strategies:**

1. **Introduction:** heavy promotion to build awareness, limited distribution, basic product, price skimming or penetration.
2. **Growth:** improve features, add new models, widen distribution, expand capacity, some price cuts to attract buyers.
3. **Maturity:** extend life by **modifying the market** (new users, new uses), **modifying the product** (quality, features, style) or **modifying the marketing mix** (price, promotion, distribution).
4. **Decline:** **maintain**, **harvest** (cut costs, ride remaining sales) or **drop** the product from the line.

**Explain more**

- A newly launched plant-based yoghurt with low sales, high promotion costs and little profit is in **introduction**. A fintech payment app with fast-rising sales, improving profits and new rivals copying it is in **growth**. A detergent brand whose sales have levelled at the peak, with most buyers already using such products and fierce price and promotion rivalry, is in **maturity**.
- **Traps:** withdrawing promotion, cutting outlets or sharp price rises are not maturity strategies; first-time awareness is introduction; new factories for soaring demand is growth.
- **Business implications:** the PLC guides budgeting (cash absorbed in introduction, generated in maturity), pricing, promotion and the timing of launches, so new products are growing as old ones decline.
- A mature milk brand such as Peak launching small sachet sizes to reach low-income buyers is a maturity move (product and mix modification to win new users).

### One-minute recap

- Market attractiveness = potential for long-term profit and growth; 7 key factors plus economic stability, risk, governance, PPP; challenges: data, dynamics, subjectivity.
- High entry barriers hurt entrants but protect incumbents; low barriers threaten incumbents' profits.
- Porter's 5 forces (rivalry at the centre), PESTEL, SWOT (S and W internal), BCG (star, question mark, cash cow, dog; RMS = own sales / largest rival's).
- Weighted score = weight x score, summed; weight = importance, score = favourability.
- Law of demand: price down, quantity demanded up, ceteris paribus; explained by substitution and income effects and diminishing marginal utility.
- Own price = movement along the curve; any other determinant = shift.
- PED above 1 elastic, below 1 inelastic; elastic: price cut raises TR. YED negative = inferior, 0 to 1 = necessity, above 1 = luxury. XED positive = substitutes, negative = complements.
- Demand is more elastic with more substitutes, luxuries, bigger income share and more time.
- PLC: introduction, growth, maturity, decline; extend maturity by modifying market, product or mix; in decline maintain, harvest or drop.

### Theory practice

**Question:** A Surulere kiosk cuts the price of a chilled drink from ₦500 to ₦400, and daily sales rise from 200 to 300 bottles. Using the midpoint method, find the price elasticity of demand, classify it, and state what happened to total revenue.

*Solution:*

- %ΔQ = (300 - 200) / [(200 + 300)/2] = 100 / 250 = 40%.
- %ΔP = (400 - 500) / [(500 + 400)/2] = -100 / 450 = -22.22%.
- PED = 40 / 22.22 = **1.8** in absolute value: **elastic**.
- TR before = ₦500 x 200 = ₦100,000; TR after = ₦400 x 300 = ₦120,000. Revenue **rose by ₦20,000**, as expected when price is cut on elastic demand.
