## Topic 5: Cost Analysis: Short Run and Long Run

**What you must know:** the cost concepts and how to classify costs; the short-run cost measures and their formulas; how to compute them from tables and cost functions; the shapes of the short-run cost curves and how they relate; the law of diminishing returns; long-run costs, economies and diseconomies of scale and the LRAC curve; break-even, shut-down and exit rules; what all this means for managers.

### Lesson 5.1: Cost concepts and classification

**Must know**

- **Short run:** a period in which **at least one factor of production is fixed** (e.g. the size of the factory). So the firm has both fixed and variable costs.
- **Long run:** a period long enough for **all factors to be varied**. **All costs are variable; there are no fixed costs.** The long run is not a set number of months; it depends on how long it takes to change every input.
- In the short run, costs are classified into **fixed costs and variable costs**.

- **Fixed cost:** a cost that does **not change with the level of output** in the short run and **must be paid even when output is zero**. It **remains constant in total** when activity changes (but falls **per unit** as output rises). Examples: rent, insurance premiums, licence fees, interest on loans, salaries of managers.
- **Variable cost:** a cost that **varies in total in proportion to changes in the level of activity**. It **remains constant per unit** at various levels of activity. Examples: raw materials (flour per batch), fuel per trip, packaging, piece-rate wages.
- **Mixed (semi-variable) cost:** contains **a fixed element and a variable element**, e.g. an electricity bill with a fixed monthly charge plus a charge per unit used.
- **Relevant range:** the **range of activity levels over which the firm expects to operate**; within it, the fixed/variable behaviour above holds.

| Cost | In total, as output rises | Per unit, as output rises |
|---|---|---|
| Fixed | stays the same | falls |
| Variable | rises in proportion | stays the same |

Other cost concepts:

- **Explicit cost:** an out-of-pocket expense paid in cash and recorded in the accounts, e.g. rent, salaries, operating expenses.
- **Implicit (imputed) cost:** no cash changes hands; it is the opportunity cost of resources the owner already has, e.g. the salary an owner gives up to run the business.
- **Opportunity cost:** the benefit given up from the next best alternative when one option is chosen.
- **Sunk cost:** money already spent in the past that cannot be recovered, e.g. machinery already bought.
- **Historical cost:** the **actual cost incurred at the time the asset was acquired**.
- **Replacement cost:** the cost of replacing the asset at **today's prices**.
- **Marginal cost:** the cost of producing one extra unit.
- **Incremental (differential) cost:** the extra cost of a decision, often for a change of more than one unit at a time (e.g. adding a new shift).
- **Relevant cost:** a cost that **differs between the alternatives** being considered. Rule: **only future costs that differ between the alternatives are relevant.**
- **Irrelevant cost:** a cost that **does not affect a decision** (sunk costs, and future costs that are the same under every option).
- **Avoidable cost:** a cost that can be saved by not taking a particular option.
- **Private cost:** the cost paid by the **individual or firm directly involved** in producing or consuming a good.
- **External cost:** the cost borne by **people not directly involved**, e.g. residents affected by a factory's pollution.
- **Social cost = private cost + external cost.**

**Explain more**

- For a Lagos bread bakery, **flour used in each batch** is variable; monthly rent, the annual fire insurance premium and the manager's salary are fixed. For a transport company, **diesel used on each trip** is variable (so it is NOT a fixed cost); rent on the park, annual vehicle licence fees and interest on a loan for buses are fixed.
- Trap: "constant **per unit**" describes a **variable** cost; "constant **in total**" describes a **fixed** cost. Exams swap these two phrases.
- Trap: a variable cost does **not** "vary per unit at every level of activity", and is not "a cost that occurs at various times of the year".
- Past costs are **never** relevant to a future decision; not all future costs are relevant (only those that differ); fixed costs that stay the same under every option are irrelevant. Example: Dangote Cement deciding whether to open a new line ignores the ₦50 million already spent on a feasibility study (sunk) and counts only the extra future costs and revenues the new line would bring.
- If a cement plant in Ogun State pays ₦5 million for raw materials and energy (private cost) while its dust costs nearby households ₦2 million in health care and cleaning (external cost), the social cost is ₦5 million + ₦2 million = ₦7 million.

### Lesson 5.2: Short-run cost measures and computations

**Must know**

Formulas (Q = output):

1. **TC = TFC + TVC** (total cost)
2. **TFC = TC at zero output** (because TVC is 0 when Q = 0)
3. **TVC = TC − TFC**
4. **AFC = TFC / Q**
5. **AVC = TVC / Q**
6. **ATC = TC / Q = AFC + AVC**
7. **MC = change in TC / change in Q** (= change in TVC / change in Q, because TFC does not change)
8. From averages: **TC = ATC × Q**, **TVC = AVC × Q**, **TFC = AFC × Q**
9. From marginal costs: **TC = TFC + sum of the MCs**

- **Marginal cost:** the **addition to total cost from producing one more unit of output** (the change in total cost from a one-unit change in output).
- In the short run, both **TVC and TC depend on output**; TFC does not.

**Explain more**

**Worked example 1: a full cost table.** A sachet-water firm has these costs:

| Q | TC (₦) | TVC (₦) | AFC (₦) | AVC (₦) | ATC (₦) | MC (₦) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | 1,000 | 0 | – | – | – | – |
| 1 | 1,400 | 400 | 1,000 | 400 | 1,400 | 400 |
| 2 | 1,700 | 700 | 500 | 350 | 850 | 300 |
| 3 | 1,900 | 900 | 333.33 | 300 | 633.33 | 200 |
| 4 | 2,200 | 1,200 | 250 | 300 | 550 | 300 |
| 5 | 2,700 | 1,700 | 200 | 340 | 540 | 500 |
| 6 | 3,400 | 2,400 | 166.67 | 400 | 566.67 | 700 |

(Figures rounded to 2 decimal places.)

- Step 1: TFC = TC at Q = 0 = **₦1,000**.
- Step 2: TVC at 3 units = 1,900 − 1,000 = **₦900**.
- Step 3: AFC at 4 = 1,000 / 4 = **₦250**. AVC at 5 = 1,700 / 5 = **₦340**. ATC at 3 = 1,900 / 3 = **₦633.33**.
- Step 4: MC of the 4th unit = TC(4) − TC(3) = 2,200 − 1,900 = **₦300**. Note: the MC of a unit is a difference, not the total cost at that unit.
- Step 5: MC is lowest over the **3rd unit** (₦200). AVC is lowest at **₦300** (3 and 4 units). ATC is lowest at **5 units** (₦540).
- Check: at the 4th unit MC = AVC = ₦300 (MC meets AVC at its minimum); the 5th unit's MC (₦500) is below ATC at 4 (₦550), so ATC is still falling; the 6th unit's MC (₦700) is above ₦540, so ATC rises.

**Worked example 2: from averages and totals.**

- AFC ₦12 and AVC ₦30 at 1,000 units: ATC = 12 + 30 = ₦42, so TC = 42 × 1,000 = **₦42,000**.
- ATC ₦90 and AFC ₦25: AVC = 90 − 25 = **₦65**.
- AFC ₦25 at 600 copies, plant unchanged, output rises to 1,000: TFC = 25 × 600 = ₦15,000; new AFC = 15,000 / 1,000 = **₦15**.
- ATC ₦1,100 at 200 units and TFC ₦40,000: TC = 1,100 × 200 = ₦220,000; TVC = 220,000 − 40,000 = **₦180,000**.
- TC ₦2,000 at zero output and ₦8,000 at 50 units: TVC = 8,000 − 2,000 = ₦6,000; AVC = 6,000 / 50 = **₦120**.

**Worked example 3: marginal cost over a range and from averages.**

- TC rises from ₦80,000 to ₦95,000 as output rises from 100 to 150 bags: MC = 15,000 / 50 = **₦300 per bag**. Divide by the change in output, not by the new output.
- AVC is ₦1,800 at 5 pairs and ₦1,900 at 6 pairs: TVC(5) = 9,000; TVC(6) = 11,400; MC of the 6th pair = **₦2,400**. Never just subtract the averages.
- ATC is ₦60 at 7 units and ₦58 at 8 units: TC(7) = 420; TC(8) = 464; MC of the 8th = **₦44**.
- TFC ₦400 and MCs of the 1st, 2nd and 3rd units ₦100, ₦80, ₦90: TC(3) = 400 + 100 + 80 + 90 = **₦670**.

**Worked example 4: cost functions.**

- Linear: TC = 20,000 + 900Q. TFC = ₦20,000 (the constant); AVC = MC = **₦900** at every output. At Q = 40: ATC = 20,000/40 + 900 = 500 + 900 = **₦1,400**.
- Quadratic: TC = 500 + 30Q + 3Q^2. TFC = 500; TVC = 30Q + 3Q^2. At Q = 8: TVC = 240 + 192 = 432, so AVC = 432 / 8 = **₦54**; TC = 932, ATC = 932 / 8 = **₦116.50**. MC of the 8th unit = TC(8) − TC(7) = 932 − 857 = **₦75**.

### Lesson 5.3: Short-run cost curves and the law of diminishing returns

**Must know**

- **Law of diminishing returns (law of variable proportions):** when more and more units of a variable factor are added to a fixed factor, the extra output from each additional unit eventually falls. So, beyond a point, **MC rises**.
- The **short-run** average cost curves (AVC, ATC) and MC are **U-shaped mainly because of the law of diminishing returns**.

Shapes:

1. **TFC:** a **horizontal straight line**.
2. **TVC:** starts from the **origin** and rises.
3. **TC:** starts at the level of fixed cost and lies **above TVC by exactly TFC at every output** (the two curves are parallel).
4. **AFC:** **slopes downward throughout and never touches the output axis** (a fixed sum spread over more units).
5. **AVC, ATC, MC:** **U-shaped**.

Relationships:

- **MC cuts AVC and ATC at their minimum points.**
- **MC below an average → that average is falling. MC above an average → that average is rising.**
- **Vertical gap between ATC and AVC = AFC**, and it **gets smaller as output rises** because AFC falls.
- The minimum of ATC comes at a **higher output** than the minimum of AVC.
- Average cost falls until the **optimum scale**, where **MC = AC**, then rises: a **U-shape**.

**Explain more**

- Picture a small Indomie noodle stall with one cooker (fixed). Adding a second and third worker raises output a lot; a sixth worker mostly gets in the way. Each extra worker adds less output, so each extra plate costs more: MC rises. David Ricardo applied this law to farming on a fixed area of land.
- Think of your CGPA: if your next result (marginal) is below your average, the average falls; if above, it rises. The same logic makes MC pass through the lowest point of AVC and ATC.
- Trap: MC cuts AVC at its **minimum**, never its maximum. Trap: AFC is not U-shaped and not horizontal; TFC is the horizontal one.
- **Input prices and the curves:** a rise in the price of a variable input (flour, fuel) shifts **MC, AVC and ATC** upward but **not AFC**. If a bakery uses 2 bags of flour per batch and flour rises by ₦3,000 a bag, MC per batch rises by 2 × 3,000 = **₦6,000**. A rise in a fixed cost (rent) shifts **AFC and ATC** but **not MC or AVC**: if rent rises from ₦200,000 to ₦260,000 and MC at 1,000 units was ₦150, MC stays **₦150**.

### Lesson 5.4: Long-run costs, economies and diseconomies of scale

**Must know**

- **Economies of scale:** the **fall in long-run average cost** a firm enjoys as it increases the scale of its operations.
- **Diseconomies of scale:** the **rise in LRAC** when a firm grows beyond its efficient size.
- **Constant returns to scale:** LRAC stays the same as scale rises; the LRAC curve is **horizontal** over that range.
- **LRAC curve:** shows the lowest average cost of producing each output when all inputs can be varied. Also called the **planning curve** or **envelope curve**, because it is an **envelope of a series of short-run average cost curves** (one SRAC for each plant size).
- The **LRAC** is U-shaped because of **economies of scale at low outputs followed by diseconomies of scale at high outputs** (not diminishing returns, which is the short-run reason).
- **LRAC = LTC / Q.**

**Internal economies of scale** (arise inside the firm as it grows):

1. **Technical:** bigger, more efficient machines; specialisation; larger production runs.
2. **Marketing (bulk-buying):** discounts for buying inputs in large quantities; advertising spread over more output.
3. **Financial:** big firms borrow more cheaply because lenders see them as less risky.
4. **Managerial:** hiring specialist managers for each function.
5. **Risk-bearing:** spreading risk over many products and markets (diversification).

**External economies of scale:** cost savings to **every firm** in an industry when the industry grows or clusters in one place: a pool of skilled labour, specialist suppliers, better roads and services, shared information.

**Diseconomies of scale:**

1. **Managerial:** too many layers of management; slow decisions, poor communication and coordination.
2. **Labour:** workers feel remote and less motivated.
3. **External:** congestion and rising input prices when an industry grows too large in one place.

**Explain more**

- **Financial economies:** Dangote Cement gets bank loans at lower interest rates than a small block-maker. **Bulk-buying (marketing) economies:** a large supermarket chain buying rice by the trailer-load at a big discount per bag. **External economies:** electronics and phone dealers clustered at Computer Village, Ikeja, where skilled technicians and specialist suppliers cut every firm's costs.
- **Diseconomies example:** a company so large that a simple approval passes through many layers, communication slows and LRAC rises.
- Trap: the **short-run** U-shape is due to **diminishing returns**; the **long-run** U-shape is due to **economies then diseconomies of scale**. Exams swap them.

**Worked example 5: reading scale from a long-run cost table.**

| Q (units) | LTC (₦) | LRAC = LTC / Q (₦) |
|---|---|---|
| 1,000 | 80,000 | 80 |
| 2,000 | 140,000 | 70 |
| 3,000 | 180,000 | 60 |
| 4,000 | 240,000 | 60 |
| 5,000 | 330,000 | 66 |

- 1,000 to 3,000 units: LRAC falls (80 → 70 → 60): **economies of scale**.
- 3,000 to 4,000 units: LRAC constant at 60: **constant returns to scale**.
- 4,000 to 5,000 units: LRAC rises to ₦66: **diseconomies of scale**.
- Always compute LRAC first; LTC itself always rises with output, so it cannot tell you the answer.

### Lesson 5.5: Costs in decisions: break-even, shut-down, exit and implications

**Must know**

- **Break-even point:** the level of activity at which **total revenue equals total cost**, so **profit is zero**. Here **total contribution margin equals total fixed cost**.
- **Contribution per unit = Price − AVC.**
- **Break-even output = TFC / (Price − AVC).**
- **Break-even revenue = break-even output × Price** (or TFC / contribution ratio, where contribution ratio = (P − AVC) / P).
- **Output for a target profit = (TFC + target profit) / (Price − AVC).**
- **Profit = TR − TC = (P × Q) − TFC − (AVC × Q).**
- **Short-run shut-down rule:** shut down if **total revenue cannot cover total variable cost**, i.e. **price is below the minimum of AVC**. The **shut-down point** is minimum AVC.
- **Long-run exit rule:** leave the market if **revenue is less than total cost** (price below ATC), because in the long run there are no fixed costs left to cover.

**Explain more**

**Worked example 6: break-even and profit.** A poultry farm sells crates of eggs at ₦1,500, AVC is ₦900 and TFC is ₦180,000.

- Contribution per crate = 1,500 − 900 = ₦600.
- Break-even output = 180,000 / 600 = **300 crates**.
- Break-even revenue = 300 × 1,500 = **₦450,000**. Check: contribution ratio = 600 / 1,500 = 0.4; 180,000 / 0.4 = ₦450,000.
- Output for a profit of ₦120,000 = (180,000 + 120,000) / 600 = **500 crates**.
- Profit at 800 crates = 800 × 1,500 − 180,000 − 800 × 900 = 1,200,000 − 180,000 − 720,000 = **₦300,000**.

**Why the shut-down rule works.** In the short run, the fixed cost must be paid even at zero output. If a firm is making a loss but its revenue covers TVC and part of TFC, producing loses less than shutting down, so it keeps going. Only when price falls below minimum AVC does every unit add to the loss. In Worked example 1, minimum AVC is ₦300, so a price-taking firm would stop producing in the short run at any price below **₦300**. Trap: a firm does not shut down simply because it makes "any loss" or because fixed costs exceed revenue; and MC above AVC is normal, not a shut-down signal.

**Implications for managers:**

1. Use only **relevant (future, differing) costs** in decisions; ignore sunk costs.
2. Watch **MC** when deciding whether to expand output; expand while each extra unit's revenue covers its MC.
3. Spread fixed costs over more output to cut AFC and ATC (why MTN and Globacom chase more subscribers on the same network).
4. Choose a plant size on the lowest part of the LRAC; grow to gain economies of scale, but guard against diseconomies with good structure and communication.
5. Know the **break-even** and **shut-down** prices before setting prices or cutting output.
6. Consider **external (social) costs**, which may lead to regulation, levies or reputation damage.

### One-minute recap

- Short run: at least one factor fixed; long run: all costs variable.
- Fixed cost: constant in total, falls per unit; variable cost: varies in total, constant per unit; mixed = both.
- TC = TFC + TVC; AFC = TFC/Q; AVC = TVC/Q; ATC = AFC + AVC; MC = change in TC / change in Q.
- TFC horizontal; AFC falls forever; AVC, ATC, MC U-shaped; MC cuts AVC and ATC at their minimum.
- Short-run U-shape: diminishing returns. Long-run U-shape: economies then diseconomies of scale.
- LRAC = planning (envelope) curve; horizontal under constant returns.
- Relevant costs are future and differ between alternatives; sunk costs are irrelevant.
- Social cost = private cost + external cost.
- Break-even Q = TFC / (P − AVC); shut down if P < minimum AVC; exit in the long run if TR < TC.

### Theory practice

**Question:** A firm's TFC is ₦200. Its TVC at 1, 2, 3 and 4 units is ₦80, ₦140, ₦210 and ₦320. Find AVC, ATC and MC at each output, and the lowest price at which a price-taking firm would keep producing in the short run.

*Solution:*

| Q | TVC (₦) | AVC = TVC/Q (₦) | ATC = (200 + TVC)/Q (₦) | MC (₦) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 80 | 80 | 280 | 80 |
| 2 | 140 | 70 | 170 | 60 |
| 3 | 210 | 70 | 136.67 | 70 |
| 4 | 320 | 80 | 130 | 110 |

(ATC at 3 rounded to 2 decimal places.) Minimum AVC is **₦70** (at 2 and 3 units), so the shut-down price is ₦70: below it, revenue would not cover variable cost. Note that MC of the 3rd unit (₦70) equals AVC at its minimum.
