## Topic 8: Market Structure: Types of Market and Competition Analysis

**What you must know:** the meaning and 7 types of market structure; revenue, profit and efficiency tools and the MR = MC rule; perfect competition, monopoly (with price discrimination), monopolistic competition, duopoly, oligopoly (kinked demand, collusion, cartels), monopsony and duopsony; how each sets price and output in the short and long run; the computations.

### Lesson 8.1: Market structure and the core tools

**Must know**

- **Market structure:** the operations of different industries and their classification in terms of the **degree and nature of competition** that differentiate them.
- **Industry:** a group of firms producing similar or identical products. **Firm:** a business unit that owns and runs one or more plants. **Plant:** a single production unit (a factory).
- **7 types of market structure:**

1. Perfect competition
2. Monopoly
3. Duopoly
4. Oligopoly
5. Monopolistic competition
6. Monopsony (one buyer)
7. Duopsony (two buyers)

- The most important basis of classification is **the number of firms and the degree of product differentiation**. Ease of entry and power over price follow from these.
- **Revenue formulas:** TR = P × Q. AR = TR ÷ Q = P. MR = change in TR ÷ change in Q (the extra revenue from one more unit).
- **Profit:** total profit = TR − TC = (P − ATC) × Q.
- **Normal profit:** the minimum profit needed to keep a firm in its present industry, earned when total revenue just covers total cost **including opportunity cost** (zero economic profit). **Supernormal (economic) profit:** any profit above normal profit.
- **The profit-maximising rule (all market structures):** produce where **MR = MC** (with MC rising). In a table: keep producing each extra unit while MR is at least MC; stop before the first unit whose MC exceeds its MR.
- **Productive efficiency:** producing at the **lowest possible average cost** (minimum ATC). **Allocative efficiency:** **P = MC** (price equals the cost of the last unit, so society gets the right amount).
- **Barriers to entry:** anything that stops new firms entering: economies of scale, patents, government licences, ownership of a strategic raw material, access to technology others cannot get, huge capital needs.

**Explain more**

- "Communism" is an economic **system**, not a market structure. So are capitalism and the mixed economy. Market structures describe competition **inside an industry**.
- **Minimum efficient scale (MES):** the smallest output at which long-run average cost reaches its lowest. If MES is very large relative to total market demand, the market can hold **only one or a few firms** (monopoly or oligopoly). If MES is small, many small firms survive.
- **Worked MR from a change in TR:** TR rises from ₦36,000 to ₦40,200 when sales rise from 150 to 165 units. MR = (40,200 − 36,000) ÷ (165 − 150) = 4,200 ÷ 15 = **₦280 per unit**. Trap: ₦4,200 is the change in TR, not MR.
- Trap: profit is maximised at MR = MC, **not** where TR is greatest and **not** where ATC is lowest.

### Lesson 8.2: Perfect competition

**Must know**

**Features:**

1. A **complete absence of rivalry** among firms; all sellers (and buyers) have **perfect knowledge** of the market.
2. A **large number of firms** and a **large number of buyers**.
3. A single firm cannot alter the market price; likewise one buyer cannot influence it.
4. Price is set solely by the **price mechanism** (market demand and supply), so firms are **price takers**.
5. The exit of a firm has no influence on price.
6. Products are **homogeneous** (identical): no room for product differentiation, so each firm's product has many **perfect substitutes**.
7. **No barriers** to entry or exit (free entry and exit), and no government regulation.

- **Demand facing one firm:** **perfectly elastic (horizontal)** at the market price. So **P = AR = MR**.
- **Equilibrium:** profit is maximised where **MR = MC**, which here means **P = MC**.
- **Short run:** the firm may earn supernormal profit, normal profit or a loss.
- **Shut-down rule (short run):** keep producing if P ≥ AVC (revenue covers variable cost and pays part of fixed cost); **shut down if P < AVC**. When shut down, the loss = total fixed cost.
- **Short-run supply curve:** the part of the MC curve lying **above the minimum point of the AVC curve**.
- **Long run:** supernormal profit attracts **new firms**, market supply rises and price falls until only **normal profit** is earned. Losses make firms **exit**, market supply falls and price rises. Entry and exit stop when firms make **zero economic profit**. Long-run equilibrium: **P = MC = minimum ATC** (both productively and allocatively efficient).

**Explain more**

- A firm's only real choices: produce more, produce less, or leave the industry. It **cannot** raise its price above the market price: it would sell nothing.
- Perfect competition is a **theoretical model**; its strict assumptions rarely all hold. The nearest real example is **farming of a staple crop** (for example many tomato or maize farmers selling identical produce at the market price). Diamonds, soft drinks (Coca-Cola, Pepsi, Bigi) and branded trainers are **not** close.
- **Producing where P is not equal to MC:** if P > MC, producing more adds more to revenue than to cost, so the firm **raises profit by producing more**. If P < MC, it should produce less.
- When firms exit, the market **quantity supplied falls** (supply shifts left), so price **rises**; exit does **not** drive prices down.

**Worked examples (check every step):**

1. **TR and MR.** A garri seller in a competitive market sells 400 buckets at ₦1,500. TR = 1,500 × 400 = **₦600,000**. MR = AR = P = **₦1,500** (every extra bucket adds exactly the price).
2. **Output from a MC table.** Price ₦1,500. MC of units 1 to 6: ₦900, ₦1,100, ₦1,300, ₦1,450, ₦1,600, ₦1,800. Units 1 to 4 each have MC below ₦1,500 (the 4th adds ₦1,500 − ₦1,450 = ₦50 profit). The 5th costs ₦1,600 > ₦1,500, so it loses ₦100. Produce **4 units**.
3. **Profit.** Q = 2,000 where MR = MC, P = ₦600, ATC = ₦520. Profit = (600 − 520) × 2,000 = **₦160,000** (supernormal, since P > ATC).
4. **Loss but keep producing.** P = ₦600, ATC = ₦650, AVC = ₦560, Q = 2,000. Loss = (600 − 650) × 2,000 = **−₦100,000**. Since P (₦600) > AVC (₦560), keep producing. If it shut down it would lose all fixed cost: AFC = 650 − 560 = ₦90, so FC = 90 × 2,000 = ₦180,000. Producing loses ₦80,000 less.
5. **Shut down.** Same costs but P = ₦500 < AVC ₦560. Producing loss = (500 − 650) × 2,000 = −₦300,000; shutting down loses only FC ₦180,000. **Shut down**: loss is ₦120,000 smaller.
6. **ATC from TC.** TC = ₦3,300,000 for 1,500 units. ATC = 3,300,000 ÷ 1,500 = **₦2,200**. At P = ₦2,000 (below ATC) the firm makes a loss of (2,000 − 2,200) × 1,500 = −₦300,000. If P were above ATC it would earn supernormal profit.

### Lesson 8.3: Monopoly and price discrimination

**Must know**

- **Monopoly:** a market with a **single firm or seller**; the firm **is** the industry and sells to numerous buyers. Its product has **no close substitutes**.

**Characteristics:**

1. Mostly happens because of **economies of scale** advantages.
2. The firm is a **price maker** (unlike price takers in perfect competition).
3. The firm creates **barriers to entry**, making it difficult for others to enter.
4. **Price discrimination** is common and a strategy.
5. The firm is a **profit maximiser**.
6. It often has access to **market intelligence or technology** that others cannot access, which puts it in a superior position.

**Sources (causes) of monopoly:** economies of scale; **patent rights**; **ownership of a strategic raw material**; **government licensing**; a **market size that does not require more than one producer** (natural monopoly); exclusive technology or market intelligence.

- **Demand:** the monopolist faces the **downward-sloping** market demand curve: it can sell more only by lowering its price. AR = P.
- **MR is less than price**, because to sell one more unit it must lower the price on **all** the units it sells.
- **Equilibrium:** output where **MR = MC**; price read off the demand curve at that output (P > MC).
- **Long run:** the monopolist can keep **supernormal profit**, because barriers to entry stop new firms competing it away.
- **Price discrimination:** selling the **same product at different prices to different buyers** for reasons not related to cost differences.

**Explain more**

- **Conditions for price discrimination:** (1) the seller has price-making power; (2) the markets can be kept separate so buyers cannot resell; (3) demand elasticity differs between groups. The **higher price goes to the group with less elastic demand**. **Degrees** (Pigou): first degree, each buyer pays the most he is willing to pay; second degree, price varies with quantity bought (bulk discounts); third degree, different prices for different groups (student fares, peak and off-peak airline fares, different electricity tariffs for homes and factories).
- **Examples:** a firm holding a 20-year patent on a new drug is a monopoly from **patent rights**. A small town that can support only one water-supply firm (laying pipes twice would never pay) is a **natural monopoly**: market size and economies of scale. The old NEPA was a government-owned electricity monopoly created by law.
- Compared with perfect competition, a monopoly charges a **higher price**, produces a **lower output**, is not allocatively efficient (P > MC) and need not produce at minimum ATC.
- A monopolist never chooses output where MR is negative. TR is greatest where MR = 0, but profit is greatest where MR = MC.

**Worked monopoly table.** Fixed cost ₦500. AR = P; TR = P × Q; MR = change in TR; TC = FC + sum of MCs so far.

| Q | P (AR) ₦ | TR ₦ | MR ₦ | MC ₦ | TC ₦ | Profit ₦ |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 1,000 | 1,000 | 1,000 | 300 | 800 | 200 |
| 2 | 850 | 1,700 | 700 | 250 | 1,050 | 650 |
| 3 | 700 | 2,100 | 400 | 300 | 1,350 | **750** |
| 4 | 550 | 2,200 | 100 | 350 | 1,700 | 500 |
| 5 | 400 | 2,000 | −200 | 400 | 2,100 | −100 |
| 6 | 250 | 1,500 | −500 | 450 | 2,550 | −1,050 |

- TR at 3 units = 700 × 3 = ₦2,100. MR of the 2nd unit = 1,700 − 1,000 = ₦700.
- AR at 3 units = 2,100 ÷ 3 = ₦700 (always equals the price).
- **TR is maximum at 4 units** (₦2,200; MR of the 5th is negative).
- **Profit-maximising output:** MR ≥ MC for units 1 to 3 (3rd: 400 ≥ 300); the 4th has MR ₦100 < MC ₦350. So **3 units**, at **price ₦700** (read from the demand schedule, not MR or MC).
- **Maximum profit** = TR − TC = 2,100 − (500 + 300 + 250 + 300) = 2,100 − 1,350 = **₦750**. The profit column confirms it.

### Lesson 8.4: Monopolistic competition

**Must know**

- **Monopolistic competition:** an industry with a **large number of buyers and sellers** whose products are **differentiated but close substitutes**.

**Features:**

1. **Free entry and exit** of firms.
2. Firms aim at **profit maximisation** in both short and long run, which drives strategies like product differentiation, advertising, product quality and price.
3. Each firm has very limited power over the market price, because rivals offer many close substitutes.
4. Products are **substitutable, but not perfect substitutes**.
5. **No firm has monopoly power**; where short-run supernormal profit appears, it **attracts more firms** into the market, which wipes away the profit.
6. **Imperfect consumer knowledge** of the firms' brands.

- **Main difference from perfect competition:** products are **homogeneous** in perfect competition but **differentiated** in monopolistic competition.
- **Equilibrium:** MR = MC. **Short run:** supernormal profit possible. **Long run:** entry wipes it out, so firms earn **zero economic profit (only normal profit)**.

**Explain more**

- Feature 3 is sometimes worded "firms are price takers", meaning no firm can push its price far from rivals' prices. Strictly, each firm faces a **downward-sloping** (very elastic) demand curve because its brand is a little different, so it can set its own price within a narrow range. In a choice between "price taker" and "price maker" for a monopolistic competitor, look at what the other options say; the "price taker" label belongs most firmly to perfect competition.
- In the long run firms produce below the output of minimum ATC, so they operate with **excess capacity**; perfect competitors do not.
- **Examples:** dozens of hair salons on a Lagos street, each with slightly different styles and ambience, and new ones opening easily; restaurants, bukas, fashion designers, bakeries.
- Edward Chamberlin (and Joan Robinson, as "imperfect competition") developed the theory in 1933.

### Lesson 8.5: Oligopoly and duopoly

**Must know**

- **Oligopoly:** a market with a **small number of firms**, none able to dominate the others, where all the firms have significant influence on market behaviour (a few firms account for most or all of industry output).

**Features of oligopoly:**

1. Products are **homogeneous and substitutable; however, product differentiation also happens** (so products can be either homogeneous or differentiated).
2. **Small number** of producers.
3. **High barriers to entry**.
4. Firms are **price setters**.
5. Firms are **interdependent**: one firm's action affects the other firms (the key distinguishing feature).
6. Each firm has its own **market share**.
7. **Advertising** and other marketing are strategic.

- **Duopoly:** an industry dominated by **two firms** whose decisions affect each other. **Features:**

1. The two firms work together and interact in many decisions.
2. Entry barriers are lower, which allows another firm to enter.
3. Products are homogeneous.
4. Price competition is common because products are highly substitutable.
5. Quantity demanded of one firm's product depends on **its own price and the price charged by the rival**.
6. Firms are strategically interdependent.

- **Equilibrium:** an oligopolist maximises profit where **MR = MC**.
- **Kinked demand curve** (Paul Sweezy, 1939): rivals **match price cuts but do not match price increases**. Demand is elastic above the current price and inelastic below it, so there is a kink and a **gap in the MR curve**; MC can change within the gap without changing price. Result: prices are **relatively rigid ("sticky")** even when costs change.
- **Collusion:** an agreement among firms to fix prices and quantities. **Cartel:** a group of firms that comes together **formally** to make joint price and output decisions (OPEC, of which Nigeria is a member). Cartels arise most in **oligopoly**.
- **Collusive models:** cartel, joint profit maximisation, market-sharing cartel, price leadership (a dominant firm sets the price and others follow). **Non-collusive models:** the **kinked demand curve**, Cournot (firms choose quantities), Bertrand (firms compete on price), Stackelberg (a leader chooses quantity first).
- **Concentration ratio:** CRn = sum of the market shares of the n largest firms (or their sales ÷ total industry sales × 100). The higher it is, the fewer firms dominate.

**Explain more**

- **Pure oligopoly:** few firms, **homogeneous** product (petroleum, cement, steel). **Differentiated oligopoly:** few firms, branded products (cars, telecom data plans).
- **Nigerian examples:** mobile telecoms (MTN, Airtel, Glo), each spending heavily on advertising and quickly answering a rival's new data price; cement (Dangote, BUA, Lafarge Africa). Two firms supplying nearly all cooking gas cylinders in a state, each matching the other's price cuts, is a **duopoly**.
- **Output ranking:** oligopoly output is usually **higher than monopoly and lower than perfect competition**. With identical products: collusion gives the lowest output (like a monopoly), Cournot more, Stackelberg more again, and **Bertrand price competition the highest** (price driven down towards MC, like perfect competition).
- **Game theory** studies decisions when each player's result depends on rivals' choices; the prisoners' dilemma shows why cartel members are tempted to cheat.
- **Contestable markets** (William Baumol, 1982): if entry and exit are free and costless, the threat of entry forces even a few large firms to behave like **perfectly competitive firms** (normal profit only).
- Trap: "complete absence of rivalry" describes perfect competition, never oligopoly. "Profit-maximising behaviour" does not distinguish oligopoly; every structure maximises profit.

**Worked concentration ratios:**

1. Market shares of the six largest firms: 28%, 22%, 15%, 10%, 8%, 7%. CR4 = 28 + 22 + 15 + 10 = **75%** (ignore the 5th and 6th).
2. Industry sales ₦800 million; four largest firms sell ₦200m, ₦160m, ₦100m, ₦60m. CR4 = (200 + 160 + 100 + 60) ÷ 800 × 100 = 520 ÷ 800 × 100 = **65%**.
3. As a rough guide: a high ratio for a few firms points to oligopoly; two firms holding most of the market (for example CR2 near 90%) points to a duopoly-like market; one firm near 100% is monopoly; a low ratio spread over many firms points to perfect or monopolistic competition.

### Lesson 8.6: Monopsony, duopsony and comparing structures

**Must know**

- **Monopsony:** a market with a **single buyer** of products supplied by numerous suppliers. **Features:**

1. Barriers to entry into the market.
2. Only **one buyer** of the goods produced; no other buyers.
3. The single buyer has **huge power** over prices and quantities produced.
4. **Low prices** for suppliers (they have low bargaining power).
5. **Causes:** government regulations, geographical location, unique consumption behaviour, and unregulated **mergers and acquisitions**.
6. **Low innovation and market inefficiency**, because competition is low.

- **Duopsony:** a market with **two large firms** buying the goods and services of many producers. **Features:**

1. The two firms determine market demand.
2. They enjoy great bargaining power.
3. Less competition and higher profit.
4. **High barriers to entry**.

| Structure | Firms | Product | Entry | Power over price | Long-run profit |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Perfect competition | very many | homogeneous | free | none (price taker) | normal only |
| Monopolistic competition | many | differentiated, close substitutes | free | slight | normal only |
| Oligopoly | few | homogeneous or differentiated | high barriers | price setter, interdependent | can be supernormal |
| Duopoly | two | homogeneous | lower barriers | interdependent | can be supernormal |
| Monopoly | one seller | no close substitute | blocked | price maker | supernormal |
| Monopsony | one buyer | many suppliers | barriers | buyer sets low prices | buyer gains |
| Duopsony | two buyers | many suppliers | high barriers | buyers dominate | higher profit |

**Explain more**

- **Memory trick:** "-poly" = sellers, "-psony" = buyers. Monopoly = one seller; monopsony = one buyer. Duopoly = two sellers; duopsony = two buyers.
- **Examples:** in a remote mining town where one company is the only employer and workers have no other job options, the labour market is a **monopsony**. Thousands of cocoa farmers who can sell only to two large processing companies, which together set how much is bought and at what price, face a **duopsony**. Nigeria's old commodity marketing boards (abolished in 1986) were the only legal buyers of export crops like cocoa: a monopsony created by government regulation.
- A large number of competing buyers is the opposite of monopsony, never a cause of it.
- **Implications for managers:** know your structure before pricing. A price taker controls only output and cost; a monopolist or oligopolist must weigh price, rivals' reactions and regulators; a monopolistic competitor must keep differentiating, because profits attract entrants.

### One-minute recap

- Market structure = classification of industries by the degree and nature of competition; 7 types; classified mainly by number of firms and product differentiation.
- Every firm maximises profit at **MR = MC**; profit = (P − ATC) × Q; normal profit = zero economic profit.
- Perfect competition: many firms, homogeneous product, free entry, price takers, horizontal demand, P = MR; shut down if P < AVC; long run P = MC = min ATC, normal profit, both efficiencies.
- Monopoly: one seller, no close substitutes, price maker, barriers (scale, patent, raw material, licence, small market), MR < P, keeps supernormal profit, price discrimination.
- Monopolistic competition: many firms, differentiated close substitutes, free entry, normal profit in the long run, excess capacity.
- Oligopoly: few firms, interdependence, high barriers, price setters; kinked demand gives sticky prices; collusion and cartels.
- Duopoly: two firms; demand depends on own and rival's price.
- Monopsony = one buyer (low supplier prices, low innovation); duopsony = two buyers (high barriers, great bargaining power).
- CRn = sum of the n largest market shares.

### Theory practice

**Question:** A perfectly competitive firm produces 800 units where MR = MC. The market price is ₦250, its ATC is ₦290 and its AVC is ₦230. Find its profit or loss and say whether it should keep producing in the short run.

*Solution:* Loss = (P − ATC) × Q = (250 − 290) × 800 = **−₦32,000**. Since P (₦250) is above AVC (₦230), it should **keep producing**. If it shut down it would lose its whole fixed cost: AFC = 290 − 230 = ₦60, so FC = 60 × 800 = ₦48,000. Producing keeps the loss ₦16,000 smaller.
