## Topic 6: Introduction to Financial Markets

**What you must know:** the financial system and the meaning, functions and types of financial markets; money market vs capital market and their instruments; primary vs secondary markets; the Nigerian stock market; participants, institutions and regulators (CBN, SEC, NGX); short-, medium- and long-term sources of finance with their merits and demerits.

### Lesson 6.1: The financial system and financial markets

**Must know**

- **Financial system:** a complex of **institutions, instruments, rules and regulations** that facilitates the **mobilisation and transfer of resources from savings-surplus units to deficit units**.
- **Financial market:** a marketplace where buyers and sellers **trade financial assets** such as stocks, bonds, currencies, derivatives and commodities. It facilitates the flow of funds between investors, businesses and governments, mobilising capital, managing risk and allocating resources efficiently.
- Other definitions: any system or structure that makes it possible to trade in different kinds of financial securities; markets where debt, debentures and equities are traded; markets where funds can be raised.
- A financial market brings together **those who have money** (lenders, buyers of financial instruments) and **those who need money** (borrowers, sellers of financial instruments). Without it, borrowers would find it difficult to locate lenders.
- **Financial security (instrument):** the receipt the borrower issues to the lender as a promise or evidence to pay back the capital. It is a tradeable claim to money or ownership: a share, bond, treasury bill.
- **Financial asset:** a claim to money: **cash**, shares, bonds, deposits. **Real (physical) assets:** machinery, buildings, factories, inventory.

**7 functions of financial markets:**

1. **Capital formation:** raising capital by issuing stocks and bonds.
2. **Liquidity provision:** assets can be converted to cash without significant loss in value.
3. **Efficient price discovery:** prices are set by **supply and demand**, reflecting available information.
4. **Risk management:** derivatives hedge price, interest-rate and currency risks.
5. **Facilitating savings and investment.**
6. **Economic growth:** capital flows to the projects with the highest potential.
7. **Reducing transaction costs:** trading is centralised.

**6 types of financial markets:**

1. **Capital markets:** long-term instruments (stocks, bonds); split into primary and secondary.
2. **Money markets:** short-term instruments (maturity **less than one year**).
3. **Foreign exchange (forex) markets:** trading currencies.
4. **Derivatives markets:** futures, options, swaps.
5. **Commodity markets:** metals, energy, agricultural products.
6. **Cryptocurrency markets:** Bitcoin, Ethereum and others.

**Explain more**

- In short: financial markets **enable funds to move efficiently** from savers to users. They do not curtail competition, raise production costs or restrict investment choice.
- Trap: a factory, machine or office building is a **real asset**, not a financial instrument. **Accounts payable** is a liability, not an asset. Cash **is** a financial asset.
- Trap: "savings market" or "economic system" are distractors for the definition of the **financial system**.
- The narrow two-way split (money market and capital market) is the one most questions use; the six-way list adds forex, derivatives, commodity and crypto markets.

### Lesson 6.2: Money market vs capital market

**Must know**

- **Money market:** where **short-term** financial securities are bought and sold. Funds raised can be used for a **maximum of one year or less**.
- **Capital market:** where securities with a life span of **more than one year** are bought and sold; the market for **medium- and long-term** funds (long-term borrowing and lending).

**Functions of the money market (5):**

1. Promotes economic growth by making money available to different sectors.
2. Platform for effective implementation of **monetary policy**.
3. Stabilises demand and supply of money for short-term transactions.
4. Avenue for short-term investment.
5. Generates savings and investment in the economy.

**Money market instruments:**

1. **Treasury bills:** used by government to borrow for a short time; issued by the **Central Bank** (on behalf of government); maturity under one year.
2. **Repurchase agreements (repos):** the seller sells a security to the buyer and agrees to **buy the same security back** later.
3. **Certificate of deposit:** a promissory note issued by a **bank** giving the bearer the right to receive interest.
4. **Banker's acceptance:** a promise by the buyer to pay the seller, with the **buyer's bank standing as guarantor**.
5. **Commercial paper:** short-term **unsecured promissory note** issued by large, creditworthy (**blue-chip**) companies, backed only by the issuer's promise to pay the face value at maturity.
6. **Call money:** very short-term lending between banks, repayable on call. **Bank deposits** are also money market instruments.

**Capital market instruments:**

1. **Ordinary shares (equities, common stock):** part ownership; each member is a fractional owner bearing the maximum entrepreneurial risk; voting rights and dividends.
2. **Preference shares:** fixed dividends, usually no voting rights.
3. **Bonds:** long-term debt issued by companies or governments, paying fixed interest and **redeemable** at maturity.
4. **Debentures (debenture stock):** debt instruments issued by companies to raise capital. The basic (naked) debenture is **not secured** by physical assets; it promises interest and principal, and holders become **creditors** of the company.

| Point | Money market | Capital market |
|---|---|---|
| Maturity | One year or less | More than one year |
| Purpose | Working capital, short-term needs | Long-term investment |
| Instruments | T-bills, CP, CDs, BAs, repos, call money | Shares, bonds, debentures |
| Apex regulator in Nigeria | **CBN** | **SEC** |

**Other instruments:** derivatives (**futures**: agreement to buy or sell at a set price on a future date; **options**: the right but not the obligation to buy or sell before expiry; **swaps**: exchange of cash flows); forex (spot, forward, currency swaps); commodity contracts; cryptocurrencies.

**Treasury bill pricing (discount basis, 365-day year):**

- Discount = Face value × discount rate × days / 365
- Price = Face value − Discount
- Yield = (Discount / Price) × (365 / days)

**Explain more**

- **Worked example.** A 91-day treasury bill has a face value of ₦1,000,000 and a discount rate of 18% a year.
  - Discount = 1,000,000 × 0.18 × 91 / 365 = **₦44,876.71**
  - Price = 1,000,000 − 44,876.71 = **₦955,123.29**
  - Yield = (44,876.71 / 955,123.29) × (365 / 91) = **18.85%** (to 2 d.p.). The yield is above the discount rate because you earn the discount on ₦955,123.29, not on ₦1,000,000.
- Nigerian T-bills run for 91, 182 or 364 days. They pay no coupon; you buy below face value and receive the full face value at maturity.
- Traps: "the stock market" is a **long-term capital market**, not a short-term lending market. Debenture stock and preference shares are **capital market**; bankers' acceptances, commercial paper and bank deposits are **money market**.
- A **bond** is debt (you lend); a **stock** is ownership (you own). A certificate of deposit and a loan are also debt.

### Lesson 6.3: Primary vs secondary markets and the stock market

**Must know**

- **Primary market:** where **new** securities are issued and sold to investors **for the first time** (e.g. IPOs). Transactions are between the **issuing company and investors**; the issuer gets the money.
- **Secondary market:** where **already issued** securities are traded among investors. The **issuing company is not involved**.
- **Stock market:** the segment of the financial market where equities and other securities are issued, bought and sold. In Nigeria it is regulated by the **SEC** and facilitated by the **Nigerian Exchange Group (NGX)**, formerly the Nigerian Stock Exchange (NSE).
- **Stock exchange:** its major role is to **facilitate the trading of securities** in an organised market.

**Primary market functions in Nigeria (7):** capital formation; facilitates economic growth; investment opportunities; promotes industrialisation; government fundraising (FGN bonds); encourages entrepreneurship; price determination (sets the initial price). **Importance:** boosts financial inclusion; supports public-private partnerships; stimulates foreign investment.

**Secondary market functions (8):** liquidity provision; continuous price discovery; portfolio diversification; encourages investment; capital reallocation; wealth creation (capital gains, dividends, interest); supports the primary market (assures buyers they can sell later); regulatory oversight by SEC and NGX. **Importance:** market confidence; foreign participation; stimulates economic growth; wealth redistribution; enables corporate governance.

**Importance of the stock market in Nigeria (10):**

1. Capital formation
2. Economic growth (adds to GDP)
3. Wealth creation
4. Encourages savings and investment
5. Liquidity provision
6. Promotes corporate governance
7. Employment generation (brokers, analysts, fund managers)
8. Attracts foreign investment
9. Supports government financing
10. Risk diversification

**Functions of the stock market (9):** mobilisation of savings; facilitating capital raising (IPOs, bonds); providing liquidity; price discovery; enhancing corporate governance; **economic indicator** (barometer of economic health); risk management; wealth redistribution; investment opportunities.

**Instruments traded on the Nigerian stock market (8):** equities (ordinary and preference shares); debt (corporate and government bonds); **ETFs**; derivatives; **mutual funds**; **REITs** (returns from real estate); commercial papers; **GDRs** (let Nigerian companies trade shares on foreign exchanges).

**Explain more**

- Memory hook: **primary = first sale, money to the company; secondary = resale, money between investors.** "Tertiary market" is a distractor. Where "primary market" is an option, pick it over the broader "capital market" for new issues.
- Share prices are set by **market supply and demand**, not by government, wages or production costs.
- **Mutual fund:** a **collective investment pool** of many investors' money, professionally managed and spread over many securities.
- In 2021 the Nigerian Stock Exchange was demutualised into the Nigerian Exchange Group; questions may still say "NSE".

### Lesson 6.4: Participants, institutions and regulators

**Must know**

- **Financial intermediary:** a **link between lenders and borrowers** (savers and users of funds): banks, mutual funds, hedge funds, investment firms.
- **Financial institutions** deal in money and financial services: commercial (deposit money) banks, stock exchanges, insurance companies, pension funds. A **supermarket is not one**.

**10 participants in financial markets:**

1. **Investors:** supply funds to earn returns (pension funds, insurers).
2. **Issuers:** governments and corporations raising capital.
3. **Brokers and dealers:** brokers act for others for a commission; dealers trade on their own account.
4. **Regulatory bodies:** e.g. SEC (US), RBI (India), FCA (UK).
5. **Financial intermediaries.**
6. **Speculators:** profit from price movements.
7. **Hedgers:** use instruments to manage risk.
8. **Arbitrageurs:** exploit price differences for risk-free profit.
9. **Central banks:** monetary policy, liquidity, interest rates.
10. **Rating agencies:** S&P, Moody's, Fitch rate creditworthiness.

**Participants in the Nigerian stock market (10):** investors (retail, mutual funds, pension funds); issuers; **stockbrokers** (licensed agents); **NGX** (platform for listing and trading); **SEC** (regulator); **market makers** (keep liquidity, stabilise prices); **investment banks** (issuing houses: help issue securities, advise); fund managers; foreign investors; clearing and settlement institutions (**CSCS**, Central Securities Clearing System).

| Body | Role |
|---|---|
| **CBN** | Apex regulator of the **money market** and banks; monetary policy; issues T-bills |
| **SEC** | Apex regulator of the **capital market** |
| **NGX** | Exchange where securities are listed and traded |
| **NDIC** | Insures bank deposits |
| **NAICOM** | Regulates insurance |
| **PenCom** | Regulates pensions |
| **Deposit money banks** | Take deposits, lend, run overdrafts |

**Explain more**

- Traps: CBN vs SEC is the most swapped pair. **Money market → CBN; capital market → SEC.** The NGX is a market, not the apex regulator; issuing houses help companies raise capital.
- Insurance companies and pension funds are major **institutional investors**: they collect premiums and contributions and invest them in bonds, shares and T-bills.
- Factories, shops and workers belong to goods and labour markets; **investors** are the financial market participants.

### Lesson 6.5: Sources of finance and financing business

**Must know**

- Classify by **how long** the funds are kept: **short-term** (within one year), **medium-term** (about one to five years), **long-term** (beyond five years, or permanent).
- **Internal source:** **retained earnings** (net income reinvested in the business instead of being paid as dividends). Everything else here is **external**.
- **Equity finance** = **ownership capital** (from owners: shares, retained earnings). **Debt finance** = loan capital (borrowed: loans, debentures, bonds).
- **Matching principle:** finance short-term needs (working capital) with short-term sources and long-term assets with long-term sources.

| Term | Source | Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|---|---|
| Short | **Trade credit:** buy now, pay suppliers later | Easy, usually no interest | Lose cash discounts; supplier goodwill at risk |
| Short | **Bank overdraft:** draw beyond the current account balance up to a limit | Flexible; interest only on amount used | Repayable on demand; high interest |
| Short | **Factoring:** sell receivables to a factor for immediate cash | Quick cash; factor collects debts | Fees; customers may see it as weakness |
| Short | **Commercial paper** | Cheaper than bank loans; unsecured | Only for blue-chip firms |
| Short | **Bill finance:** discounting bills of exchange, bankers' acceptances | Cash before the bill matures | Discount cost |
| Medium | **Term loan:** fixed amount, fixed period, scheduled repayment | Certain, planned; interest is tax-deductible | Interest must be paid; often secured |
| Medium | **Hire purchase:** pay by instalments; ownership passes after the **last** instalment | Use the asset at once | Costly; asset repossessed if you default |
| Medium | **Leasing:** pay rentals to use an asset owned by the **lessor** | No large outlay; rentals tax-deductible | No ownership; total cost high |
| Long | **Ordinary shares** | No obligation to repay; no fixed dividend | **Dilutes control**; dividends not tax-deductible; issue costs |
| Long | **Preference shares:** fixed dividend, priority over ordinary shares | No vote, so no loss of control | Fixed dividend, not tax-deductible |
| Long | **Debentures** | No loss of control; interest tax-deductible | Fixed interest and repayment; raises gearing |
| Long | **Retained earnings** | No issue cost, no dilution, no interest | Limited by profit; shareholders forgo dividends |

**Equity vs debt:**

| Point | Equity | Debt |
|---|---|---|
| Holder is | Owner | Creditor (lender) |
| Return | Dividend (not compulsory) | Interest (legal obligation) |
| Repayment | None | At maturity |
| Tax | Dividends not deductible | Interest **tax-deductible** |
| Control | Dilutes control | No dilution |

**Factors in choosing a source:** cost, period (purpose) of need, control, risk and gearing, security available, flexibility, size of the firm.

**Explain more**

- **Factoring example.** A firm has ₦2,000,000 of receivables. The factor advances 80% at once and charges a 2% fee. Advance = 0.80 × 2,000,000 = **₦1,600,000**. Fee = 0.02 × 2,000,000 = **₦40,000**. The firm gets cash today instead of waiting for customers.
- **Leasing** is medium- to long-term, not short-term: it is the odd one out among factoring, bill finance and trade credit.
- **Bonds** and debentures are long-term; a **bank loan** is usually short- or medium-term. Where "debenture" is an option for long-term finance, it is the safe choice.
- Advantage of equity: **no obligation to repay**. Tax-deductible interest is an advantage of **debt**; diluting control is a **disadvantage** of equity.
- **Equity security** = **ordinary share**. Preference shares and convertibles are hybrids; debenture stock is pure debt.
- **Hire purchase vs leasing:** under HP you eventually **own** the asset; under a lease the lessor keeps ownership.

### One-minute recap

- Financial system = institutions, instruments, rules and regulations moving funds from surplus to deficit units.
- Financial markets: capital formation, liquidity, price discovery, risk management, savings, growth, lower transaction costs.
- Money market: one year or less (T-bills, CP, CDs, BAs, repos); capital market: over one year (shares, bonds, debentures).
- Primary = new issues, money to the issuer; secondary = existing securities between investors.
- CBN = apex money market regulator; SEC = apex capital market regulator; NGX = the exchange.
- Financial intermediary links lenders and borrowers; mutual fund = collective investment pool.
- Short-term: trade credit, overdraft, factoring, CP, bill finance. Medium: term loans, HP, leasing. Long: ordinary and preference shares, debentures, retained earnings.
- Equity = ownership capital, never repaid; debt interest is tax-deductible.

### Theory practice

**Question:** A supplier invoices ₦500,000 on terms of 2% discount if paid within 10 days, otherwise full payment in 30 days. What is the approximate annual cost of using the trade credit (giving up the discount)?

*Solution:*
- Pay on day 10: 500,000 × 0.98 = ₦490,000. Pay on day 30: ₦500,000.
- Giving up the discount costs ₦10,000 for the use of ₦490,000 for 20 extra days.
- Annual cost = (10,000 / 490,000) × (365 / 20) = **37.24%** (to 2 d.p.). Trade credit is "free" only within the discount period; after that it can be dearer than a bank overdraft.
