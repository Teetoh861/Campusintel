## Topic 7: Forms of Business Firm

**What you must know:** the sole proprietorship, partnership, limited liability company (private and public), cooperative and public corporation: the definition, types, advantages, disadvantages and sources of funds of each; limited liability and the corporate form; how to choose a form of business.

### Lesson 7.1: Sole proprietorship

**Must know**

- **Sole proprietor:** someone who owns an **unincorporated** business by himself or herself. A sole proprietorship is the type of business organisation **owned, managed and controlled by a single owner**. "Sole" means **only**; "proprietor" means **owner**. Also called a **sole trader**.
- **Key features:** one owner; **unlimited liability**; **no separate legal personality**; few formalities; owner takes all profit and bears all loss; profit taxed as the owner's **personal income**.

**5 types of sole proprietorship:**

1. Service-based
2. Retail-based
3. Technology
4. Manufacturing-based
5. Consulting-based

**8 advantages:**

1. Complete control and greater flexibility
2. Easy set-up
3. Low registration and start-up costs
4. Lower accounting fees
5. Greater privacy
6. No sharing of profits (although the owner also carries any debt alone)
7. Less paperwork
8. Simplified taxes

**7 disadvantages:**

1. Unlimited liability
2. Potential credibility issues
3. Sole responsibility
4. Fewer tax planning opportunities
5. Barriers to finance
6. Sale limitations
7. Limited expertise

**Sources of funds:** owner's personal savings; loans from family and friends; bank loans and overdrafts; microfinance bank and cooperative loans; trade (suppliers') credit; hire purchase; equipment leasing; ploughed-back profits. **Not** issue of shares or debentures.

**Explain more**

- **Unlimited liability** means the owner's personal property (house, car, savings) can be taken to pay business debts. If Bisi's provision shop owes ₦8,000,000 and the shop's assets fetch only ₦5,000,000, creditors can recover the shortfall of 8,000,000 − 5,000,000 = **₦3,000,000** from Bisi personally.
- **Easy to form because it has few legal hurdles.** In Nigeria the owner usually just registers a **business name** with the **Corporate Affairs Commission (CAC)** and starts trading. No state charter, co-owners or shares are needed.
- **It ends when the owner dies or shuts it down.** Having no separate legal existence, it has no perpetual succession. Rapid growth does not end it; selling shares to the public or admitting partners would turn it into a different form.
- **Total owner control** is the classic advantage; **unlimited liability** is the classic disadvantage and the classic characteristic. Limited liability, legal personality and separate taxation belong to companies, not to sole traders.
- "Sale limitations": the business is hard to sell, as its value is tied to the owner. "Credibility issues": banks and big customers may trust a one-person firm less.

### Lesson 7.2: Partnership

**Must know**

- **Partnership:** a legal arrangement that allows **two or more people** to share responsibility for a business. Partners share the ownership and profits, and also the work, responsibility and potential losses.
- **Legal definition:** the relation between persons who have agreed to share the profits of a business carried on by all or any of them acting for all.
- The law does **not** require a written partnership agreement to form a partnership (it may be oral or implied by conduct). An ordinary partnership usually has **2 to 20 partners**; professional firms such as lawyers and accountants may be larger.
- **Partnership agreement (deed):** sets out **how profits and losses are shared**, capital contributions, interest on capital and drawings, partners' salaries, rights and duties, admission and retirement of partners, and settling disputes.
- **Where there is no agreement**, the usual rules are: profits and losses are shared **equally**; no interest on capital; no salary for any partner; interest at 5% a year on a partner's loan to the firm (over and above agreed capital).

**4 types of partnerships (business alliances):**

1. Strategic alliances between non-competitors
2. Strategic partnerships between competitors (called **coopetition**)
3. Joint ventures to develop new business
4. Buyer-supplier relationships to assure reliable supplies

**8 types of partners:**

1. **Active or managing partner:** takes part in the firm's management.
2. **Inactive or sleeping (dormant) partner:** contributes capital and shares profit but does not manage.
3. **Nominal partner:** lends only his name, yet is liable to outsiders.
4. **Partner by estoppel or holding out:** lets himself be presented as a partner, so is liable to those who relied on it.
5. **Partner in profits only:** shares profits but not losses.
6. **Minor as a partner:** admitted to the benefits only; not personally liable for losses.
7. **Secret partner:** active, but hidden from the public.
8. **Sub-partner:** an outsider sharing one partner's profit share; no rights against the firm.

**7 advantages:**

1. Bridging the gap in expertise and knowledge
2. Additional capital
3. Cost savings
4. More business opportunities
5. Responsibilities can be shared
6. Emotional support
7. New perspective

**8 disadvantages:**

1. Potential liabilities
2. Loss of autonomy
3. Emotional issues
4. Conflict and disagreements
5. Future selling complications
6. Lack of stability
7. Higher taxes
8. Splitting profits

**Sources of funds:** partners' capital contributions; admitting a new partner; partners' loans; ploughed-back profits; bank loans and overdrafts; suppliers' credit; equipment leasing; hire purchase. **Not** issue of shares.

**Explain more**

- **Unlimited personal liability** ("potential liabilities") is the main disadvantage of an ordinary partnership: each partner is liable for all the firm's debts, even those caused by another partner.
- **Pooled skills and resources** is the main advantage.
- **Decision-making is distributed (shared) among partners.** Control by one person describes a sole proprietor; voting by equity holders describes a company.
- **Lack of stability:** death, retirement or bankruptcy of a partner dissolves an ordinary partnership unless the agreement says otherwise.
- **Trap:** a partnership **cannot issue shares**, because only companies have share capital. Suppliers' credit, leasing and hire purchase are all open to it.
- **CAMA 2020** (Companies and Allied Matters Act 2020) also allows a **Limited Liability Partnership (LLP)** and a **Limited Partnership (LP)**, registered with the CAC. In an LP at least one general partner keeps unlimited liability.

**Worked example (sharing profit):** Ade (capital ₦6,000,000) and Ngozi (capital ₦4,000,000) make a profit of ₦3,000,000.

- (a) Agreement: share in capital ratio 6:4 = 3:2. Ade = 3/5 × 3,000,000 = **₦1,800,000**; Ngozi = 2/5 × 3,000,000 = **₦1,200,000**.
- (b) Agreement: 10% interest on capital, Ngozi gets a salary of ₦400,000, remainder shared equally. Interest: Ade ₦600,000, Ngozi ₦400,000. Remainder = 3,000,000 − 600,000 − 400,000 − 400,000 = ₦1,600,000, so ₦800,000 each. Ade = **₦1,400,000**; Ngozi = 400,000 + 400,000 + 800,000 = **₦1,600,000** (total ₦3,000,000).
- (c) No agreement: equal shares, no interest or salary: **₦1,500,000 each**.

### Lesson 7.3: The limited liability company (private and public)

**Must know**

- **Limited company:** a general form of incorporation that **limits the amount of liability** undertaken by the company's shareholders. It is a legal structure that ensures the liability of members or subscribers is **limited to their stake** in the company (their investments or commitments).
- **Limited liability company (LLC):** a business structure where owners are **not held personally liable** for the debts or other liabilities the business may incur.
- **Corporation / company:** a **distinct (separate) legal entity** from its owners; it can own property, sue and be sued in its own name. It is **owned by shareholders**, each holding a stake.
- **Features of the corporate form:** separate legal entity; limited liability; **perpetual succession** (it continues when owners die or sell); transferable shares; separation of ownership (shareholders) from control (directors); raises capital by **issuing equity shares** and debentures.

**2 types (by number of members):**

1. **Single-member LLC:** operates like a sole proprietorship but with the protection of the law on the liability of the business.
2. **Multi-member LLC:** two or more members form and own the business.

**LLC vs other forms:**

| Point | Enterprise / sole proprietor | Company (LLC) |
|---|---|---|
| Legal entity | Not separate from owner | Separate legal entity |
| Liability | Owner personally liable for all debts | Members not personally liable (limited) |
| Owners | Always one person (sole proprietor) | One or more members |

**6 advantages of LLC:**

1. Limited personal liability for the company's business debts.
2. Fairly easy to organise and get running.
3. Many business expenses can be written off as deductions, lowering taxable income.
4. Can be run by its members, or members can hire a manager for day-to-day affairs; members choose how involved to be.
5. The LLC designation lends greater credibility to a sole proprietorship or partnership.
6. Less record-keeping than corporations, so more focus on business goals.

**4 disadvantages of LLC:**

1. Depending on state law, it may have to be dissolved on the death or bankruptcy of a member (a corporation can exist in perpetuity).
2. Under pass-through taxation, members pay self-employment taxes on top of personal income tax.
3. Unless the Operating Agreement is properly conceived, executed and understood, roles and responsibilities may be unclear.
4. Penalties, stated in the Operating Agreement, for failing to make capital contributions.

**Private vs public company:**

| Point | Private company (Ltd) | Public company (Plc) |
|---|---|---|
| Name ends in | "Limited" (Ltd) | "Public Limited Company" (Plc) |
| Members | One person upwards; at most 50 (excluding employees) | No maximum |
| Offer of shares | Cannot invite the public to buy its shares | Can offer shares to the public |
| Share transfer | Restricted by its articles | Freely transferable; may be listed on the NGX |
| Regulation | Less | Heavier: SEC rules, published accounts |

**Explain more**

- Limited liability is the **advantage** that exam options call "limited owner liability" or "limited liability for investors": a shareholder loses at most what he paid, or still owes, for his shares. If Bisi's shop (Lesson 7.1) had been Bisi Stores Ltd, the ₦3,000,000 shortfall would fall on creditors, not on Bisi's house.
- **Double taxation** is the classic disadvantage of a corporation: profit is taxed once as company income tax and again when dividends are taxed in shareholders' hands. **Worked example (illustrative rates):** profit ₦10,000,000; company tax at 30% = ₦3,000,000; profit after tax ₦7,000,000 paid as dividend; withholding tax at 10% = ₦700,000; shareholders receive ₦6,300,000. Total tax = 3,000,000 + 700,000 = ₦3,700,000, which is **37%** of the original profit.
- Other corporate disadvantages: complex, costly formation; heavy regulation; agency conflict.
- **Nigeria:** companies are formed under **CAMA 2020** by registering with the **CAC**. CAMA 2020 lets **one person** form a private company. Companies may be limited by shares, limited by guarantee (often non-profit bodies) or unlimited.
- The LLC disadvantages use United States terms ("state law", "Operating Agreement"); learn them as listed. The Nigerian counterpart of the Operating Agreement is the articles of association.
- **Trap:** only **public** companies can trade shares on a public market. "Public market trading" is not a feature of every LLC.

### Lesson 7.4: Public corporations and cooperatives

**Must know**

- **Public corporation:** a corporation **owned and operated by a government**, established for the administration of certain public programmes. It is set up by an **act of parliament** (in Nigeria, the National Assembly or a state House of Assembly), owned and managed by government to provide **essential services to the public at affordable cost**. Also called a public enterprise or statutory corporation.

**2 types of public corporation:**

1. Those providing **essential services**: water corporations, the National Electric Power Authority (NEPA), Federal Housing Authority.
2. Those of a **commercial nature**: state-owned banks, hotels, textile mills, transport services.

**Public corporation vs public company:** a **public corporation** is owned and controlled by **government** and provides essential services. A **public limited company** is a **privately owned** business that **offers shares to the public**.

**8 examples of public enterprises in Nigeria:** Nigerian Railway Corporation (NRC); Central Bank of Nigeria (CBN); water corporations across the country; Nigerian Postal Service; Defence Industries Corporation of Nigeria; National Universities Commission (NUC); NAFDAC; NDLEA.

**4 advantages:**

1. Flexibility and independence retained
2. Governmental review promotes public interest
3. Recruitment power
4. Debt shared by more investors

**4 disadvantages:**

1. Complex legal requirements
2. Increased governmental oversight
3. Many company records are public
4. Ownership and management are split

- **Cooperative society:** a business **owned and controlled by its members**, formed voluntarily to **serve the needs of its member-owners**, not to maximise profit for outside shareholders.
- **Principles:** voluntary and open membership; **democratic control (one member, one vote**, however many shares held); members contribute capital and share any surplus; autonomy; education and training of members; cooperation among cooperatives; concern for the community.
- **Types:** consumer (retail); producer and agricultural; marketing; **credit and thrift** (savings and loans); housing; multipurpose.
- **Sources of funds:** members' shares, entrance fees and savings; retained surplus; loans; grants.

**Explain more**

- A public corporation keeps flexibility because it is a **separate legal body** with its own board, while government review keeps it serving the public. "Ownership and management split": government owns it, a board runs it.
- **Sources of funds for public corporations:** government grants, subventions and budget allocations; government loans; internally generated revenue (fees and charges); bonds; local and foreign loans, often guaranteed by government.
- **Trap:** "public" in **public corporation** means government-owned; "public" in **public limited company** means its shares are offered to the public. Dangote Cement Plc is a public company, not a public corporation.
- Many public enterprises have since been privatised; NEPA was unbundled into separate electricity companies.
- **Cooperative surplus** is usually returned as a **patronage dividend**, in proportion to each member's dealings with the society, and interest on members' capital is limited. **Worked example:** a consumer cooperative earns a surplus of ₦1,200,000; members bought goods worth ₦24,000,000 from it. Rate = 1,200,000 / 24,000,000 = **5 kobo per naira (5%)**. Mrs Okon bought ₦600,000, so she receives 600,000 × 0.05 = **₦30,000**.
- A staff cooperative that takes monthly savings and gives members cheap loans is a credit and thrift cooperative. Liability is **not** what defines a cooperative; serving its members is.

### Lesson 7.5: Choosing a form of business

**Must know**

**Factors to consider in choosing a form:**

1. **Capital required** and ease of raising it (shares are open only to companies; a public offer only to a Plc)
2. **Liability** of owners (limited or unlimited)
3. **Control** and management (how much the owner wants to keep)
4. **Ease and cost of formation** and legal formalities
5. **Continuity** (perpetual succession or ending with the owner)
6. **Taxation** (personal income tax, or company tax plus tax on dividends)
7. **Nature and size** of the business and its risk
8. **Profit sharing** (kept by one owner or split)
9. **Privacy** (how much must be disclosed publicly)
10. **Government regulation**
11. **Transferability** of ownership interest

**Comparison:**

| Point | Sole proprietor | Partnership | Company | Cooperative | Public corporation |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Owners | One | 2 or more partners | Shareholders | Members | Government |
| Liability | Unlimited | Unlimited (ordinary) | Limited | Usually limited | Government bears |
| Legal entity | No | No (ordinary) | Yes | Yes, once registered | Yes |
| Continuity | Ends with owner | Unstable | Perpetual | Continues | Perpetual |
| Main aim | Profit | Profit | Profit | Serve members | Public service |
| Raises funds by | Savings, loans | Partners' capital, loans | Shares, debentures, loans | Members' shares, savings | Government funds, bonds |

**Explain more**

- Match form to need: a small barbing salon suits a sole proprietorship; professionals sharing skills, a partnership; a manufacturer needing billions of naira, a public company selling shares on the NGX; market women wanting cheap loans, a cooperative; essential services, a public corporation.
- **Trap:** the forms of business ownership are sole proprietorship, partnership, company (corporation), cooperative and public enterprise. Financial contracts such as an **interest rate swap** (exchanging interest payments), a bond or a lease are **not** forms of business ownership.

### One-minute recap

- Sole proprietor: one owner, unlimited liability, total control, easy to form, ends when the owner dies or closes it.
- Partnership: 2 or more share profits and losses; no written agreement needed; no agreement = share equally; pooled skills but unlimited liability; cannot issue shares.
- 8 partners: active, sleeping, nominal, estoppel, profits only, minor, secret, sub-partner.
- Company: separate legal entity owned by shareholders, limited liability, perpetual succession, raises capital by issuing shares; double taxation is its classic drawback.
- Private (Ltd) cannot offer shares to the public; public (Plc) can and may list on the NGX. In Nigeria, CAMA 2020 and the CAC.
- Public corporation: government-owned by statute for essential services; two types, essential and commercial; not the same as a public company.
- Cooperative: owned and controlled by members to serve members; one member, one vote; surplus shared by patronage.
- Choose by capital, liability, control, formation cost, continuity, tax, size, privacy, regulation.

### Theory practice

**Question:** Tunde and Amaka are partners with capitals of ₦5,000,000 and ₦3,000,000. Profit for the year is ₦2,400,000. (a) The deed allows 5% interest on capital and shares the remainder 3:1. Find each partner's share. (b) What would each get if there were no partnership agreement?

*Solution:*
- (a) Interest: Tunde 5% × 5,000,000 = ₦250,000; Amaka 5% × 3,000,000 = ₦150,000. Remainder = 2,400,000 − 250,000 − 150,000 = ₦2,000,000. Tunde 3/4 × 2,000,000 = ₦1,500,000; Amaka 1/4 × 2,000,000 = ₦500,000. Totals: Tunde = **₦1,750,000**; Amaka = **₦650,000** (check: ₦2,400,000).
- (b) No agreement: no interest on capital, profit shared equally: 2,400,000 / 2 = **₦1,200,000 each**.
