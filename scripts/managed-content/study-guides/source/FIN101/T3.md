## Topic 3: Analysis of Risk in Financial Decisions

**What you must know:** risk vs uncertainty; the types of risk; the probability method (expected value, variance, standard deviation, coefficient of variation) and the non-probability methods; choosing between securities for a risk-averse or risk-taking investor; the risk-return trade-off; diversification and risk management.

### Lesson 3.1: Risk, uncertainty and the risk-return trade-off

**Must know**

- **Risk:** the situation in which the future outcome is unknown but the likelihood of its occurrence can be estimated or determined. In other words, the **probability** of future occurrence **can** be estimated.
- **Uncertainty:** the situation in which the future outcome is unknown and the likelihood of its occurrence **cannot** be estimated or determined. The probability **cannot** be estimated.
- **Risk in finance:** uncertainty in expected returns; the chance that the actual return differs from the expected return.
- **Risk-return trade-off:** higher risk offers higher potential reward; investors take on more risk only if they expect a higher return. A financial manager must balance **risk exposure and potential returns**.
- **Risk premium:** the extra return investors demand, above the risk-free rate, for accepting risk.

| | Risk | Uncertainty |
|---|---|---|
| Outcome known? | No | No |
| Probability can be estimated? | **Yes** | **No** |
| Can be measured with EV, SD, CV? | Yes | No |

**Explain more**

- The difference is only about the **probability**. In both cases you do not know the outcome. If an option says "the outcome is known", it describes certainty, not risk.
- A Federal Government of Nigeria treasury bill issued through the CBN is treated as almost risk-free. A share on the NGX is risky, so investors expect a higher return on it. If treasury bills pay 18% and an investor wants 25% from a share, the risk premium is 25% − 18% = 7%.
- Traps: "greater risk yields lower gains" reverses the trade-off; "minimal risk ensures big returns" is false. Risk and return are **connected**, not disconnected.

### Lesson 3.2: Types of risk

**Must know**

1. **Business risk:** concerned with the future uncertainty about a company's operating prospects (sales, costs, profits), even with no debt.
2. **Financial risk:** concerned with the introduction of **debt** into a company's capital structure.
3. **Economic risk:** the risk an organisation faces due to shifts in macroeconomic variables or forces.
4. **Interest rate risk:** the exposure of a bank's current or future earnings and capital to adverse changes in market rates. It mainly affects **fixed-income assets** (bonds): when rates rise, bond prices fall.
5. **Market risk:** the potential for losses arising from factors influencing the overall performance of financial markets. Also called **systematic risk**.
6. **Liquidity risk:** the risk of loss from the inability to meet payment obligations in full and on time when they become due; also, difficulty turning assets into cash quickly without losing value.

Other types you must also know:

- **Credit (default) risk:** a borrower fails to repay a loan or interest.
- **Inflation (purchasing power) risk:** inflation reduces the purchasing power of returns.
- **Exchange-rate risk:** losses from changes in exchange rates on foreign dealings.
- **Operational risk:** loss from internal failures (human error, technology breakdown).
- **Reinvestment risk:** cash flows must be reinvested at a lower rate.
- **Systemic risk:** collapse of an entire market or financial system.

| | Systematic (market) risk | Unsystematic risk |
|---|---|---|
| Affects | All firms, the whole market | One firm or one sector |
| Causes | Recession, inflation, interest rate changes | Poor leadership, strike, defective product |
| Removed by diversification? | **No** (non-diversifiable) | **Yes** (diversifiable) |

**Explain more**

- Business risk vs financial risk: a debt-free firm still has business risk. Once it borrows, the fixed interest must be paid whatever happens, which adds financial risk.
- A market-wide economic downturn, a CBN rise in the monetary policy rate or high inflation hits nearly every firm: systematic. A strike at one company is unsystematic.
- Naira depreciation hurts a Lagos importer paying suppliers in dollars: exchange-rate risk.
- Do not confuse **systematic** (market-wide) with **systemic** (the whole system collapsing). Market risk is never "diversifiable" or "firm-specific".

### Lesson 3.3: Measuring risk: the probability method

**Must know**

**2 ways of treating risk:** (1) probability method, (2) non-probability method.

**Probability method (4 measures):**

1. Expected return (mean, expected value)
2. Variance
3. Standard deviation
4. Coefficient of variation

**Formulas** (P = probability, R = return or cash flow):

- Expected return: ER = Σ (P × R)
- Variance = Σ P × (R − ER)^2
- Standard deviation (SD) = square root of variance
- Coefficient of variation (CV) = SD / ER × 100 (in %)
- Check: the probabilities must add up to 1. The returns with their probabilities form a **probability distribution**.

**Decision rules:**

- The higher the variance, SD and CV, the higher the risk, and vice versa.
- **Risk-averse** investor: selects the security with the **lowest** risk (variance, SD or CV).
- **Risk-taker (risk-seeking)** investor: selects the security with the **highest** risk.
- **Risk-neutral** investor: ignores risk and looks only at expected return.

**Explain more**

**Worked example.** James is considering two securities.

Security A: ₦4,000 (0.4), ₦5,000 (0.5), ₦6,000 (0.1).
Security B: ₦6,000 (0.7), ₦4,000 (0.2), ₦3,000 (0.1).

*Step 1: P × R, summed, gives ER. Step 2: subtract ER from each return. Step 3: square and multiply by P, summed, gives the variance.*

**Security A**

| Return (R) | P | P × R | R − ER | (R − ER)^2 | P × (R − ER)^2 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 4,000 | 0.4 | 1,600 | −700 | 490,000 | 196,000 |
| 5,000 | 0.5 | 2,500 | 300 | 90,000 | 45,000 |
| 6,000 | 0.1 | 600 | 1,300 | 1,690,000 | 169,000 |
| Total | 1.0 | **ER = 4,700** | | | **Variance = 410,000** |

- SD = square root of 410,000 = ₦640.31 (₦640 to the nearest naira)
- CV = 640.31 / 4,700 × 100 = 13.62%, about **14%**

**Security B**

| Return (R) | P | P × R | R − ER | (R − ER)^2 | P × (R − ER)^2 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 6,000 | 0.7 | 4,200 | 700 | 490,000 | 343,000 |
| 4,000 | 0.2 | 800 | −1,300 | 1,690,000 | 338,000 |
| 3,000 | 0.1 | 300 | −2,300 | 5,290,000 | 529,000 |
| Total | 1.0 | **ER = 5,300** | | | **Variance = 1,210,000** |

- SD = square root of 1,210,000 = ₦1,100
- CV = 1,100 / 5,300 × 100 = 20.75%, about **21%**

**Recommendation:** James is risk averse, so recommend **Security A**: its variance, SD and CV are all lower than B's (lower risk).

**Shortcut check for the variance:** Variance = Σ (P × R^2) − ER^2. For A: 0.4(16,000,000) + 0.5(25,000,000) + 0.1(36,000,000) = 22,500,000; 22,500,000 − 4,700^2 = 22,500,000 − 22,090,000 = 410,000. Same answer.

**Traps:**

- Variance is in squared units and is huge (e.g. 44,000,000); SD is its square root (e.g. 6,633.25). Options often place both side by side. Read which one is asked.
- Options also list the separate terms of a sum (e.g. 15,000, 18,000, 28,000) as distractors. Always add the whole column.
- "Nearest whole percent": **round**, do not cut off. 13.7% becomes 14%, not 13%. Keep SD to 2 decimal places before dividing.
- "Expected value", "expected return", "mean" and "expected cash flow" all mean ER.

### Lesson 3.4: Comparing securities and the coefficient of variation

**Must know**

- **CV = risk per naira of expected return.** When expected returns differ, the CV is the fairest comparison.
- If SD and CV point to different securities, a risk-averse investor goes by the **lowest CV**.
- Reversing the order of the probabilities on equally spaced returns changes ER and CV but leaves variance and SD **unchanged**.

**Explain more**

**Worked example 1: a risk taker.** Willy is a risk taker.

Security X: ₦5,000 (0.1), ₦6,000 (0.3), ₦7,000 (0.6).
Security Y: ₦8,000 (0.2), ₦7,000 (0.4), ₦6,000 (0.4).

| Security X | P | P × R | R − ER | P × (R − ER)^2 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 5,000 | 0.1 | 500 | −1,500 | 225,000 |
| 6,000 | 0.3 | 1,800 | −500 | 75,000 |
| 7,000 | 0.6 | 4,200 | 500 | 150,000 |
| Total | | **6,500** | | **450,000** |

| Security Y | P | P × R | R − ER | P × (R − ER)^2 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 8,000 | 0.2 | 1,600 | 1,200 | 288,000 |
| 7,000 | 0.4 | 2,800 | 200 | 16,000 |
| 6,000 | 0.4 | 2,400 | −800 | 256,000 |
| Total | | **6,800** | | **560,000** |

| | ER | Variance | SD | CV |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| X | ₦6,500 | 450,000 | ₦670.82 | 10.32% (about 10%) |
| Y | ₦6,800 | 560,000 | ₦748.33 | 11.01% (about 11%) |

Willy is a risk taker, so recommend **Security Y**: its variance, SD and CV are the highest.

**Worked example 2: SD and CV disagree.** Security P has ER ₦10,000 and SD ₦1,500. Security Q has ER ₦40,000 and SD ₦4,000.

- CV of P = 1,500 / 10,000 × 100 = 15%
- CV of Q = 4,000 / 40,000 × 100 = 10%

Q has the larger SD but carries less risk per naira, so a risk-averse investor chooses **Q** (lowest CV). The same thing happens with three or four securities: compute the CV of each and pick the lowest. A four-outcome table is worked exactly like a three-outcome table, with one more row.

**Worked example 3: reversed probabilities.** Returns ₦10,000, ₦20,000, ₦30,000.

| Probabilities | ER | Variance | SD | CV |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0.2, 0.3, 0.5 | ₦23,000 | 61,000,000 | ₦7,810.25 | 33.96% (about 34%) |
| 0.5, 0.3, 0.2 | ₦17,000 | 61,000,000 | ₦7,810.25 | 45.94% (about 46%) |

The spread is a mirror image, so variance and SD are identical, but ER moves, so CV changes. Do not reuse a CV from one version for the other.

### Lesson 3.5: Non-probability methods, diversification and risk management

**Must know**

**Non-probability methods (5):**

1. Sensitivity analysis
2. Payback period
3. Risk-adjusted method (risk-adjusted discount rate)
4. Certainty equivalent factor
5. Simulation

- **Portfolio:** a combination (collection) of financial investments, such as shares and bonds, held by one investor.
- **Diversification:** spreading investments across different assets or industries to reduce risk. It reduces **unsystematic** risk only.
- **Risk management:** identifying, assessing and reducing (or controlling) risks. It never eliminates all uncertainty.

**6 steps in risk analysis:**

1. Identify risks (brainstorming, expert consultation, checklists)
2. Measure risks (sensitivity analysis, scenario analysis, Monte Carlo simulation)
3. Assess risk tolerance
4. Evaluate the risk-return trade-off (metrics such as risk-adjusted return, e.g. the Sharpe ratio, plus NPV and IRR)
5. Develop risk mitigation strategies
6. Monitor and review risks

**Mitigation strategies:** **hedging** (with derivatives such as options, futures, forwards), **diversification**, **insurance** (transferring risk to an insurer). Risk can be reduced, transferred or managed.

**Other tools:** ratio analysis (debt-to-equity, interest cover), Value at Risk (VaR), scenario and sensitivity analysis, risk mapping (heat maps), Monte Carlo simulation, stress testing.

**Explain more**

- **Sensitivity analysis** tests how changes in one key variable (say, selling price) affect the outcome; **scenario analysis** changes several at once (best, likely, worst case); **simulation** (Monte Carlo) uses computer models and probability distributions to generate many possible outcomes.
- **Payback period** treats risk by preferring projects that return their cost faster. **Certainty equivalent** converts risky cash flows into the certain amounts the investor would accept instead. **Risk-adjusted discount rate** adds a risk premium to the discount rate for riskier projects.
- **VaR** measures the possible loss in value of an investment over a period under normal conditions. **Stress testing** simulates extreme conditions. A **heat map** plots risks by probability and impact.
- Diversification in practice: an investor holding only one bank's shares on the NGX loses heavily if that bank has a fraud scandal. Holding bank, telecom, consumer goods and oil shares plus treasury bills means one loss can be offset by gains elsewhere. A recession still hurts all of them: systematic risk remains.
- Traps: concentrating funds in one asset, raising debt (leverage) and narrowing the investment scope **increase** risk. Avoiding all investments is not risk management; it gives up return.

### One-minute recap

- Risk: outcome unknown, probability **can** be estimated. Uncertainty: probability **cannot** be estimated.
- Business risk = operating prospects; financial risk = debt in the capital structure; market risk = systematic risk.
- Probability method: ER = Σ(P × R); variance = Σ P(R − ER)^2; SD = square root of variance; CV = SD / ER × 100.
- Higher variance, SD, CV = higher risk. Risk averse picks the lowest; risk taker picks the highest; use CV when expected returns differ.
- Round CV to the nearest percent; never mix up variance (huge) and SD (its square root).
- Non-probability methods: sensitivity analysis, payback period, risk-adjusted method, certainty equivalent, simulation.
- Higher risk, higher potential return; the risk premium is the extra return for bearing risk.
- Diversification removes unsystematic risk only; hedging, diversification and insurance manage risk.

### Theory practice

**Question:** A project's cash flows and probabilities are ₦10,000 (0.2), ₦15,000 (0.3), ₦20,000 (0.4) and ₦30,000 (0.1). Calculate the expected value, variance, standard deviation and coefficient of variation.

*Solution:*

| R | P | P × R | R − ER | (R − ER)^2 | P × (R − ER)^2 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 10,000 | 0.2 | 2,000 | −7,500 | 56,250,000 | 11,250,000 |
| 15,000 | 0.3 | 4,500 | −2,500 | 6,250,000 | 1,875,000 |
| 20,000 | 0.4 | 8,000 | 2,500 | 6,250,000 | 2,500,000 |
| 30,000 | 0.1 | 3,000 | 12,500 | 156,250,000 | 15,625,000 |
| Total | 1.0 | **17,500** | | | **31,250,000** |

- Expected value = ₦17,500
- Variance = 31,250,000
- SD = square root of 31,250,000 = ₦5,590.17
- CV = 5,590.17 / 17,500 × 100 = 31.94%, about 32%
