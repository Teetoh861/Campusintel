## Topic 4: Accounting Concepts, Conventions and Limitations

**What you must know:** what concepts and conventions are and why they exist (GAAP); all 16 concepts and conventions with their other names; which concept explains a given treatment; and the limitations of financial accounting.

### Lesson 4.1: Meaning, objectives and GAAP

**Must know**

- **Accounting concepts:** the fundamental assumptions made by accountants in preparing financial statements; the **basic points of agreement** on which financial accounting theory and practice are founded. They are the backbone of financial reporting.
- **Accounting conventions:** generally accepted practices that guide accountants where transactions are complicated or unclear. They are **not legally binding**, but they keep financial statements consistent and comparable.
- **GAAP (Generally Accepted Accounting Principles):** the widely accepted rules and standards used to prepare financial statements so that they report performance fairly and consistently. Concepts and conventions together make up GAAP.
- **Objectives of accounting concepts:**
  1. To keep **uniformity and regularity** in preparing accounting statements.
  2. To act as underlying principles that help accountants prepare and maintain business records.
  3. To set out the rules all business entities must follow, giving **detailed and comparable** financial information.

### Lesson 4.2: Entity, dual aspect, money measurement, cost, objectivity and fairness

**Must know**

- **Business entity:** the business is a separate entity, distinct from its owners or managers, for sole traders, partnerships and companies alike. Only transactions that affect the business are recorded, from the business's point of view.
  - Capital the owner puts in is owed by the business to the owner.
  - Cash or goods the owner takes for personal use are **drawings**, not business expenses. Drawings **reduce** the owner's capital.
  - The owner's **private assets must not appear** in the business balance sheet.
- **Dual aspect (duality):** the basis of double entry. Every transaction has two aspects, a receiver and a giver, and affects two accounts on opposite sides: every debit has a corresponding credit. Hence **Assets = Liabilities + Capital** always holds.
- **Money measurement:** only transactions that can be expressed in money (in Nigeria, the Naira) are recorded. Money is the common denominator for measuring assets and liabilities. **Stable monetary unit:** money is assumed to keep a stable value, so no adjustment is made for inflation (changes in purchasing power).
- **Historical cost:** assets are recorded at the price paid to acquire them, **including acquisition and installation costs**, not at market or selling value. Cost is kept throughout, except for allocating part of it to expense (depreciation) as the asset is used. Its justification is **objectivity**: cost can be traced to a source document.
- **Objectivity:** accounting statements should not be influenced by the personal bias of management. Figures should be backed by vouchers, invoices, cheques and bills.
- **Fairness:** an extension of objectivity. Reports should be prepared so as **not to favour** any group or segment of users or society.
- **Substance over form:** transactions are accounted for and presented according to their financial **substance and reality**, not merely their **legal form** (e.g. a lease contract). Where the legal form differs from the economic substance, the economic substance is used, so that assets and liabilities are accounted for accurately.

**Explain more**

*Entity example.* Ade starts business with ₦100,000 in his business bank account and brings in his own furniture worth ₦25,000. He pays ₦5,600 business expenses from the business account and ₦4,300 for his son's school fees from his **personal** account.

| Business bank account | ₦ |
|---|---|
| Capital introduced | 100,000 |
| Less business expenses | (5,600) |
| **Balance** | **94,400** |

The furniture adds ₦25,000 to assets and capital but never passed through the bank. The school fees are a private transaction and are ignored. (Had Ade paid the fees from the **business** account, it would be drawings of ₦4,300, reducing both bank and capital.)

*Cost example.* A machine is bought for ₦1,000,000, with ₦2,000 transport to the factory and ₦2,000 installation.

| Machine at cost | ₦ |
|---|---|
| Purchase price | 1,000,000 |
| Transport to site | 2,000 |
| Installation | 2,000 |
| **Recorded cost** | **1,004,000** |

- Land bought for ₦5,000,000 stays at ₦5,000,000 in the balance sheet even if it could now sell for ₦8,000,000 (cost). Showing inventory at its **retail (selling) value** breaks the cost concept.
- A talented chief executive is not shown as an asset: no measurable price was paid for the talent (**cost**), and it cannot be measured reliably in money (**money measurement**). Pick whichever of the two is offered.
- Trap: ignoring inflation is the **monetary unit** concept, not cost.
- Trap: owner's private assets in the balance sheet violate **entity**, not materiality or consistency. "Limited liability" and "can sue and be sued" describe companies, not the entity concept for a sole trader.

### Lesson 4.3: Going concern, periodicity, accrual, realisation and matching

**Must know**

- **Going concern:** the business will continue in operation for the foreseeable future (in principle indefinitely, in perpetuity) and will not be liquidated soon; long enough to carry out its objectives and commitments. A business is a going concern if it can earn a reasonable net income and there is no intention or threat to curtail its business significantly. This is why assets are shown at cost less depreciation, not at break-up (forced-sale) value.
- **Periodicity (accounting period):** the business's indefinite life is divided into accounting periods, and the profit and loss account and balance sheet are prepared at regular intervals (one month, three months, six months; usually **one year**). Needed for measuring profit, tax and financial position, and for comparing periods. Limited liability companies must present financial statements to members **annually**. The year may be a **calendar year** (1 January to 31 December) or any other 12-month **financial year** (e.g. 1 April to 31 March).
- **Accrual:** income is recognised when **earned**, not when received; expenses when **incurred**, not when paid. Both are recorded in the period to which they relate. It gives rise to **accrued expenses** (incurred, not yet paid) and **prepayments** (paid, benefit not yet received).
- **Realisation (revenue recognition):** decides **when** revenue is earned: when goods are sold or services rendered, the point at which the customer incurs a liability and a legal right to receive money is created. Receiving an **order** is not realisation; waiting for **cash** is not required.
- **Matching:** revenue for a period is matched with the costs incurred to earn it, whether paid or not. Costs relating to revenue of a later period are carried forward. **Accrual accounting** and **end-of-year adjustments** (accruals, prepayments, depreciation, provisions) rest on matching. **Depreciation** follows the matching concept.

**Explain more**

*Matching example.* A trader buys 50 pairs of shoes for ₦50,000 and sells 35 pairs for ₦70,000.

| | ₦ |
|---|---|
| Sales | 70,000 |
| Cost of goods sold (35/50 × ₦50,000) | 35,000 |
| **Gross profit** | **35,000** |
| Cost of 15 unsold pairs carried forward (closing inventory) | 15,000 |

*Which concept? (quick drill)*

| Situation | Concept |
|---|---|
| ₦600,000 borrowed in December; ₦36,000 interest for 6 months paid at the end; ₦6,000 charged as December expense (₦36,000 ÷ 6) | Matching |
| Property rates of ₦120,000 paid once a year; ₦10,000 charged each month | Matching |
| Machine costing ₦1,200,000 depreciated over 10 years (₦120,000 a year) instead of expensed at once | Matching |
| Goods sold on credit for ₦80,000 in December, paid in January; included in December sales | Realisation (revenue recognition) |
| ₦2,000,000 customer deposit for work starting next year shown as a liability, not income | Realisation (revenue recognition) |
| Plant costing ₦10,000,000 with a 10-year life: ₦1,000,000 expensed in year 1, ₦9,000,000 shown as an asset | Going concern (and matching) |

- Large businesses report revenue when it is **earned**, not when cash is received (cash basis).
- Trap: "revenue recognition" only deals with income. A question about **expenses** related to revenue, paid or not, is **matching**.
- Trap: there is no "adjustment concept"; year-end adjustments satisfy **matching**.

### Lesson 4.4: The conventions: conservatism, consistency, full disclosure, materiality

**Must know**

- **Conservatism (prudence):** be cautious with estimates. Do not recognise income until it is earned (reasonably certain); recognise expenses and losses as soon as they are **reasonably possible (probable)** and make provision for all losses. Where two acceptable values exist, choose the one giving **lower profit, lower assets or higher liabilities**. Purpose: profit and net worth are not **overstated**.
- **Consistency:** once a method is adopted for an item, use it from period to period, and treat like items in the same way. Example: if straight-line depreciation is chosen (rather than reducing balance or sum of the digits), keep using straight line. It allows comparison of the business's statements over time.
- **Full disclosure:** all information that is potentially significant and relevant to users must be disclosed, even if unfavourable to the business, often in **notes (footnotes)** to the accounts.
- **Materiality:** items significant enough to affect users' evaluation or decisions must be disclosed separately; immaterial items may be treated in the simplest way or combined. Materiality is **relative** and needs judgement: what is material to a sole trader may be immaterial to a large firm.
- **Industry practice:** an industry may depart from normal presentation where its special nature makes another approach more useful (e.g. public utilities list plant assets before current assets).

**Explain more**

- **Conservatism in action:** inventory costing ₦400,000 that can now sell for only ₦300,000 is written **down** to ₦300,000. Inventory costing ₦400,000 that could sell for ₦550,000 stays at ₦400,000: never written **up**. Losses are recognised; gains wait until realised.
- **Changing a method (consistency):** allowed only if the new method gives a fairer view, and **users must be informed** of the change and its effect on profit. Frequent changes or changes to manipulate profit break consistency.
- Trap: consistency does **not** mean all companies in the same industry must use the same methods. It applies within one business over time.
- **Materiality in action:** a large company expenses a ₦250,000 camera at once instead of depreciating it; a big company rounds figures to the nearest ₦1,000. Both are acceptable because the amounts would not change any user's decision. Materiality is the convention that **justifies departing** from a strict rule for immaterial amounts.
- Trap: full disclosure asks for **more** information; materiality allows **less** detail for small items.

### Lesson 4.5: Limitations of financial accounting

**Must know**

Financial accounting is like a **post-mortem report**: it shows what has happened but cannot control past events. Its 15 limitations:

1. It records only **quantitative** information.
2. It records only **historical cost**; future uncertainties have no place.
3. It ignores **price level changes** (inflation).
4. It gives information on the **whole concern** only, not by product, process or department.
5. Cost figures are not known in advance, so prices cannot be fixed in advance; it gives no guide to raising or cutting selling price.
6. It has no technique for comparing actual performance with **budgeted** targets, so performance cannot be evaluated.
7. It does not show whether profit is optimum or how to increase it.
8. In a loss, it does not show whether cost control and cost reduction can reduce the loss or turn it into profit.
9. It does not reveal which departments perform well or which make losses, and how much.
10. It does not provide the **cost of products** manufactured.
11. It provides no means to reduce **wastage**.
12. It does not show whether, how and how far expenses can be cut to reduce product cost.
13. It does not help management in **strategic decisions** (replacing assets, new products, dropping a line, expanding capacity).
14. It gives scope for **manipulation** (overvaluation or undervaluation), reducing reliability.
15. It is **technical**: a person not conversant with accounting gets little use from it.

**Explain more**

- In short form, accounting **lacks qualitative analysis**, is **not free from bias** (judgement and possible manipulation), and shows only an **estimated position** (depreciation and provisions are estimates).
- Trap: "it is value free" is **not** a limitation. Accounting is never value free; it depends on judgement.
- Link to concepts: limitations 2 and 3 come from the **historical cost** and **stable monetary unit** concepts; limitation 1 from **money measurement**.

### One-minute recap

- Concepts = basic assumptions; conventions = accepted practices; together = GAAP (Generally Accepted Accounting Principles).
- Entity: business separate from owner; drawings reduce capital; private assets stay out.
- Dual aspect: every debit has a credit; A = L + C.
- Money measurement: only money items; stable monetary unit ignores inflation.
- Historical cost: price paid plus transport and installation; land stays at cost.
- Going concern: business continues; periodicity: life split into periods, usually a year.
- Accrual: earned/incurred, not received/paid. Realisation: revenue when goods sold, not order or cash.
- Matching: costs matched with the revenue they earn; depreciation and year-end adjustments.
- Conventions: conservatism (lower profit/assets), consistency (same method, disclose changes), full disclosure (notes), materiality (small items, judgement).
- Financial accounting is a post-mortem: 15 limitations, mainly quantitative, historical, whole-concern only.

### Theory practice

**Question:** Bola, a sole trader, buys 40 bags of rice for ₦800,000 and sells 30 bags on credit for ₦900,000 in December; the customer pays in January. In December she also takes one bag home for her family. Calculate gross profit for December and name the concept that applies to (i) the credit sale, (ii) the cost charged, (iii) the bag taken home.

*Worked solution:* Cost per bag = ₦800,000 ÷ 40 = ₦20,000. Cost of goods sold = 30 × ₦20,000 = ₦600,000. Gross profit = ₦900,000 − ₦600,000 = **₦300,000**. (i) **Realisation**: revenue is recognised in December when the goods are sold, not in January when cash arrives. (ii) **Matching**: only the cost of the 30 bags sold is set against December's sales. (iii) **Business entity**: the bag (₦20,000 at cost) is drawings, reducing her capital, not a business expense. The 9 bags left (₦180,000) are carried forward as closing inventory.
