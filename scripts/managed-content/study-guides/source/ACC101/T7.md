## Topic 7: End-of-Year Adjustments

**What you must know:** what year-end adjustments are and why they are made; closing stock and drawings; accrued and prepaid expenses; accrued and prepaid (deferred) income; depreciation by straight-line, reducing-balance and sum-of-the-years'-digits methods, and disposal; bad debts, provision for bad and doubtful debts and provision for discount on debtors.

### Lesson 7.1: Meaning, reasons, types; closing stock and drawings

**Must know**

- **End-of-year adjustments:** amendments made in the books of account at the end of each financial period, affecting the profit and loss account (income statement) and the balance sheet (statement of financial position), so that the accounts show a **true view** of the organisation's transactions.
- **Reasons:**
  1. To provide for stock valuation at the year end.
  2. To prepare the accounts in line with generally accepted accounting principles or concepts (accrual, matching, prudence).
  3. To record properly all income earned during the year.
  4. To provide for depreciation of the organisation's fixed (non-current) assets.
  5. To apply the double entry principle properly.
- **7 types of adjustment:**
  1. Closing stock.
  2. Depreciation of assets.
  3. Accrued and prepaid income.
  4. Accrued and prepaid expenses.
  5. Drawings by the owner.
  6. Provision for discounts (on debtors and on creditors).
  7. Bad debts and provision for doubtful debts.
- **Closing stock (inventory):** unsold goods at the year end, valued at the lower of cost and net realisable value. Entry: **Dr Stock (inventory) account, Cr Trading account / cost of sales.** It reduces cost of sales and appears as a **current asset**.
- **Drawings:** anything the owner takes from the business for **personal** use: cash, goods, or a "salary" paid to a sole trader.
  - Cash: **Dr Drawings, Cr Cash/Bank.**
  - Goods at cost: **Dr Drawings, Cr Purchases.**
  - Drawings are deducted from capital in the balance sheet; they are **never** an expense.

**Explain more**

- Trap: when options give both "Dr inventory, Cr profit and loss" and "Dr inventory, Cr cost of sales", choose **cost of sales**: it names the exact figure reduced.
- Worked example: during the year the owner took a ₦100,000 "salary", drew ₦10,000 cash for school fees and took goods costing ₦2,400; half the goods went to his children, half were gifts to long-standing customers.

| Item | Drawings (₦) | Business expense (₦) |
|---|---:|---:|
| Owner's salary | 100,000 | |
| School fees | 10,000 | |
| Goods for children | 1,200 | |
| Gifts to customers | | 1,200 |
| **Total** | **111,200** | **1,200** |

- Gifts to customers serve the business (advertising), so they are an expense; everything for the owner's private benefit is drawings.

### Lesson 7.2: Accrued and prepaid expenses

**Must know**

- **Accrual concept:** revenue and expenses are recognised when earned or incurred and shown in the profit and loss account of the period to which they relate, **not** when cash is paid or received.
- **Accrued expense (expense owing):** incurred but not yet paid at the year end. **Current liability.** Adjustment: **Dr Expense, Cr Accrued expense (liability).** No cash moves.
- **Prepaid expense (payment in advance):** paid this year for a future period. **Current asset** (a deferred expense). Adjustment: **Dr Prepaid expense (asset), Cr Expense.**
- **Formula (charge to profit and loss):**

  Expense = cash paid − opening accrual + closing accrual + opening prepayment − closing prepayment

| Item | Effect on P&L charge | Balance sheet |
|---|---|---|
| Closing accrual | Add | Current liability |
| Closing prepayment | Deduct | Current asset |

- **Order of events:** accrued expense: **incurred first**, paid later. Prepaid (deferred) expense: **paid first**, incurred later as the benefit is used.

**Explain more**

- Worked example 1 (accrual): motor expenses paid ₦108,000; owing at the start ₦30,000; owing at the end ₦20,700. Charge = 108,000 − 30,000 + 20,700 = **₦98,700**.

| Motor expenses a/c | ₦ | | ₦ |
|---|---:|---|---:|
| Cash | 108,000 | Balance b/d (owing) | 30,000 |
| Balance c/d (owing) | 20,700 | Profit and loss | 98,700 |
| | **128,700** | | **128,700** |

  The ₦20,700 comes down as a **credit** balance: a current liability.
- Worked example 2 (prepayment): insurance prepaid at the start ₦7,400; paid ₦50,000; prepaid at the end ₦25,000. Charge = 7,400 + 50,000 − 25,000 = **₦32,400**.

| Insurance a/c | ₦ | | ₦ |
|---|---:|---|---:|
| Balance b/d (prepaid) | 7,400 | Profit and loss | 32,400 |
| Cash | 50,000 | Balance c/d (prepaid) | 25,000 |
| | **57,400** | | **57,400** |

  The ₦25,000 comes down as a **debit** balance: a current asset. It is the **unexpired** portion of the premium; the expired portion is the expense.
- Quick check: rent prepaid at the start ₦1,000, paid ₦8,000, owing at the end ₦600: rent for the year = 1,000 + 8,000 + 600 = **₦9,600**. Rent ₦6,000 with ₦500 more to accrue = ₦6,500; insurance ₦1,500 with ₦250 prepaid = ₦1,250.
- **Effect of omitting an accrual:** expenses too low, so **profit overstated** and **liabilities understated** by the same amount (₦1,800 of unpaid wages left out overstates profit by ₦1,800).
- Using up a prepayment: **Dr Insurance expense, Cr Prepaid insurance.** Prepaid assets and expenses both have **debit** normal balances, so a **credit** decreases them. Cash is never part of an accrual or prepayment adjustment.
- **Reversing entry:** made on the first day of the new period to cancel an accrual-type adjustment from the previous period, so the later payment or receipt can be recorded in the normal way. (A reconciliation compares two records; it removes nothing.)
- Trap: prepaid rent in a trial balance is an **asset (deferred expense)**, not deferred income.

### Lesson 7.3: Accrued and prepaid income

**Must know**

- **Accrued income (outstanding income, income receivable):** earned during the period but not received by the year end, e.g. rent receivable, commission receivable, interest receivable. **Current asset.** Adjustment: **Dr Income receivable (asset), Cr Income account.**
- **Prepaid income (income received in advance; deferred or unearned revenue):** received this period but relating to the next, e.g. rent received in advance, fees received before the work is done. **Current liability**: the business owes the customer goods or services. Adjustment: **Dr Income account, Cr Income received in advance (liability).**
- **Formula (income credited to profit and loss):**

  Income = cash received − opening accrued + closing accrued + opening in advance − closing in advance

| Item | Effect on income | Balance sheet |
|---|---|---|
| Closing accrued income | Add | Current asset |
| Closing income in advance | Deduct | Current liability |

- **Order of events:** accrued income: **earned first**, received later. Deferred (unearned) income: **cash received first**, earned later.

**Explain more**

- Worked example 3 (accrued income): commission received ₦58,000; owing to the business at the start ₦18,000; owing at the end ₦10,450. Income = 58,000 − 18,000 + 10,450 = **₦50,450**.

| Commission receivable a/c | ₦ | | ₦ |
|---|---:|---|---:|
| Balance b/d (owing) | 18,000 | Cash | 58,000 |
| Profit and loss | 50,450 | Balance c/d (owing) | 10,450 |
| | **68,450** | | **68,450** |

- Worked example 4 (income in advance): rent received in cash ₦120,000; received in advance at the start ₦10,000; received in advance at the end ₦25,000. Income = 120,000 + 10,000 − 25,000 = **₦105,000**.

| Rent received a/c | ₦ | | ₦ |
|---|---:|---|---:|
| Profit and loss | 105,000 | Balance b/d (in advance) | 10,000 |
| Balance c/d (in advance) | 25,000 | Bank | 120,000 |
| | **130,000** | | **130,000** |

- When unearned revenue is later earned: **Dr Unearned (deferred) revenue, Cr Fees earned (revenue).** No cash; the cash came in earlier.
- A bank's accrued interest: **Dr Interest receivable, Cr Interest income.** The borrower's accrued interest: **Dr Interest expense, Cr Interest payable.**
- Trap: "rent received in advance" is the only **liability** among prepaid rent, prepaid insurance, rent received in advance and accrued rent received; the other three are assets.

### Lesson 7.4: Depreciation and disposal of fixed assets

**Must know**

- **Depreciation:** the systematic allocation of the cost of a fixed (non-current) asset, less its residual value, over its useful life, charged as an expense each year. It is **not** a fund for replacing the asset.
- **Three factors used:** (1) **cost**, (2) estimated **residual (salvage, scrap) value**, (3) expected **useful life**. **Repairs are ignored**: they are revenue expenditure charged straight to profit and loss.
- **Formulas:**
  - **Straight-line:** annual charge = (cost − residual value) ÷ useful life. Same charge every year. When a question gives cost, residual value and useful life but **names no method, use straight-line**. Rearranged: useful life = (cost − residual value) ÷ annual charge.
  - **Reducing balance:** annual charge = rate % × net book value at the start of the year. Charge falls each year. The rate is applied to NBV; residual value is not deducted first (it only sets the rate: rate = 1 − (residual ÷ cost)^(1/n)).
  - **Sum of the years' digits:** sum of digits for n years = n(n + 1) ÷ 2. Year charge = (remaining years ÷ sum of digits) × (cost − residual).
- **Net book value (NBV, carrying amount)** = cost − accumulated depreciation (provision for depreciation).
- **Entry each year:** **Dr Depreciation (profit and loss), Cr Provision for (accumulated) depreciation.**
- **Yearly method:** a full year's depreciation in the year of purchase and **none** in the year of sale. (The alternative is time-apportioning by months.)
- **Disposal:** profit or loss = proceeds − NBV at disposal. A **gain** is credited to the income statement; a **loss** is debited.

**Explain more**

- Worked example 5 (straight-line): vehicle cost ₦170,000, residual ₦20,000, life 5 years. Charge = 150,000 ÷ 5 = ₦30,000 a year; accumulated depreciation after 3 years = **₦90,000**; NBV = ₦80,000. (₦102,000 forgets the residual; ₦150,000 is all 5 years.) Check: cost ₦360,000, residual ₦12,000, charge ₦69,600: life = 348,000 ÷ 69,600 = **5 years**.
- Worked example 6 (reducing balance, 20%): cost ₦200,000.

| Year | Opening NBV (₦) | Charge 20% (₦) | Closing NBV (₦) |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| 1 | 200,000 | 40,000 | 160,000 |
| 2 | 160,000 | 32,000 | 128,000 |
| 3 | 128,000 | 25,600 | 102,400 |

  Accumulated depreciation after 3 years = ₦97,600.
- Worked example 7 (sum of the years' digits): cost ₦100,000, life 5 years, residual nil. Sum = 5 × 6 ÷ 2 = 15. Charges: 5/15 = **₦33,333.33**, 4/15 = ₦26,666.67, 3/15 = ₦20,000, 2/15 = ₦13,333.33, 1/15 = ₦6,666.67; total ₦100,000. (₦20,000 a year would be straight-line.)
- **Disposal entries:**
  1. Transfer cost: **Dr Disposal, Cr Asset account.**
  2. Transfer accumulated depreciation: **Dr Provision for depreciation, Cr Disposal.**
  3. Proceeds: **Dr Bank, Cr Disposal.**
  4. Balance = gain (Dr Disposal, Cr P&L) or loss (Dr P&L, Cr Disposal).
- Worked example 8: machine cost ₦500,000, accumulated depreciation ₦300,000 (NBV ₦200,000), sold for a cheque of ₦150,000. Loss = 150,000 − 200,000 = **₦50,000**.

| Machinery disposal a/c | ₦ | | ₦ |
|---|---:|---|---:|
| Machinery (cost) | 500,000 | Provision for depreciation | 300,000 |
| | | Bank (proceeds) | 150,000 |
| | | Profit and loss (loss) | 50,000 |
| | **500,000** | | **500,000** |

  Had the proceeds been ₦230,000, the gain of ₦30,000 would be debited in the disposal account and **credited** to the income statement.
- Working backwards: cost ₦100,000 with NBV ₦40,000 means depreciation provided on the asset sold = **₦60,000**.
- Trap: if a trial balance shows cost ₦100,000 and provision ₦20,000, and this year's charge is ₦2,000, the balance sheet NBV = 100,000 − 20,000 − 2,000 = **₦78,000**. The trial balance provision is **before** this year's charge.
- Reducing balance on disposal: van cost ₦20,000, 20% for 3 years: NBV = 20,000 × 0.8³ = ₦10,240; sold for ₦18,000, profit = **₦7,760**.
- Provision for depreciation is a **contra asset**: a credit balance deducted from cost, increased by a **credit**.

### Lesson 7.5: Bad debts and provisions on debtors

**Must know**

- **Bad debt:** a debt that will not be paid. Write it off: **Dr Bad debts (P&L), Cr Debtor.**
- **Bad debt recovered:** reinstate the debt (**Dr Debtor, Cr Bad debts recovered/Bad debts**), then record the cash (**Dr Bank, Cr Debtor**). The recovery is income.
- **Provision for bad and doubtful debts (allowance for receivables):** an estimate of debts that **may** not be paid, usually a % of debtors **after** writing off bad debts. Based on **prudence**.
  - Only the **change** goes to profit and loss. Increase: **Dr P&L, Cr Provision.** Decrease: **Dr Provision, Cr P&L** (a gain).
  - **P&L charge** = bad debts written off + (new provision − old provision).
  - **Balance sheet:** debtors − provision = net debtors (net realisable value).
- **Provision for discount on debtors:** a % of debtors **after** deducting the provision for doubtful debts. Recorded like the doubtful-debts provision. (A provision for discount on creditors is the mirror image: it reduces creditors and is a gain.)
- Provisions for doubtful debts and accumulated depreciation are **contra asset** accounts with normal **credit** balances; a **credit** increases them. They are not liabilities.

**Explain more**

- Worked example 9: debtors in the trial balance ₦410,000; bad debts to write off ₦10,000; old provision ₦15,000; new provision 5% of debtors; provision for discount 2%.

| Step | Working | ₦ |
|---|---|---:|
| Debtors after write-off | 410,000 − 10,000 | 400,000 |
| New provision (5%) | 5% × 400,000 | 20,000 |
| Increase in provision | 20,000 − 15,000 | 5,000 |
| P&L charge (bad and doubtful) | 10,000 + 5,000 | 15,000 |
| Provision for discount (2%) | 2% × (400,000 − 20,000) | 7,600 |
| Net debtors in balance sheet | 400,000 − 20,000 − 7,600 | 372,400 |

- Quick checks:
  - Debtors ₦2,500,000, provision rising from ₦500,000 to ₦750,000: P&L charge = **₦250,000**, not ₦750,000.
  - Bad debts ₦3,000, provision rising from ₦3,500 to ₦4,000: P&L debit = 3,000 + 500 = **₦3,500**.
  - Debtors ₦100,000, provision ₦70,000: net = **₦30,000**.
  - Debtors ₦30,000; 5% doubtful = ₦1,500; 2% discount on ₦28,500 = ₦570; net = **₦27,930** (₦27,900 wrongly takes 2% of the full ₦30,000).
- Trap: never charge the whole new provision; the old balance was charged in earlier years.

### One-minute recap

- Adjustments make the accounts show a true view; 7 types: closing stock, depreciation, accrued/prepaid income, accrued/prepaid expenses, drawings, provision for discounts, bad debts and provisions.
- Closing stock: Dr Stock, Cr cost of sales (trading account). Goods taken by the owner: Dr Drawings, Cr Purchases.
- Accrued expense = current liability (add to expense); prepaid expense = current asset (deduct).
- Accrued income = current asset (add to income); income in advance (deferred/unearned revenue) = current liability (deduct).
- Expense = paid − opening accrual + closing accrual + opening prepayment − closing prepayment.
- Straight-line = (cost − residual) ÷ life; reducing balance = rate × NBV; SYD year 1 = n ÷ [n(n+1)/2] × (cost − residual).
- NBV = cost − accumulated depreciation; disposal gain = proceeds − NBV, credited to the income statement.
- P&L charge for debts = bad debts + increase in provision; discount provision is on debtors after the doubtful-debts provision.

### Theory practice

**Question:** On 1 January rates prepaid were ₦4,000. During the year ₦30,000 was paid. At 31 December rates of ₦6,000 were owing. Calculate the rates charge for the year and state how the ₦6,000 appears in the balance sheet.

*Worked solution:* Charge = 4,000 + 30,000 + 6,000 = **₦40,000** to profit and loss. The ₦6,000 owing is an accrued expense, shown as a **current liability**. Entry for the accrual: Dr Rates, Cr Accrued rates.
