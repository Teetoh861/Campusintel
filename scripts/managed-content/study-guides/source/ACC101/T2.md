## Topic 2: Double entry and the accounting equation

**What you must know:** the five elements; A = C + L and its expanded forms; the dual-aspect rule; the double entry matrix; posting to T accounts; balancing and closing off accounts; capital vs revenue receipts and expenditure.

### Lesson 2.1: The five elements and the accounting equation

**Must know**

- **Assets:** the resources a business owns. **Non-current assets** are kept for use, not for sale, for more than one accounting period (motor vehicles, plant and machinery, land and building, fixtures and fittings). **Current assets** are cash or items meant to turn into cash within the period (inventory, receivables (debtors), cash in hand, cash at bank, prepayments). Non-current + current = total assets.
- **Capital (owner's equity):** finance provided by the owners, not meant to be repaid in the ordinary course of business. Because the business is separate from its owner (entity concept), capital is the **indebtedness of the business to the proprietor**.
- **Liabilities:** the part of the assets provided by outsiders (loans, credit purchases, bank overdraft). **Non-current:** not due within 12 months (long-term bank loan, debentures). **Current:** due within the year (trade payables (creditors), accrued expenses, income received in advance, bank overdraft).
- **Revenue:** income earned (sales, commission received, rent received, interest received, discount received).
- **Expenses:** costs used up in earning revenue (purchases of goods, wages, rent, electricity).

**The equation and its forms**

1. At the start, if only the owner supplies assets: **Assets = Capital**.
2. Basic form: **Assets = Capital + Liabilities** (A = C + L), also written Assets = Liabilities + Owner's Equity.
3. **Assets − Liabilities = Capital**, i.e. **Net assets = Owner's equity**.
4. Capital = original contribution + profit, and profit = revenue − expenses, so **Assets = Original contribution + Revenue − Expenses + Liabilities**.
5. Final form: **Assets + Expenses = Capital + Revenue + Liabilities** (A + E = C + R + L).

- **Closing (current) capital = opening capital + further owner investment + revenue − expenses − drawings.**
- So **Assets = Liabilities + opening capital + further investment + (revenue − expenses) − drawings.**
- **Raise capital:** revenue (profit) and new investment. **Reduce capital:** expenses (losses) and drawings.
- The equation holds **at all times**, for every organisation, whatever its size or the number of transactions.

**Explain more**

**Finding the missing figure** (use A = C + L):

| Assets ₦ | Liabilities ₦ | Capital ₦ |
|---|---|---|
| 55,000 | 16,900 | **38,100** |
| **41,600** | 17,200 | 24,400 |
| 36,100 | **7,600** | 28,500 |

**Capital from a list:** premises ₦20,000, closing stock ₦8,000, cash ₦100, creditors ₦3,000, loan ₦4,000. Assets = ₦28,100; liabilities = ₦7,000; capital = ₦28,100 − ₦7,000 = **₦21,100**. Trap: deduct **both** the creditors and the loan.

**Closing capital:** started with ₦50,000, borrowed ₦5,000 from a relative, profit ₦10,000, drawings ₦9,000. Closing capital = 50,000 + 10,000 − 9,000 = **₦51,000**. Trap: the loan is a **liability**, not capital, so ₦56,000 is wrong; forgetting drawings gives ₦60,000.

**How transactions move the equation**

| Transaction | Effect |
|---|---|
| Owner brings in cash | Asset up, capital up |
| Loan received | Asset up, liability up |
| Goods bought on credit | Asset/expense up, liability up |
| Goods or assets bought for cash | One asset up, another asset down (no change in total) |
| Cash paid to a creditor | Asset down, liability down |
| Goods sold on credit | Asset (receivable) up, revenue (so capital) up |
| Owner takes cash (drawings) | Asset down, capital down |

- A **liability** is owed to outsiders; it never increases owner's equity.
- **Dividends** in a company are like drawings: a distribution of profit to shareholders. They reduce retained earnings and so shareholders' equity, but they are **not an expense**, so they do not reduce net profit.

### Lesson 2.2: The dual aspect and the debit and credit rules

**Must know**

- **Duality concept:** every transaction has a dual effect, so it is recorded twice: **for every debit entry there must be a corresponding credit entry of equal value, and vice versa** (a receiver and a giver). It applies to **every transaction**, which is why the equation always balances.
- Every transaction affects **two or more** accounts. Total debits always equal total credits.
- **Debit (Dr)** and **credit (Cr)** describe the direction of change in an account. In a ledger (T) account the **left side is debit** and the **right side is credit**.
- **Rule:** items on the **left** of A + E = C + R + L (assets, expenses) are **debit in nature**; items on the **right** (capital, revenue, liabilities) are **credit in nature**. To **increase** an account, enter it on its nature side; to **decrease** it, do the opposite.

**The double entry matrix**

| Element | Nature (normal balance) | Increase | Decrease |
|---|---|---|---|
| Assets | Debit | Debit | Credit |
| Expenses | Debit | Debit | Credit |
| Capital | Credit | Credit | Debit |
| Revenue | Credit | Credit | Debit |
| Liabilities | Credit | Credit | Debit |

- **Drawings** reduce capital, so the drawings account is **debited**.
- Debit does **not** mean reduce and credit does **not** mean increase. The nature of the account and the transaction decide.

**4 steps to record a transaction**

1. Make sure a transaction has taken place: at least two parties, measurable in money.
2. Identify the two (or more) accounts involved.
3. Classify each account: asset, expense, capital, revenue or liability.
4. Decide whether each account increases or decreases, then apply the matrix.

**Explain more**

| Transaction | Debit | Credit |
|---|---|---|
| Motimo puts ₦250,000 cash into his business | Cash | Capital |
| Bought motor car for ₦7,500,000 cash | Motor vehicle | Cash |
| Sold goods ₦500,000 on credit to Sola & Co | Sola & Co (receivable) | Sales |
| Bought motor vehicle ₦1.5m on credit from Biafra Ltd | Motor vehicle | Biafra Ltd (payable) |
| Paid wages ₦75,000 by cash | Wages | Cash |
| Wrote a cheque to pay a supplier | Supplier (payable) | Bank (cash at bank, so the cash account is credited) |
| Owner took ₦75,000 from the bank for his son's hospital bill | Drawings | Bank |

**Account types exams test**

- Interest receivable, fees receivable, rent receivable: **assets** (owed **to** the business). Debiting rent receivable **increases** it.
- Accrued expenses (owed at the year end): **liability**, increased by a **credit**.
- Unearned (deferred) revenue, income received in advance: **liability**, decreased by a **debit** when the income is earned.
- Electricity, insurance, interest, repairs expense: increased by a **debit**. Service revenue: increased by a **credit**.

**Compound entry (three accounts):** you owe Kola ₦50,000 and pay by cheque, taking a 10% cash discount. Dr Kola ₦50,000; Cr Bank ₦45,000; Cr Discount received ₦5,000. Debits (₦50,000) = credits (₦45,000 + ₦5,000).

- **Trap:** trade discount is never recorded. Goods listed at ₦45,700 less 8% trade discount are recorded at ₦42,044. A cash discount offered is not recorded until it is actually taken on payment.

### Lesson 2.3: Recording transactions in T accounts

**Must know**

- A **ledger account** records all transactions on one item. Each has a debit side (left) and a credit side (right), with date, details (the name of the **other** account) and amount.
- Each transaction is posted **twice**: once on the debit side of one account and once on the credit side of another.
- Cash sales and credit sales both go to **sales**; goods bought for resale go to **purchases**, never to the asset account.

### Lesson 2.4: Balancing and closing off accounts

**Must know**

- **Closing off** summarises each account at the period end before a trial balance is extracted. The steps depend on the account type.
- **Statement of financial position (balance sheet) accounts** (assets, capital, liabilities) are perpetual: the closing balance becomes next period's opening balance.
- **Profit or loss accounts** (revenue, expenses) end with the year: their balances are **not** carried forward.

**Steps for statement of financial position accounts**

1. Add up both sides.
2. Find the greater side.
3. Put the greater total on **both** sides.
4. Insert the balancing figure on the **lower** side as **"balance carried down" (bal c/d)**: the closing balance.
5. Carry it to the **opposite** side below the totals as **"balance brought down" (bal b/d)**: the opening balance of the next period.

**Steps for revenue and expense accounts:** steps 1 to 3 as above; then insert the balancing figure on the lower side as **"transfer to profit/loss account"**: the revenue earned or expense incurred for the year. (To extract a trial balance first, these accounts are balanced with c/d and b/d like the others.)

**Explain more**

**Bank account (balanced)**

|---|---|---|---|
| Capital | 800,000 | Furniture | 150,000 |
| | | Rent | 40,000 |

| | | Balance c/d | 460,000 |
| | **800,000** | | **800,000** |

**Sales account (closed off)**

| Dr | ₦ | Cr | ₦ |
|---|---|---|---|
| Profit/loss | 210,000 | Cash | 120,000 |
| | | Bisi | 90,000 |

- The balance is **whichever side is short**, and it is named after where it goes, not what it is. Sales has a credit balance even though "Profit/loss" is written on the debit side.

### Lesson 2.5: Capital and revenue receipts and expenditure

**Must know**

- **Capital expenditure:** spending on acquiring or improving non-current assets, kept to earn profit over several periods; it increases the future earning capability of the business. It goes to the statement of financial position and is charged to profit gradually through **depreciation**.
- **Revenue (recurrent) expenditure:** spending on items consumed in the period or bought for resale; it maintains present earning capability. It is charged **in full** to the profit or loss account of the period.
- **Capital receipts:** capital introduced by the owner, long-term loans received, proceeds from selling non-current assets. They are not income and do not enter profit.
- **Revenue receipts:** sales, commission, rent and interest received, dividends on the business's own investments, discount received. (The owner paying his personal dividend into the business is capital introduced.) They are income in the profit or loss account.
- The **expired (used-up) portion of capital expenditure is an expense** (depreciation); the unexpired portion remains an **asset**.

| Capital expenditure | Revenue expenditure |
|---|---|
| Buying land, buildings, vehicles, new equipment | Wages and salaries (e.g. teachers' salaries in a school) |
| Carriage and installation of a new machine | Rent, electricity, insurance |
| Improvements that extend useful life or add capacity | Repairs and maintenance that keep the original condition |
| Adding a new feature (e.g. automatic control fitted to a vehicle) | Purchases of goods for resale; depreciation |

**Explain more**

- **The test for repairs:** compare with the asset's condition **before** it needed repair. Spending that only restores or keeps the original useful life is **revenue**. Spending that improves it (longer life, more capacity, a new function) is **capital**. ₦7,000 spent overhauling an engine to add 30 months to its life is capital; repairing a vehicle just to keep its original estimated life is revenue.
- Where one bill is part repair and part improvement, split it: the like-for-like part is revenue, the upgrade is capital.
- **Why it matters:** treating capital expenditure as revenue **understates** profit and assets; treating revenue expenditure as capital **overstates** both.
- "Recurrent expenditure" is the public-sector name for revenue expenditure: costs that keep coming back to keep the organisation running.

### One-minute recap

- Five elements: assets, liabilities, capital, revenue, expenses.
- A = C + L; A − L = C (net assets = owner's equity); A + E = C + R + L.
- Closing capital = opening capital + investment + revenue − expenses − drawings. Loans are liabilities, never capital.
- Every transaction: a debit and an equal credit, in two or more accounts, so the equation always balances.
- Assets and expenses (and drawings): debit to increase. Capital, revenue, liabilities: credit to increase.
- Left side of a T account = debit; right side = credit.
- Statement of financial position accounts: bal c/d and bal b/d. Revenue and expense accounts: transfer to profit/loss.
- Capital expenditure → asset (then depreciation); revenue expenditure → expense in full. Repairs that only maintain are revenue.

### Theory practice

**Question:** Tunde's opening capital was ₦400,000. During the year he introduced a further ₦50,000, made a profit of ₦120,000 and took drawings of ₦35,000. His liabilities at the year end were ₦90,000. Calculate his closing capital and total assets, and give the double entry for the drawings if taken in cash.

*Worked solution:* Closing capital = 400,000 + 50,000 + 120,000 − 35,000 = **₦535,000**. Total assets = capital + liabilities = 535,000 + 90,000 = **₦625,000**. Drawings: **Dr Drawings ₦35,000, Cr Cash ₦35,000**.
