## Topic 2: Models of Marketing Communication and MarCom Planning

**What you must know:** the basic communication model and its elements; linear, interactional and transactional views; one-step, two-step and multi-step flow; the response hierarchy models (AIDA, Lavidge and Steiner, innovation-adoption, information processing, DAGMAR); the process of marketing communications and SMART objectives; SOSTAC; the MCPF and its seven elements; push, pull and profile strategies; budgeting methods.

### Lesson 2.1: The basic communication model

**Must know**

- **Model of marketing communication:** a simplified picture of how a persuasive message passes from a sender to a receiver. Its usefulness lies in its ability to **explain how persuasive messages are transmitted and received**, so a marketer can see where communication can break down.
- **Basic elements:** **sender, encoded message, medium (channel), receiver and feedback**, plus noise.
- **Basic sequence:** **Sender - encoding - message/medium - decoding - receiver - feedback.**

| Element | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Sender (source) | The person or organisation with an idea to share, e.g. the brand |
| Encoding | Turning the sender's ideas into a message made of words, pictures, symbols or sounds |
| Message | The set of symbols the sender transmits |
| Medium (channel) | The path that carries the message: TV, radio, press, social media, a salesperson |
| Decoding | The receiver interpreting the symbols in the message to give it meaning |
| Receiver | The person who gets and decodes the message (the target audience) |
| Response | What the receiver does after exposure |
| Feedback | The part of the response that goes back to the sender |
| Noise | Any unplanned interference or distortion that stops the receiver getting the message as intended |
| Field of experience | The experiences, attitudes, values and perceptions each party brings; understanding is greatest where the two fields overlap |

**Three views of communication:**

1. **Linear (Shannon and Weaver, 1949):** a **one-way** flow from sender, through encoding, message and channel, to decoding by a receiver. **Weakness:** it treats the receiver as **passive** and ignores feedback.
2. **Interactional (Schramm):** two-way; sender and receiver take turns, both encode and decode; adds **feedback** and **field of experience**.
3. **Transactional:** both parties send and receive **at the same time**, so meaning is created jointly within a shared context.

**Explain more**

- A sound model stresses **feedback as a way of refining the effectiveness of messages**. Without feedback, the firm cannot tell whether its message was understood.
- **Feedback example:** after hearing a radio jingle for a new noodle brand, a listener calls the toll-free line to ask where to buy it. The call is **feedback**, not the channel. Likes, comments, complaints, sales figures and survey answers are also feedback.
- **Noise examples:** a power cut during a TV advert, traffic noise drowning a radio jingle, a rival's advert in the same break, a blurred billboard, a receiver who is distracted.
- **Field of experience trap:** a Lagos bank uses slang and references its young target customers in Ibadan do not understand, so the advert fails. The cause is **too little overlap between the fields of experience** of sender and receiver, not noise. The fix is to encode in words and images the audience shares.
- Exams swap encoding and decoding. Remember: the **sender encodes**, the **receiver decodes**. Options that put the receiver first, or decoding before the medium, are wrong.
- The basic model is a **communication** model. Do not confuse it with the 4Ps (product, price, place, promotion), STP (segmentation, targeting, positioning) or SWOT.

### Lesson 2.2: Flow models: one-step, two-step and multi-step

**Must know**

1. **One-step flow (hypodermic needle or magic bullet):** the mass media reach the audience **directly**, with **no opinion leaders** in between. Every receiver is assumed to take in the message the same way.
2. **Two-step flow (Lazarsfeld and colleagues; later Katz and Lazarsfeld):** messages flow from the **mass media to opinion leaders**, who then pass them on (with their own interpretation) to **opinion followers**.
3. **Multi-step flow:** develops the two-step model by recognising that information can flow **in several directions among the media, opinion leaders and opinion followers**. Followers also use the media directly, and opinion leaders also seek advice from others.

- **Opinion leader:** a person within a group whose views on a product or topic others respect and copy.

**Explain more**

- **Key difference:** in the one-step model the media reach the audience directly; in the two-step model **opinion leaders stand in between**. Neither model's distinguishing feature is feedback.
- **Nigerian application:** a skincare brand sends free products to a well-known Nigerian beauty blogger, hoping her followers will copy her recommendations. This relies on the **two-step flow**. Influencer marketing on Instagram and TikTok, and telecoms such as MTN or Glo using celebrity ambassadors, follow the same logic.
- The two-step idea came from Lazarsfeld, Berelson and Gaudet's study of how American voters decided in the 1940 election: personal influence mattered more than media alone.

### Lesson 2.3: Response hierarchy (hierarchy-of-effects) models

**Must know**

- **Hierarchy-of-effects (response hierarchy) models:** models that assume buyers pass through a **set sequence of stages, from awareness to purchase**, in response to marketing communication.
- **Three components (learn, feel, do):**
    - **Cognitive (thinking):** what the receiver **knows or thinks**, e.g. awareness, knowledge.
    - **Affective (feeling):** what the receiver **feels**, e.g. liking, preference.
    - **Conative (behavioural):** what the receiver **does**, e.g. trial, purchase.

| Model (author) | Cognitive | Affective | Conative (behaviour) |
|---|---|---|---|
| AIDA (E. St. Elmo Lewis) | Attention | Interest, Desire | Action |
| Hierarchy of effects (Lavidge and Steiner, 1961) | Awareness, Knowledge | Liking, Preference | Conviction, Purchase |
| Innovation-adoption (Rogers) | Awareness | Interest, Evaluation | Trial, Adoption |
| Information processing (McGuire) | Presentation, Attention, Comprehension | Yielding, Retention | Behaviour |
| DAGMAR (Russell Colley, 1961) | Awareness, Comprehension | Conviction | Action |

- **AIDA** = **A**ttention, **I**nterest, **D**esire, **A**ction (4 stages). Desire comes immediately after Interest.
- **Lavidge and Steiner** (6 stages): **awareness, knowledge, liking, preference, conviction, purchase.**
- **Innovation-adoption** (5 stages): **awareness, interest, evaluation, trial, adoption.**
- **Information processing** (6 stages): presentation, attention, comprehension, yielding, retention, behaviour.
- **DAGMAR** = **D**efining **A**dvertising **G**oals for **M**easured **A**dvertising **R**esults, by **Russell Colley** (1961). Stages (ACCA): **awareness, comprehension, conviction, action.**
- **DAGMAR principle:** advertising objectives must be **specific and measurable**, with a defined **target audience**, a **benchmark** (starting point), the **degree of change** sought and a **time period**. Objectives are communication tasks, not just sales.

**Explain more**

- **AIDA in practice:** a poster for a new Lagos gym uses bright colours and a bold headline so passers-by stop and look: **Attention**. A "Shop now - offer ends at midnight" button on a Jumia-style advert: **Action**. Showing how tasty a new Indomie flavour is builds **Interest**; showing someone enjoying it and saying "you deserve this" builds **Desire**.
- **Kotler's buyer-readiness stages** are the same six as Lavidge and Steiner. Exams swap the order of liking and knowledge, or of preference and conviction: knowledge always comes before liking, and preference before conviction.
- Some writers place conviction in the affective stage rather than the conative; in every version, **liking and preference are affective** and **purchase is conative**.
- Do not mix up the stage lists: "awareness, comprehension, conviction, action" is DAGMAR; "awareness, interest, evaluation, trial, adoption" is innovation-adoption; "attention, interest, desire, action" is AIDA. "Exposure, reception, cognitive response ... behaviour" belongs to a different communications-response sequence, not innovation-adoption.
- **DAGMAR example:** "Raise awareness of our new juice among Lagos undergraduates from 20% to 45% within six months." Target audience = Lagos undergraduates; benchmark = 20%; degree of change = 25 percentage points; time period = six months.
- **Criticism of hierarchy models:** consumers **do not always pass through the stages in a fixed order**. With low-involvement or impulse buys (a sachet of Pure Water, a Gala at a traffic jam), people may buy first and form attitudes afterwards (do-feel-learn or learn-do-feel). The models also make it hard to link one advert to one stage, and they ignore the effect of other influences such as friends and price.

### Lesson 2.4: Developing a marketing communications plan

**Must know**

- **Why a plan is needed:** it sets out **in a logical order what the firm wants to achieve with communication and how**, so activities are coordinated and can be evaluated. It does **not** replace the marketing plan or guarantee success.
- An effective plan requires **setting measurable goals, selecting appropriate tools and evaluating outcomes**.
- A well-executed plan ensures **integration and consistency across all communication touchpoints** (one clear message across every tool).
- A **foundational element** is formulating **precise, actionable and time-bound objectives**.

**Process of marketing communications (in order):**

1. **Identify the target audience** (first stage): focus on distinct segments, not everybody.
2. **Determine the company's unique value (selling) proposition (UVP or USP):** what makes the business stand out from rivals.
3. **Bring the solution to customers' queries:** strategies that answer customers' pain points with the product.
4. **Select channels to deliver the brand message:** based on objectives and customers' choices.
5. **Budget allocation:** how much to spend on each channel.
6. **Message development:** crafting key messages that resonate with the audience.
7. **Execution:** implementing the chosen plan.
8. **Evaluate and measure the results:** track outcomes after the campaign runs.
9. **Develop control measures:** measure effectiveness against objectives and make adjustments.

- **SMART objectives:** **S**pecific, **M**easurable, **A**chievable, **R**ealistic, **T**ime-bound.
- **SOSTAC** (P.R. Smith): **S**ituation, **O**bjectives, **S**trategy, **T**actics, **A**ction, **C**ontrol.

**Explain more**

- **USP example:** Domino's Pizza positioning itself as the business that delivers the newest and hottest pizza faster than competitors, in only 30 minutes, is its **unique value (selling) proposition**. A Nigerian parallel: a bank promising account opening in five minutes on its app.
- **Count trap:** this process is often introduced as "six stages", yet nine steps are named above. Learn the order: the first is **identifying the target audience**, and the last two are evaluating results and developing control measures.
- **Good objective:** "Increase awareness of our new brand among undergraduates in Lagos from 20% to 45% within six months" is well written because it is **specific, measurable and time-bound, with a clear target audience and benchmark**.
- **Bad planning habits** exams offer as distractors: choosing media first and setting objectives after the campaign; copying a competitor's plan without audience analysis; spending equally on every tool; repeating last year's plan unchanged; leaving objectives undefined; choosing the cheapest medium regardless of audience; making each tool send a different, unrelated message.
- **SOSTAC** stages: Situation = where are we now; Objectives = where do we want to be; Strategy = how do we get there overall; Tactics = the details (which tools); Action = who does what and when; Control = how we measure and adjust. Smith adds the **3Ms**: men (people), money, minutes (time).
- **Kotler's steps for effective communication:** identify the target audience; determine communication objectives (buyer-readiness stage); design the message; choose the media; select the message source; collect feedback.

### Lesson 2.5: The Marketing Communications Planning Framework (MCPF)

**Must know**

- **MCPF (Fill):** a **sequence of linked stages that guides the development of a marketing communications plan**. It provides a **structured approach for aligning communication strategies with business objectives**.

**The seven elements in order:**

1. **Context analysis** (first): customer, business, internal and external contexts.
2. **Communication objectives:** derived from the **corporate and marketing objectives**; SMART.
3. **Marketing communications strategy:** the overall approach: push, pull or profile, and positioning.
4. **Coordinated communications mix:** **choosing and combining the tools, media and messages so they work together**.
5. **Resources:** the **budget, people and time** needed to carry out the plan.
6. **Scheduling and implementation:** **when each activity will happen and in what order**.
7. **Evaluation and control** (last): measuring results against objectives and adjusting.

- **Feedback:** results from evaluation **feed back into earlier stages** so that future plans improve.

**Four contexts examined in context analysis:**

| Context | What it covers |
|---|---|
| Customer | Buyers' needs, behaviour, segments, how they decide |
| Business | Competitors, the market, the firm's marketing strategy |
| Internal | The firm's resources, culture, finance, skills |
| External (wider environment) | Political, economic, social, technological, legal factors (PESTEL) |

**Three strategies (3Ps):**

1. **Pull:** aimed at **end-user consumers**, to encourage them to ask for and buy the product, so it is "pulled" through the channel.
2. **Push:** aimed at **wholesalers and retailers** (the trade), to encourage them to stock and promote the product.
3. **Profile:** aimed at **a wide range of stakeholders**, to build the organisation's **corporate image and reputation**.

**Explain more**

- **Context analysis is the foundation** because it provides the information on which objectives, strategy and the mix are based. Final evaluation of results is **not** part of context analysis; it is the last stage.
- **External context example:** a Nigerian telecom firm studying new NCC regulations, inflation and changes in technology before a campaign is analysing the **external (wider environment) context**.
- **Strategy example:** "Position our bank as the friendliest choice for students, using a pull strategy built mainly on social media" is a **marketing communications strategy** statement.
- **Push example:** a detergent maker giving extra margins and display allowances to distributors and supermarkets in Kano. **Pull example:** heavy TV and social media adverts for Peak Milk so shoppers ask for it by name. **Profile example:** Dangote publicising its corporate social responsibility to investors, government and communities.
- **Control and evaluation example:** six weeks after launch, a Port Harcourt restaurant checks whether awareness rose to the target and adjusts its adverts where it fell short.
- Order trap: some versions list scheduling before resources. What never changes is that **context analysis comes first, objectives come immediately after it, and evaluation and control come last**. Anything such as "recruitment of factory workers" or "deciding the corporate mission" is **not** an MCPF stage.

### Lesson 2.6: Budgeting methods

**Must know**

1. **Affordable method:** spend whatever is left after all other costs and the profit target are covered.
2. **Percentage-of-sales method:** budget = percentage x sales (past or forecast sales).
3. **Competitive parity method:** match competitors' spending, or the industry's ratio of promotion to sales.
4. **Objective-and-task method:** set objectives, list the tasks needed to reach them, cost each task and add them up. Budget = sum of task costs. Considered the most logical method.
5. **Arbitrary method:** management simply picks a figure.

- **Share of voice (SOV)** = brand's promotion spend / total category promotion spend x 100.

**Explain more**

- **Percentage of sales:** sales ₦500 million, 4% rule. Budget = 0.04 x ₦500,000,000 = **₦20,000,000**. Weakness: it treats sales as the cause of promotion rather than the result, so budgets fall when sales fall, exactly when more promotion may be needed.
- **Competitive parity:** the industry spends 5% of sales on promotion; your sales are ₦400 million. Budget = 0.05 x ₦400,000,000 = **₦20,000,000**. Weakness: it assumes competitors know best and ignores your own objectives.
- **Objective-and-task:** to raise awareness from 20% to 45%, a firm needs TV ₦12m + radio ₦4.5m + social media ₦3m + campus sampling ₦2.5m. Budget = 12 + 4.5 + 3 + 2.5 = **₦22 million**. Strength: spending is tied to objectives.
- **Affordable:** expected revenue ₦120m, all other costs ₦100m, profit target ₦15m. Promotion budget = 120 - 100 - 15 = **₦5 million**. Weakness: promotion is treated as a leftover, not an investment.
- **Share of voice:** your brand spends ₦20m in a category where total spend is ₦80m. SOV = 20 / 80 x 100 = **25%**.

### One-minute recap

- Basic model: sender - encoding - message/medium - decoding - receiver - feedback, with noise; Schramm adds field of experience.
- Linear model is one-way with a passive receiver; interactional adds feedback; transactional is simultaneous.
- One-step: media direct to audience. Two-step: media - opinion leaders - followers. Multi-step: many directions.
- AIDA: attention, interest, desire, action. Lavidge and Steiner: awareness, knowledge, liking, preference, conviction, purchase.
- Innovation-adoption: awareness, interest, evaluation, trial, adoption. DAGMAR (Colley): awareness, comprehension, conviction, action.
- Cognitive = think, affective = feel, conative = do.
- MarCom process starts with identifying the target audience; objectives must be SMART; SOSTAC = situation, objectives, strategy, tactics, action, control.
- MCPF: context analysis, objectives, strategy, coordinated mix, resources, scheduling and implementation, evaluation and control.
- Pull = consumers; push = trade; profile = all stakeholders.
- Budgets: affordable, percentage of sales, competitive parity, objective-and-task.

### Theory practice

**Question:** A Lagos snack maker had sales of ₦250 million last year and forecasts ₦300 million this year. It budgets 3% of sales for promotion. (a) Find the budget using past sales and using forecast sales. (b) If rivals together spend ₦36 million, what is the firm's share of voice using the forecast-based budget?

**Solution:**

- (a) Past sales: 0.03 x ₦250,000,000 = **₦7,500,000**. Forecast sales: 0.03 x ₦300,000,000 = **₦9,000,000**.
- (b) Total category spend = ₦9m + ₦36m = ₦45m. SOV = 9 / 45 x 100 = **20%**.
