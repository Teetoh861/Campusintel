## Topic 4: Source, Message, Receiver and Medium; Opinion Leaders

**What you must know:** the four building blocks (source, message, receiver, medium) and how they fit the communication process; source credibility, attractiveness and power and the process each works through; message appeals, sidedness, order and conclusion drawing; the receiver's selective filters and fields of experience; personal and non-personal channels and media choice; opinion leaders, opinion formers, two-step and multi-step flow; adoption and diffusion of innovations.

### Lesson 4.1: The four building blocks and the process

**Must know**

- **Source (sender):** the originator of the message, such as a brand, company or spokesperson. For it to work, the audience must see the source as **credible, knowledgeable and relevant**.
- **Message:** the crafted content intended to inform, persuade or evoke a response. An effective message is **clear, compelling and tailored to the audience's needs and values**; a strategically crafted one **connects with the audience's aspirations, problems and motivations**.
- **Receiver (audience):** the intended audience that **decodes and responds to** the message. Their reaction depends on **their own interpretation, cultural background and how relevant the message is to them**.
- **Medium (channel):** the channel, such as digital platforms or broadcast media, that delivers the message. It should be **selected to match the target audience's media preferences and habits**.
- The interplay of source, message, receiver and medium makes up **the framework of the marketing communication process**.

**Elements of the process (9):**

1. **Sender:** the party sending the message.
2. **Encoding:** putting thought into symbols (words, pictures, music).
3. **Message:** the set of symbols sent.
4. **Media (channel):** the path the message travels.
5. **Decoding:** the receiver giving meaning to the symbols.
6. **Receiver:** the party getting the message.
7. **Response:** the receiver's reactions after exposure (e.g. awareness, liking, buying).
8. **Feedback:** the **part of the receiver's response that is communicated back to the sender**.
9. **Noise:** **unplanned distortion or interference** during the process, which can make the receiver get a different message from the one sent.

- **Field of experience:** the experiences, attitudes, values and language a person brings. Communication is most effective when the sender's and receiver's fields of experience **overlap**.

**Explain more**

- **Noise example:** a paint brand's radio jingle is drowned by generator sound in many homes, so listeners miss the brand name. The generator sound is **noise**. Other noise: a poor network signal, a rival advert in the same break, a distracted viewer.
- **Feedback example:** after an advert runs, a firm tracks calls to its hotline, changes in sales and comments on its social media page. All are **feedback**. Do not confuse feedback with response: response is everything the receiver does; feedback is only the part that returns to the sender.
- **Field of experience trap:** an advert full of Lagos teenage slang aimed at elderly rural farmers fails because there is **too little overlap** between the fields of experience of sender and receiver. That is why a firm advertising in the North may use Hausa, and in the South-West Yoruba.
- The sender encodes; the receiver decodes. Exams often swap these two.

### Lesson 4.2: The source

**Must know**

**3 categories of source characteristics** (Kelman's model, used by Belch and Belch), each working through a different process:

| Source characteristic | What it is made of | Process of influence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Credibility** | expertise, trustworthiness | **Internalisation** |
| **Attractiveness** | similarity, familiarity, likability | **Identification** |
| **Power** | ability to reward or punish | **Compliance** |

- **Expertise:** the knowledge, experience or skill the source is seen to have on the subject.
- **Trustworthiness:** the honesty, integrity and believability of the source; the audience's belief that the source has **no motive to mislead** them.
- **Internalisation:** the receiver accepts a credible source's view **because they believe the information is accurate**, so it becomes part of their own beliefs.
- **Similarity:** the source resembles the receiver (same needs, lifestyle, background). **Familiarity:** knowing the source through exposure. **Likability:** affection for the source because of looks, behaviour or other traits.
- **Identification:** the receiver adopts the source's view because they want to be like, or relate to, the source.
- **Power:** the source can **administer rewards or punishments**. The receiver goes along mainly through **compliance**, which may be outward only.
- **Direct source:** a spokesperson who delivers the message or demonstrates the product. **Indirect source:** someone (e.g. a model) who delivers no message but **draws attention** to and enhances the look of the advert.
- **Sleeper effect:** the persuasive impact of a message from a **low-credibility** source **increases over time**, as people forget the source but remember the message.

**Explain more**

- **Applications:** a qualified dentist in a clinic explaining how a toothpaste prevents decay = **expertise**. An ordinary mother of three in a busy Lagos home, just like the women the brand targets, praising a detergent = **similarity**. A popular Afrobeats star in a drink advert = attractiveness (familiarity and likability).
- **Why power is rarely usable in mass advertising:** advertisers usually **cannot reward or punish consumers, nor check whether they comply**. Power shows up more in settings such as a manager instructing staff or a big buyer pressing a supplier.
- **Risks of celebrity endorsers:**
    1. A **scandal** involving the celebrity harms the brand's image.
    2. **Overexposure:** endorsing so many brands that the endorsement loses impact.
    3. **Overshadowing the product:** viewers remember the star but not the brand.
    4. **Mismatch** between the celebrity's image and the brand.
    5. **High cost** and loss of popularity over time.
- **Trap:** a celebrity **attracting attention** to an advert is a **benefit**, not a risk.
- Trust falls when the audience sees the source as paid to say it; this is why a neutral expert or a satisfied customer is often more believable than the company itself.

### Lesson 4.3: The message: appeals, sidedness and structure

**Must know**

**Message appeals** (the theme that gets the audience to respond):

1. **Rational (informational) appeal:** focuses on the product's **features, quality, economy, value or performance**.
2. **Emotional appeal:** stirs feelings (love, warmth, joy, pride, fear, guilt, shame) rather than giving facts.
3. **Moral appeal:** directed to the audience's **sense of what is right and proper**; used for social causes (a cleaner environment, helping the needy).
4. **Fear appeal:** shows the bad consequences of not using the product or not changing behaviour.
5. **Humour appeal:** uses jokes and wit to attract attention and liking.
6. **Sex appeal:** uses sexual imagery or attractive models to draw attention.
7. **Comparative advertising:** **directly or indirectly names a competitor** and compares the brand with it on one or more attributes.

**Message sidedness:**

| Type | What it does | Works best when the audience |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **One-sided** | presents **only positive** points | already favours the brand, is less educated, is unlikely to hear opposing claims |
| **Two-sided** | presents **both good and bad** points | is **well educated, initially opposed, or likely to hear competitors' counter-arguments** |
| **Refutational appeal** | presents both sides, then **argues against (refutes)** the opposing view | faces rival claims; it builds resistance to them |

**Message structure:**

- **Primacy effect:** information presented **first** is learned best, so place the strongest arguments at the **beginning**.
- **Recency effect:** information presented **last** is remembered best, so place the strongest arguments at the **end**.
- **Conclusion drawing:** leave the conclusion **open** when the audience is **highly educated and highly involved**; state it clearly when the audience is less educated, less interested, sees the message only once, or the issue is complex.
- **Verbal vs visual:** pictures are often remembered better than words; the two should support each other.

**Explain more**

- **Applications:** a milk advert showing a mother lovingly feeding her smiling baby = **emotional**. An advert saying a generator uses less fuel per hour = **rational**. A campaign urging Lagosians not to dump refuse in drains = **moral**.
- **Fear appeals work** when they show the threat **and give a clear, easy action** to avoid it (e.g. a drink-driving campaign that says "drink, do not drive; take a ride"). Very shocking images with no solution make people switch off. Moderate fear usually works better than extreme fear.
- **Humour:** it draws attention and liking, but the joke can **distract attention from the brand** and **wear out with repetition**. It may also not translate across cultures.
- **Sex appeal:** draws attention but can distract from the brand and offend, especially in a conservative society; the product must be relevant to it.
- **Comparative risk:** consumers may get confused and credit the competitor (selective distortion; see Lesson 4.4). In Nigeria such adverts must also stay truthful and fair under the advertising code enforced by ARCON (formerly APCON).
- **Sidedness traps:** a smartphone advert listing only positives = **one-sided**. "Some say our prices are higher; here is why that is false" = **refutational**. Two-sided messages raise credibility because the source admits a weakness.
- Rational and emotional appeals are often combined, e.g. a bank advert showing a happy family (emotion) and an interest rate (reason).

### Lesson 4.4: The receiver and the medium

**Must know**

- **Perception:** the process of selecting, organising and interpreting information inputs to create meaning.
- **Selective perception:** the receiver's filtering of messages. Consumers notice only some adverts, interpret them to fit what they already believe, and remember only part of them. It has these parts:
    1. **Selective exposure:** choosing which messages to come into contact with (e.g. changing the channel during adverts).
    2. **Selective attention:** noticing only some of the messages one is exposed to.
    3. **Selective distortion (comprehension):** twisting information that conflicts with one's beliefs.
    4. **Selective retention:** remembering information that supports one's beliefs and forgetting the rest.
- **Decoding** is done by the receiver, through their own field of experience, culture and needs.

**Personal vs non-personal channels:**

| | Personal channels | Non-personal channels |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Meaning | two or more people communicate directly (face to face, phone, chat) | carry messages **without personal contact or feedback** |
| Types | **advocate** (company salespeople), **expert** (independent experts), **social** (neighbours, friends, family, associates) | **major media** (print, broadcast, display, online), **atmospheres** (designed environments), **events** (staged occurrences) |
| Example | a salesperson's face-to-face talk with a customer | TV commercial, newspaper advert, roadside billboard |

**Main media compared:**

| Medium | Strengths | Weaknesses |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Television** | sight, sound and motion; very wide mass reach | **high absolute cost**, clutter, short exposure |
| **Radio** | low cost, local, reaches people on the move, wide reach in Nigeria | audio only, fleeting, low attention |
| **Newspapers** | timely, local, believable | short life, poor reproduction |
| **Magazines** | specific audiences, good colour, long life | long lead time, high cost |
| **Outdoor (billboards)** | high repeat exposure, low cost per exposure | little selectivity, short message |
| **Internet/digital** | targeted, interactive, measurable, low cost | audience controls exposure, ad blocking |

- Choose the medium **after** identifying the target audience, matching their media habits, and weighing **reach** (how many people), **frequency** (how often) and **impact** (quality of exposure) against cost.

**Explain more**

- **Social channel example:** a buyer's friends, neighbours and family talk to them about a product. A pharmacist recommending a drug is an **expert** channel; a Jumia sales agent is an **advocate** channel.
- Personal channels are more persuasive because they allow two-way talk and instant feedback; non-personal channels reach far more people at a lower cost per person.
- **Medium trap:** the medium should not always be the cheapest, nor always TV. A firm targeting university students may favour Instagram and campus radio; one targeting market women may use radio in local languages.
- **Comparative advertising and selective distortion:** viewers may misremember which brand made the claim and credit the rival.

### Lesson 4.5: Opinion leaders, opinion formers and the flow of communication

**Must know**

- **Opinion leader:** a person **within a reference group** who, because of **special skills, knowledge, personality or other characteristics**, exerts social influence on others. A trusted person whose views and recommendations shape other people's attitudes and actions. Usually **not paid** and has no formal position.
- **Opinion former:** holds a **formal or professional position** (e.g. journalist, critic, doctor) that gives their views authority.
- **Opinion follower:** a person who seeks and accepts the views of opinion leaders.
- **Why opinion leaders matter:** they **increase a message's reach and credibility through their social influence**.

**Typical traits of opinion leaders:**

1. More **exposed to media** in their area of interest than their followers.
2. More knowledgeable and involved in the product category.
3. Usually from the **same social class** as their followers.
4. Influential in **specific categories**, not all categories.
5. More innovative, sociable and confident.

- **Two-step flow model** (Lazarsfeld and colleagues, 1940s): information flows **from the mass media to opinion leaders, who then pass it on to the wider population**.
- **Multi-step flow model:** improves on it by recognising that **media reach both leaders and followers directly, and influence flows in several directions** (leaders to followers, followers to leaders, follower to follower).

**Explain more**

- **Leader or former?** Tunde, unpaid, reads technology blogs and tries new phones early, so friends in his estate ask his advice = **opinion leader**. A professional food critic with a weekly newspaper column = **opinion former** (her influence comes from her formal position).
- **Application of two-step flow:** marketers **target opinion leaders**, e.g. with free samples or early access, so they spread the word. Examples: giving a new phone to tech reviewers, sampling a new seasoning cube to respected caterers.
- **Traps:** opinion leaders do not remove the need for paid advertising, do not control distribution, and are not equally influential across all categories. A fashion leader may know nothing about cars.
- The two-step model was criticised because followers also get information straight from the media; this led to the multi-step model.

### Lesson 4.6: Adoption and diffusion of innovations

**Must know**

- **Adoption process:** the **mental process an individual goes through** from first hearing about an innovation to finally adopting it. Stages (Kotler): **awareness, interest, evaluation, trial, adoption**.
- **Diffusion of innovation:** the **spread of an innovation through a whole market over time** (Everett Rogers).

**5 adopter categories, first to last** (Rogers):

1. **Innovators** (2.5%): venturesome, take risks.
2. **Early adopters** (13.5%): guided by respect; **opinion leaders** in their community.
3. **Early majority** (34%): deliberate; adopt before the average person.
4. **Late majority** (34%): sceptical; adopt after most people have.
5. **Laggards** (16%): **tradition-bound, suspicious of change**, adopt only when the innovation has become something of a tradition itself.

**5 characteristics affecting the rate of adoption:**

1. **Relative advantage:** how much better it seems than what exists.
2. **Compatibility:** how well it fits the values and experiences of users.
3. **Complexity:** how hard it is to understand or use (more complex = slower).
4. **Divisibility (trialability):** whether it can be tried on a small scale.
5. **Communicability (observability):** how easily its results can be seen or described to others.

**Explain more**

- **Relative advantage example:** mobile money spread quickly in Nigeria partly because users saw it as clearly better than queuing in banking halls to send money.
- **Trap:** adoption is about **one individual**; diffusion is about the **whole market**. Grouping buyers into innovators, early adopters and so on is the adopter categorisation, not the adoption process.
- **Order trap:** innovators come **before** early adopters. Memory aid: I-E-E-L-L (Innovators, Early adopters, Early majority, Late majority, Laggards).
- **Worked example:** a fintech app targets 400,000 potential users. Using Rogers' percentages:
    - Innovators: 2.5% of 400,000 = 0.025 x 400,000 = 10,000
    - Early adopters: 13.5% x 400,000 = 54,000
    - Early majority: 34% x 400,000 = 136,000
    - Late majority: 34% x 400,000 = 136,000
    - Laggards: 16% x 400,000 = 64,000
    - Check: 10,000 + 54,000 + 136,000 + 136,000 + 64,000 = 400,000. The first two groups together = 16% = 64,000 people, the group to win first because early adopters lead opinion.

### One-minute recap

- Source, message, receiver and medium together form the framework of the marketing communication process.
- Noise = unplanned interference; feedback = the part of the response sent back; overlap of fields of experience aids understanding.
- Source characteristics: credibility (expertise, trustworthiness) to internalisation; attractiveness (similarity, familiarity, likability) to identification; power to compliance.
- Sleeper effect: a low-credibility source's message gains impact over time.
- Appeals: rational, emotional, moral, fear, humour, sex, comparative. Fear needs a clear way out.
- Two-sided for educated, opposed or counter-argued audiences; refutational = both sides plus rebuttal; primacy = strongest first; open conclusion for educated, involved audiences.
- Selective exposure, attention, distortion and retention make up selective perception.
- Personal channels: advocate, expert, social. Non-personal: media, atmospheres, events.
- Opinion leader = informal peer; opinion former = formal position. Two-step: media to leaders to others; multi-step: many directions.
- Adopters: innovators, early adopters (opinion leaders), early majority, late majority, laggards.

### Theory practice

**Question:** Name the three categories of source characteristics and state the process through which each influences the receiver, with one example each.

*Answer outline:*

1. **Credibility** (expertise and trustworthiness) works through **internalisation**: the receiver accepts the view because they believe it is accurate. Example: a dentist recommending a toothpaste.
2. **Attractiveness** (similarity, familiarity, likability) works through **identification**: the receiver wants to relate to or be like the source. Example: an ordinary Lagos mother praising a detergent.
3. **Power** (ability to reward or punish) works through **compliance**: the receiver goes along to gain a reward or avoid punishment. Example: a manager instructing staff; rarely usable in mass advertising because advertisers cannot reward, punish or monitor consumers.
