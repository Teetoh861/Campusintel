## Topic 5: Communication, Business Communication and the Communication Process

**What you must know:** the meaning, origin, importance and 7 Cs of communication; what business communication is, its purposes and examples; the forms of business communication (internal/external, formal/informal and the grapevine, upward/downward/horizontal/diagonal, verbal/non-verbal, oral/written, visual); the elements of the communication process; the Lasswell, Shannon-Weaver, Schramm and transactional models; barriers to communication and how to overcome them.

### Lesson 5.1: Meaning, importance and principles of communication

**Must know**

- **Origin:** "communication" comes from the Latin word **communis**, meaning **common**. To communicate is to create a **common (shared) understanding** between people.
- **Communication:** the process of **transmitting information and shared meaning from one person or group to another person or group**. It is also the exchange of thoughts or conversation through written words, verbal conversation, electronic or digital media and social media to exchange ideas and information for a pre-planned purpose, or the process of using words, sounds or visual cues to supply information to one or more people.
- **Effective communication:** occurs when the **meaning understood by the receiver matches the meaning intended by the sender**. Sending a message is not enough; it must be understood.
- **One-way communication:** the message flows from sender to receiver with no immediate reply, e.g. a **roadside billboard**, a radio jingle, a printed notice.
- **Two-way communication:** the receiver can reply, so there is feedback, e.g. a telephone call, a face-to-face sales negotiation, a question-and-answer session.

**Importance of communication:**

1. Passes information and instructions.
2. Coordinates the work of people and departments.
3. Supports planning, decision-making and control.
4. Persuades and motivates (customers, staff, investors).
5. Builds relationships and trust with stakeholders.
6. Is the base of all marketing: without communication a firm cannot tell buyers what it offers.

**The 7 Cs of effective communication:**

1. **Clarity:** one clear idea, plain familiar words.
2. **Conciseness:** say it in as few words as needed; no repetition.
3. **Concreteness:** specific facts and figures, not vague claims.
4. **Correctness:** accurate facts, correct grammar and spelling, right level of language.
5. **Completeness:** all the information the receiver needs to act.
6. **Consideration:** see things from the receiver's side (the "you" attitude).
7. **Courtesy:** polite, respectful, sincere tone.

**Explain more**

- Some versions of the list replace **consideration** with **coherence** (logical, connected ideas). If an exam offers coherence as one of the 7 Cs, accept it; "complexity", "cost" or "confusion" are never among the 7 Cs.
- **Concreteness example:** "Our data is cheap" is vague; "₦1,000 for 1.5GB, valid 30 days" is concrete.
- **Effectiveness trap:** an option saying communication is effective "once the sender has transmitted the message, whether or not it is understood" is wrong. Effectiveness is judged at the receiver's end.
- A billboard on the Third Mainland Bridge is one-way: drivers cannot reply to it. A customer chatting with a bank's agent on the phone is two-way.

### Lesson 5.2: Business communication

**Must know**

- **Business communication:** the **exchange of information between people within and outside an organisation in order to achieve the organisation's goals**. It covers both internal (staff, departments) and external (customers, suppliers, investors, government, media) audiences, and both written and oral forms.

**Purposes of business communication:**

1. To **inform** employees and customers about decisions, policies and offers.
2. To **persuade** customers and other stakeholders to take action.
3. To **coordinate** the work of different departments.
4. To **instruct and direct** staff.
5. To **build relationships and goodwill** with internal and external stakeholders.
6. To get **feedback** and information for decisions.

**Examples of business communication:** analytical reports circulated to stakeholders; presentations used to align a team on its objectives; oral discussions during meetings; memos, circulars and letters; emails; press releases; staff newsletters; advertisements; non-verbal cues (body language, dress, punctuality) that shape how stakeholders see a firm.

**Explain more**

- **What is NOT business communication:** building a prototype, machine maintenance, counting stock, manufacturing, product design, financial forecasting, budget allocation, auditing and recruitment are business **activities**, not communication. The report or presentation **about** them is communication.
- **Trap options:** "communication only among employees" (too narrow, ignores external), "messages sent only to customers" (too narrow, ignores internal), "only written letters and memos" (ignores oral and non-verbal). The complete definition says **within and outside** the organisation, **to achieve its goals**.
- "To remove the need for planning and organising" is **not** a purpose. Communication supports planning; it never replaces it.
- **Strategic role:** the communication process helps a business **build relationships with internal and external stakeholders**. Cutting contact with customers, lowering quality or restricting supply are not things communication supports.
- Example: when GTBank announces a new feature, it informs customers (external), briefs branch staff (internal) and issues a press release to newspapers (external, through the media).

### Lesson 5.3: Forms of business communication

**Must know**

**1. By audience: internal vs external**

- **Internal:** within the organisation, e.g. **staff newsletter**, memo, circular, staff meeting, intranet notice.
- **External:** with people outside, e.g. **press release**, advertisement, billboard, letter to a supplier, product launch press conference, customer email.

**2. By official status: formal vs informal**

- **Formal:** follows **official channels and lines of authority** set by the organisation (chain of command), e.g. reports, memos, official meetings.
- **Informal:** spreads through friendships and casual conversation, outside official lines. Its main network is the **grapevine**.
- **Grapevine:** the informal network through which **news and rumours spread among employees outside official channels**.
  - Advantages: spreads information **very quickly**; shows managers staff feelings; builds social bonds; fills gaps when formal communication is slow.
  - Disadvantages: messages are often **distorted** and can spread **false rumours**; no one controls it; leaves no official record; can damage morale.

**3. By direction**

1. **Downward:** from superiors to subordinates, e.g. MD's circular on new resumption time, a supervisor's instructions, job descriptions, policy memos.
2. **Upward:** from subordinates to superiors, e.g. weekly sales reports, **suggestion box**, complaints, feedback surveys, requests.
3. **Horizontal (lateral):** between people **at the same level**, e.g. marketing manager and production manager agreeing a launch date.
4. **Diagonal (crosswise):** between people at **different levels in different departments**, e.g. a sales officer contacting the finance manager directly.

**4. By mode: verbal vs non-verbal (and visual)**

- **Verbal** uses words: **oral** (spoken: meetings, phone calls, presentations, interviews) and **written** (letters, memos, reports, emails, agreements, brochures).
- **Non-verbal** uses no words:
  - **Kinesics:** body movements: gestures, posture, facial expressions, eye contact.
  - **Paralanguage:** how the voice is used: tone, pitch, speed, volume.
  - **Proxemics:** use of personal space and distance.
  - **Chronemics:** use of time (punctuality, lateness, waiting).
  - **Haptics:** touch (handshake, pat on the back).
  - **Appearance and artefacts:** dress, grooming, office layout.
- **Visual:** charts, graphs, pictures, logos, signs, videos, infographics.

| Feature | Oral | Written |
|---|---|---|
| Feedback | Immediate, with clarification | Delayed |
| Record | None (unless recorded) | Permanent record; can serve as evidence |
| Speed | Fast | Slower to prepare |
| Editing | Little chance to edit | Can be checked and edited before sending |
| Best for | Discussion, quick decisions, sensitive talks | Contracts, policies, legal and long messages |
| Weakness | Easily forgotten or denied | Costly in time; no tone of voice |

**Explain more**

- **Channel richness** (Daft and Lengel): a channel's ability to carry several cues at once (words, tone, face) and give immediate feedback. From richest to leanest: **face-to-face meeting**, telephone or video call, personal email or letter, impersonal written notice, formal report or bulletin. Use rich channels for complex or sensitive messages and lean channels for routine ones.
- **Written for disputes:** a Kano distributor agreeing ₦5 million credit payable in 60 days should use a **signed written agreement**, because it creates a permanent record that can be relied on if a dispute arises. A phone call or the grapevine gives no proof.
- **Non-verbal traps:** a smile and firm handshake are non-verbal (kinesics plus haptics); tone of voice is non-verbal (paralanguage); dress is non-verbal. A **memo** is written verbal communication, not non-verbal. A telephone conversation is oral.
- **Chronemics example:** a supplier keeps a client waiting two hours past the meeting time in Abuja. The client reads it as "they do not value my business". The message was sent through the use of time.
- **Direction trap:** the suggestion box is **upward**, even though management set it up. A job description given to a new employee is **downward**. Two managers at the same level talking is **horizontal**, not diagonal.
- **Internal vs external trap:** a press release goes to newspapers and the public, so it is **external** even if staff also read it. A letter of complaint to a supplier is external.

### Lesson 5.4: The communication process and its elements

**Must know**

- **Communication process:** an idea is **encoded** into a message, **transmitted through a channel** and **decoded by the receiver**, who responds and sends feedback, with noise able to interfere at any point.
- **Basic business model:** **sender, message, channel, receiver and feedback** (with encoding, decoding and noise added in the full model).

**Elements of the process:**

1. **Sender (source):** the person or organisation that **has an idea to share and starts** the communication.
2. **Encoding:** **putting thoughts or ideas into symbolic form**, such as words, pictures, music or sounds.
3. **Message:** the **set of words, pictures, sounds or symbols** that the sender transmits.
4. **Channel (medium):** the **means or path through which the message travels** from sender to receiver, e.g. radio, TV, email, SMS, a face-to-face meeting.
5. **Decoding:** the process by which the **receiver assigns meaning to (interprets) the symbols** sent by the sender.
6. **Receiver:** the **person or group the message is intended for, who decodes it**.
7. **Response:** the **receiver's reaction** after receiving the message.
8. **Feedback:** the **part of the receiver's response that is communicated back to the sender**. It shows the sender how the message was received so it can be **corrected or refined**.
9. **Noise:** **any unplanned interference or distortion** that prevents the message from being received as intended. It can occur at any stage.
10. **Field of experience:** the background, knowledge, culture, language and experience each party brings. Communication works best when the sender's and receiver's fields of experience **overlap**.

**Explain more**

- **Full Nigerian walk-through:**
  - A Lagos cement company (**sender**) wants buyers to see its brand as strong and durable.
  - With an agency it turns this into a TV script showing a building standing firm in heavy rain (**encoding**).
  - The finished advert (**message**) runs on TV (**channel**).
  - A viewer in Ibadan sees it and concludes "this cement lasts" (**decoding**); the viewer is the **receiver**.
  - The viewer's interest and later purchase is the **response**.
  - Calls to the company and rising orders reported by dealers are **feedback**.
  - A power cut in the middle of the advert is **noise**.
- **Decoding example:** a viewer watches a soft drink advert of friends laughing at a party and concludes the drink is for happy social occasions. Interpreting the meaning is decoding.
- **Noise example:** a trader at Balogun Market cannot hear a bank's radio jingle because of a loud generator. The generator sound is noise. Noise is **not** the loud music inside the advert itself, and it is not the channel.
- **Feedback example:** a telecoms company sends an SMS offering a ₦1,000 data bundle. Customers who reply "YES" and those who call the helpline with questions are giving **feedback**.
- **Response vs feedback:** response is the receiver's whole reaction (some silent, like liking the brand); feedback is only the part that reaches the sender.
- **Encoding vs decoding:** the sender encodes, the receiver decodes. Exams swap them often.
- **Not an element:** budget, price, product, AIDA stages and the promotion mix tools are **not** elements of the communication process model. "Product, price, place and promotion" is the marketing mix; "attention, interest, desire, action" is AIDA; "advertising, sales promotion, PR, personal selling" is the promotion mix.
- An effective process ensures messages are **crafted, delivered and interpreted with clarity and purpose**. Ignoring the receiver or blocking feedback makes communication fail.

### Lesson 5.5: Models of communication

**Must know**

| Model | Author and date | Type | Key idea |
|---|---|---|---|
| Lasswell's formula | **Harold Lasswell, 1948** | Linear | "**Who says what, in which channel, to whom, with what effect?**" |
| Shannon-Weaver | **Claude Shannon and Warren Weaver, 1949** | Linear (one-way) | Built from work on **telephone signals** at Bell Labs; introduced **noise** |
| Schramm (Osgood-Schramm) | **Wilbur Schramm, 1954** | Interactive (circular) | Both parties encode and decode in turns; **feedback** and **field of experience** |
| Transactional | Dean Barnlund, 1970 | Transactional | Both parties **send and receive at the same time**, continuously |

1. **Linear model:** one-way flow from sender to a passive receiver. Elements in Shannon-Weaver: information source, transmitter (encoder), channel, receiver (decoder), destination, plus **noise source**. **Main weakness: no feedback** from the receiver.
2. **Interactive model:** two-way; the receiver sends feedback, so communication is a cycle. Meaning is shared only where the two **fields of experience overlap**.
3. **Transactional model:** communication is a **continuous process in which both parties send and receive messages at the same time** (e.g. a speaker reads the listener's face while still talking). Both are "communicators"; noise and context still apply.

**Why the communication model is useful to a business:**

1. It **explains how organisational messages are sent and received**.
2. It helps **identify barriers** (noise) that can stop a message being delivered and understood.
3. It shows the need to **know the target audience** and its field of experience.
4. It stresses **feedback** for checking and refining messages.
5. It guides choice of **channel** and message design.

**Explain more**

- **Lasswell's five questions map onto the elements:** who = sender; says what = message; in which channel = channel; to whom = receiver; with what effect = effect/response. The quote is Lasswell's, not Kotler's, Schramm's or Shannon and Weaver's.
- **Shannon and Weaver trap:** the 1949 model came from engineering (signals on telephone lines), so "noise" first meant static on a line. AIDA is a response model (Topic 2), not a communication process model.
- **Schramm's contribution:** communication is two-way and depends on shared experience. A Hausa-language radio advert in Kano works because it overlaps the listeners' field of experience.
- A model **never guarantees** that every message will be understood, does not fix prices and does not remove the need to know the audience.
- **Linear vs transactional:** a billboard fits the linear model; a live sales negotiation, where both sides talk, watch and react at once, fits the transactional model.

### Lesson 5.6: Barriers to communication

**Must know**

- **Barrier:** anything that blocks, distorts or delays a message so that the receiver's meaning differs from the sender's.

**Types of barriers:**

1. **Physical:** environmental noise (generators, traffic), distance, poor network, faulty equipment, poor lighting.
2. **Semantic (language):** **jargon**, technical terms, ambiguous words, different languages, poor translation.
3. **Psychological (emotional):** anger, fear, stress, anxiety, distrust, prejudice that stop a person listening.
4. **Perceptual:** **selective exposure** (seeing only some messages), **selective perception** (noticing and interpreting messages to fit existing beliefs), **selective retention** (remembering mainly what supports existing beliefs).
5. **Cultural:** differences in values, gestures, customs and language between groups or countries.
6. **Organisational (status):** too many levels of authority, rigid rules, status gaps, poor structure; junior staff fear speaking to top management.
7. **Information overload:** receiving more messages than one can process, so important ones are missed or read late.
8. **Poor listening and lack of feedback.**

**How to overcome barriers:**

1. Use **simple, clear language** suited to the receiver; avoid jargon.
2. **Encourage feedback** to check understanding; ask questions.
3. Practise **active listening**.
4. Choose the **right channel** and timing; use rich channels for complex messages.
5. Understand the receiver's **culture and field of experience**.
6. Reduce physical noise and fix equipment.
7. Cut unnecessary levels of authority; keep an open-door policy.
8. Prioritise and filter messages to reduce overload.
9. Control emotions; wait for a calm moment.

**Explain more**

- **Semantic:** an insurance agent explains a policy to market women in Aba using "indemnity", "actuarial loading" and "subrogation". They leave confused. Jargon is a semantic barrier.
- **Psychological:** a customer wrongly debited is so angry he does not listen to the bank officer. Emotion is the barrier.
- **Cultural:** a foreign firm's advert uses a hand gesture friendly at home but rude to many Nigerians. This is cultural, not semantic (no jargon is involved).
- **Information overload:** a bank officer gets over 200 emails a day and misses her manager's messages.
- **Organisational:** junior staff of a large Lagos firm fear reporting problems to the MD because of many levels and his high status.
- **Selective perception vs selective retention:** perception is how you **notice and interpret** a message now; retention is what you **remember** later. A loyal Indomie buyer who notices only the good points of an Indomie advert shows selective perception; one who later forgets a rival noodle brand's price cut shows selective retention.
- **Not a barrier:** active listening is a cure, not a barrier. Physical noise, jargon and emotional stress are all barriers.
- **Best cure trap:** using more technical terms, avoiding the receiver's questions or blasting one message through every channel at once do not overcome barriers. Simple language plus feedback does.

### One-minute recap

- Communication comes from Latin **communis** (common): transmitting information and shared meaning; effective when the receiver's meaning matches the sender's.
- 7 Cs: clarity, conciseness, concreteness, correctness, completeness, consideration, courtesy.
- Business communication = exchange of information **within and outside** the organisation to achieve its goals; purposes: inform, persuade, coordinate, instruct, build relationships.
- Forms: internal/external; formal/informal (grapevine: fast but distorted); downward, upward, horizontal, diagonal; oral, written, non-verbal, visual.
- Non-verbal: kinesics (body), paralanguage (voice), proxemics (space), chronemics (time), haptics (touch).
- Oral gives immediate feedback; written gives a permanent record. Face-to-face is the richest channel.
- Process: sender, encoding, message, channel, decoding, receiver, response, feedback, noise, field of experience.
- Models: Lasswell 1948 (who says what...), Shannon-Weaver 1949 (linear, noise, no feedback), Schramm 1954 (interactive, field of experience), transactional (simultaneous sending and receiving).
- Barriers: physical, semantic, psychological, perceptual, cultural, organisational, information overload; cure with simple language and feedback.

### Theory practice

**Question:** Using a Nigerian example, explain the elements of the communication process and show two points at which noise can occur.

*Answer outline:*

1. **Sender:** MTN wants subscribers to buy a new data bundle.
2. **Encoding:** its team turns the idea into an SMS: "Get 1.5GB for ₦1,000. Reply YES."
3. **Message:** the words and figures in the SMS.
4. **Channel:** the SMS network.
5. **Receiver and decoding:** a subscriber reads the SMS and understands the offer.
6. **Response and feedback:** she decides to buy (response) and replies "YES" (feedback, because it returns to MTN).
7. **Noise, point 1 (channel):** network failure delays or garbles the SMS (physical noise).
8. **Noise, point 2 (receiver):** she receives dozens of promotional SMSs that day and ignores this one (information overload), or does not understand an abbreviation used (semantic).
9. Close by stating that the message works best when the fields of experience of MTN and the subscriber overlap and when feedback is used to refine later messages.
