## Topic 4: Green Supply Chain

**What you must know:** what a green supply chain and green supply chain management (GSCM) mean; how GSCM differs from a traditional supply chain; the main green practices along the chain; reverse logistics, the 3Rs and the circular economy; what drives firms to go green, including Nigerian regulation and industry action; the benefits and challenges of GSCM.

### Lesson 4.1: Meaning of green supply chain management

**Must know**

- **Green supply chain:** a supply chain that is designed and run to **reduce harm to the environment** at every stage, from sourcing raw materials to the use and disposal of the product.
- **Green supply chain management (GSCM):** integrating **environmental thinking into supply chain management**, including **product design**, **sourcing and selection of materials**, **manufacturing**, **delivery of the final product** to consumers, and **end-of-life management** of the product after use (Srivastava, 2007).
- GSCM is part of the wider **sustainable supply chain management**, which covers social and economic issues as well as environmental ones.
- **Triple bottom line (John Elkington):** sustainable supply chains are judged on **people, planet and profit**, that is social, environmental and economic performance.
- **Traditional versus green supply chain:**

| Point | Traditional supply chain | Green supply chain |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Main objective | Cost, speed and profit | Cost and profit **plus** environmental protection |
| Product life | Ends when the product is sold | Includes use, return, recycling and disposal |
| Supplier selection | Price, quality, delivery | Also the supplier's environmental performance |
| Waste | Treated as a by-product | Reduced, reused or recycled |
| Direction | Forward flow only | Forward **and** reverse flow (closed loop) |

**Explain more**

- The key idea is that the firm takes responsibility for the **whole life** of the product, not just until the customer pays. A drinks company in a green supply chain thinks about the plastic bottle after the drink is finished.
- Green does not mean ignoring profit. Many green practices save money: less packaging means lower material cost, and better route planning means less fuel.
- Trap: a green supply chain is not only about recycling. Recycling is one practice; GSCM starts at **design** and **purchasing**.

### Lesson 4.2: Green supply chain practices

**Must know**

- **Main green practices along the chain:**
  1. **Green design (eco-design):** designing products to use fewer materials and less energy, and to be easy to repair, reuse or recycle.
  2. **Green purchasing (procurement):** buying environmentally friendly materials and choosing suppliers with good environmental records.
  3. **Green manufacturing (cleaner production):** reducing energy use, emissions and waste in production.
  4. **Green packaging:** less packaging, and recyclable or biodegradable materials.
  5. **Green warehousing:** energy-efficient buildings, lighting and handling equipment.
  6. **Green transportation and distribution:** fuel-efficient vehicles, fuller loads, better routes, cleaner modes (for example rail instead of road for bulk).
  7. **Reverse logistics:** collecting used products and packaging for reuse, recycling or safe disposal.
  8. **Waste management:** reducing, treating and disposing of waste safely.
  9. **Internal environmental management:** management commitment, environmental policies and systems such as **ISO 14001**.
- **ISO 14001** = the international standard for an **environmental management system (EMS)**.
- **Zhu and Sarkis** grouped GSCM practices into 5 areas: internal environmental management, green purchasing, cooperation with customers on environmental requirements, eco-design, and investment recovery (selling off excess stock, scrap and used equipment).

**Explain more**

- Match each practice to a stage: design (eco-design) → sourcing (green purchasing) → making (cleaner production) → packing (green packaging) → storing (green warehousing) → moving (green transport) → after use (reverse logistics).
- Green transport example: a distributor that sends half-empty trucks on separate trips can combine loads so that each truck travels full. Fewer trips means less diesel and fewer emissions, and lower cost too.
- Investment recovery means getting value back from things a firm no longer needs, such as selling scrap metal or old machinery, instead of dumping them.
- Oil and gas example: in the Niger Delta, **gas flaring** burns off the gas that comes up with crude oil, wasting it and polluting the air. Capturing that associated gas and using it as LPG or for power generation is a green practice: it cuts emissions and turns a waste product into something of value.

### Lesson 4.3: Reverse logistics, the 3Rs and the circular economy

**Must know**

- **Reverse logistics:** the movement of products and materials **from the customer back up the chain** for reuse, repair, remanufacturing, recycling or proper disposal.
- **The 3Rs:**
  1. **Reduce:** use less material and energy, and create less waste.
  2. **Reuse:** use products or packaging again (for example returnable bottles).
  3. **Recycle:** turn waste into raw material for new products.
- **Linear economy** = take, make, use, dispose. **Circular economy** = keep products and materials in use for as long as possible through reuse, repair, remanufacturing and recycling, so that waste is designed out.
- **Closed-loop supply chain:** a supply chain that combines the forward flow with the reverse flow, so returned products and materials go back into production.
- **Carbon footprint:** the total greenhouse gas emissions caused by a product, firm or activity.
- **GHG Protocol emission scopes:** **Scope 1** = direct emissions from sources the firm owns or controls (its own vehicles, generators, boilers); **Scope 2** = indirect emissions from the electricity or energy it buys; **Scope 3** = all other indirect emissions in its value chain, including its **suppliers**, transport by others, and the use and disposal of its products.

**Explain more**

- Nigerian example of reuse: returnable glass bottles for soft drinks and beer are collected from shops, washed and refilled. This is reverse logistics and the "reuse" R working together.
- Nigerian example of recycling: used plastic (PET) bottles are collected by recyclers, sorted and sold to firms that turn them into flakes and new products.
- A closed loop is the ideal of a green supply chain: what goes out to customers comes back as input instead of ending up in gutters and landfill.

### Lesson 4.4: Drivers of green supply chains

**Must know**

- **Drivers** = the pressures and motives that push firms to adopt GSCM:
  1. **Government regulation:** environmental laws, standards and penalties.
  2. **Customer pressure:** customers who prefer environmentally responsible brands.
  3. **Cost savings:** less energy, material, fuel and waste.
  4. **Competitive advantage and brand image:** standing out as a responsible firm.
  5. **Investor and lender pressure:** demand for good environmental, social and governance (**ESG**) performance.
  6. **Global supply chain requirements:** foreign buyers that demand environmental standards from their suppliers.
  7. **Community and NGO pressure:** public concern about pollution and waste.
- **Nigerian context:**
  - **NESREA** (National Environmental Standards and Regulations Enforcement Agency), established in **2007**, enforces environmental standards and regulations in Nigeria.
  - **Extended Producer Responsibility (EPR):** producers are responsible for their products and packaging **after use**, including collection and recycling.
  - The **Food and Beverage Recycling Alliance (FBRA)**, formed by food and beverage companies in Nigeria, works on collecting and recycling packaging waste under EPR.
  - Nigeria's **Climate Change Act** was signed into law in **2021**.

**Explain more**

- Drivers can be **internal** (management commitment, cost savings) or **external** (regulation, customers, investors, communities). Exam questions often ask you to list them, so learn the seven above.
- EPR shifts the cost of packaging waste back onto the producer. This is why beverage and packaged-food companies in Nigeria have joined together to fund collection and recycling instead of leaving plastic waste to the public.
- Large food and beverage firms in Lagos also face pressure from foreign parent companies and investors to report on sustainability, which pushes green practices down to their own suppliers.

### Lesson 4.5: Benefits and challenges of green supply chains

**Must know**

- **Benefits of GSCM:**
  1. Lower costs (energy, materials, fuel, waste disposal)
  2. Less pollution and waste, and a healthier environment
  3. Better brand image and customer loyalty
  4. Compliance with regulations and fewer fines
  5. Access to markets and buyers that demand green standards
  6. Innovation in products and processes
  7. Long-term sustainability of resources
- **Challenges of GSCM (especially in Nigeria):**
  1. High initial cost of green technology and equipment
  2. Low awareness and commitment among managers and suppliers
  3. Weak enforcement of environmental laws
  4. Poor recycling and waste collection infrastructure
  5. Suppliers lacking the capability to meet green standards
  6. Difficulty measuring environmental performance
  7. Customers unwilling to pay more for green products

**Explain more**

- Benefits often come in the long run, while costs come up front. A firm that installs solar panels at its warehouse pays a lot at the start but saves on diesel for years. This timing gap is one reason many firms are slow to go green.
- Weak enforcement and poor infrastructure are the biggest barriers in Nigeria: a firm may want to recycle its packaging, but without collection systems the material never comes back.
- Collaboration with suppliers (Topic 3) helps overcome supplier capability problems: a large buyer can train its suppliers and share green know-how.

### One-minute recap

- Green supply chain = a chain designed to reduce environmental harm at every stage.
- GSCM (Srivastava, 2007) = environmental thinking in design, sourcing, manufacturing, delivery and end-of-life management.
- Green chains add environmental goals, cover the whole product life and run forward and reverse flows.
- Practices: eco-design, green purchasing, cleaner production, green packaging, green warehousing, green transport, reverse logistics, waste management, internal environmental management (ISO 14001).
- 3Rs: reduce, reuse, recycle; circular economy and closed-loop chains keep materials in use.
- Drivers: regulation, customers, cost savings, competitive advantage, investors (ESG), global buyers, communities.
- Nigeria: NESREA (2007), EPR, FBRA, Climate Change Act (2021).
- Benefits: lower cost, less pollution, brand image, compliance, market access, innovation. Challenges: cost, awareness, enforcement, infrastructure, supplier capability, measurement, price sensitivity.

### Theory practice

**Question:** Explain five green supply chain practices a Nigerian beverage company could adopt, naming the supply chain stage each one applies to.

*Answer:* (1) **Green purchasing** at the sourcing stage: buy recycled or recyclable packaging materials and choose suppliers with good environmental records. (2) **Cleaner production** at the manufacturing stage: cut water and energy use and treat wastewater before release. (3) **Green packaging** at the packing stage: use lighter bottles, less plastic wrap and returnable glass bottles. (4) **Green transportation** at the distribution stage: plan routes and send full truckloads to cut diesel use and emissions. (5) **Reverse logistics** after use: collect empty bottles for refilling and used plastic bottles for recycling, for example through industry recycling schemes under Extended Producer Responsibility. Together these reduce environmental harm while also lowering costs and improving the brand's image.
