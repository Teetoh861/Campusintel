## Topic 2: Scope of Logistics and SCM, and the Elements of SCM

**What you must know:** how wide the scope of logistics and SCM is and the three levels of supply chain decisions; the six elements of SCM (customers, planning, purchasing, inventory, production, transportation) and what each involves; the bullwhip effect; the purchasing cycle; inventory types, costs and the EOQ formula; production approaches; the five modes of transport and how to choose between them.

### Lesson 2.1: Scope of logistics and supply chain management

**Must know**

- **Scope** = the range of activities and parties that logistics and SCM cover.
- SCM covers the whole chain **from the sourcing of raw materials to the delivery of the finished product to the final consumer, and back again** (returns and recycling).
- **Activities within the scope:**
  1. Purchasing and procurement (sourcing)
  2. Demand forecasting and planning
  3. Production and operations
  4. Inventory management
  5. Warehousing and materials handling
  6. Transportation and distribution
  7. Order processing and customer service
  8. Information management (sharing data along the chain)
  9. Reverse logistics (returns, recycling, disposal)
- **Internal scope** = activities inside the firm (production, stores, internal movement). **External scope** = links with suppliers, distributors, retailers and customers.
- **Three levels of supply chain decisions:**

| Level | Time horizon | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Strategic (design) | Long term, years | Where to build plants and warehouses, which products to make or buy, choice of key suppliers |
| Tactical (planning) | Medium term, months to a year | Demand forecasts, production and inventory plans, transport contracts |
| Operational | Short term, daily to weekly | Scheduling deliveries, filling individual orders, loading trucks |

**Explain more**

- Logistics on its own sits inside this scope (transport, warehousing, inventory, handling). SCM spreads wider, across purchasing, planning, production and customer relationships.
- Strategic decisions are expensive and hard to reverse: a company that builds a factory in Ogun State is committed to it for years. Operational decisions change every day.
- Trap: "Choosing where to locate a new distribution centre" is a **strategic** decision, not operational, even though it involves logistics.

### Lesson 2.2: Elements 1 and 2: customers and planning

**Must know**

- **The 6 elements of SCM:**
  1. Customers
  2. Planning
  3. Purchasing
  4. Inventory
  5. Production
  6. Transportation
- **Customers:** the **starting point and end point** of the supply chain. Customer demand triggers every activity, and customer satisfaction is the final test of how well the chain works.
  - **External customers** buy the final product. **Internal customers** are departments or stages inside the firm that receive work from another stage.
- **Planning:** deciding in advance **what to produce, how much, when and with which resources**, so that supply matches demand.
  - **Demand forecasting:** estimating future customer demand.
  - **Supply planning:** arranging materials, capacity and people to meet that demand.
  - **Sales and operations planning (S&OP):** bringing sales, production, purchasing and finance together to agree one plan.
- **Bullwhip effect:** small changes in **customer demand** become **larger and larger swings in orders** as you move **upstream** from retailer to wholesaler to manufacturer to supplier.
- **4 causes of the bullwhip effect (Lee, Padmanabhan and Whang, 1997):**
  1. Demand forecast updating
  2. Order batching
  3. Price fluctuations (promotions, discounts)
  4. Rationing and shortage gaming
- **Remedies:** share real sales information along the chain, order in smaller and more frequent batches, keep prices stable, and allocate stock fairly during shortages.

**Explain more**

- Planning connects customers to everything else. If a noodle manufacturer forecasts 100,000 cartons for December but customers want 150,000, shelves go empty; if it forecasts 200,000, cash is tied up in unsold stock.
- Bullwhip example: shoppers buy about 1,000 cartons of a drink a week, steady. A retailer sees a slightly busy week, worries, and orders 1,300. The distributor sees that jump and orders 1,800 to be safe. The factory sees 1,800 and buys enough sugar for 2,500. Real demand never changed much, but orders at the top swung wildly. The answer is **information sharing**: if everyone could see the real 1,000-a-week sales figure, nobody would over-order.
- Shortage gaming means customers order more than they need when they expect a shortage (for example during fuel scarcity), then cancel later. This sends false demand signals upstream.

### Lesson 2.3: Element 3: purchasing

**Must know**

- **Purchasing:** buying the materials, components, goods and services the firm needs, at the **right quality, right quantity, right price, right time and from the right source**.
- **Procurement** is wider than purchasing: it covers the whole process of identifying needs, selecting suppliers, contracting, buying, receiving and paying, and managing supplier relationships.
- **The purchasing cycle (steps):**
  1. Recognise the need (a requisition is raised)
  2. Describe the need (specification of quality and quantity)
  3. Identify and evaluate possible suppliers
  4. Select the supplier (quotations, negotiation)
  5. Place the order (purchase order)
  6. Follow up and expedite the order
  7. Receive and inspect the goods
  8. Check the invoice and pay
  9. Keep records and review supplier performance
- **Make-or-buy decision:** whether to **produce** an item internally or **buy** it from a supplier.

**Explain more**

- Purchasing is where the supplier selection points from Topic 1 are applied: capability, antecedents (track record), understanding the firm's goals, and having multiple options.
- A typical make-or-buy question: a biscuit company can print its own wrappers or buy them from a packaging firm. If the packaging firm is cheaper, reliable and better at printing, the company buys and focuses on baking. If wrappers are a key part of its brand secret or no reliable supplier exists, it may make them.
- Trap: purchasing is **one part** of procurement. "Procurement" includes supplier selection and relationship management; "purchasing" is the buying transaction itself.

### Lesson 2.4: Element 4: inventory

**Must know**

- **Inventory:** stock of materials and goods held by a firm for production or sale.
- **4 types of inventory:**
  1. Raw materials
  2. Work-in-progress (WIP): partly finished goods
  3. Finished goods
  4. MRO (maintenance, repair and operating supplies)
- **Reasons for holding inventory:** to meet expected demand, protect against uncertainty (**safety stock**), take advantage of bulk discounts, keep production running smoothly, and guard against price rises.
- **3 inventory costs:**
  1. **Holding (carrying) cost:** cost of keeping stock (storage, insurance, spoilage, money tied up).
  2. **Ordering cost:** cost of placing and receiving an order (administration, transport, inspection).
  3. **Stockout (shortage) cost:** cost of running out (lost sales, unhappy customers, idle machines).
- **Economic Order Quantity (EOQ):** the order size that **minimises total ordering and holding cost**.
  - EOQ = square root of (2 × D × S ÷ H)
  - D = annual demand (units), S = cost per order (₦), H = holding cost per unit per year (₦)
  - At the EOQ, **total annual ordering cost = total annual holding cost**.
- **Just-in-time (JIT):** materials arrive **exactly when needed** for production, so very little inventory is held.

**Explain more**

- Worked EOQ example: a bakery uses 12,000 bags of flour a year. Each order costs ₦5,000 to place and deliver, and holding one bag for a year costs ₦480.
  - EOQ = square root of (2 × 12,000 × 5,000 ÷ 480) = square root of (120,000,000 ÷ 480) = square root of 250,000 = **500 bags**.
  - Number of orders a year = 12,000 ÷ 500 = **24 orders**.
  - Annual ordering cost = 24 × ₦5,000 = **₦120,000**.
  - Average stock = 500 ÷ 2 = 250 bags; annual holding cost = 250 × ₦480 = **₦120,000**.
  - Total cost = ₦120,000 + ₦120,000 = **₦240,000**. Ordering cost equals holding cost, as expected at the EOQ.
- Too much inventory ties up cash and risks spoilage; too little causes stockouts. Inventory management is about finding the balance.
- JIT works well when suppliers are close and reliable. Where roads, ports or power are unreliable, firms often hold more safety stock than JIT would suggest.

### Lesson 2.5: Elements 5 and 6: production and transportation

**Must know**

- **Production:** the process of **transforming inputs** (materials, labour, machines, energy, information) **into outputs** (goods and services) that customers value.
- **Production approaches:**

| Approach | Meaning | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Make-to-stock | Produce in advance, then sell from stock | Bottled water, noodles |
| Make-to-order | Produce only after a customer orders | Custom furniture, tailored suits |
| Assemble-to-order | Keep parts ready, assemble after the order | Customised computers |

- **Push versus pull:** a **push** process is carried out **in anticipation of forecast demand** (make-to-stock is push). A **pull** process is **triggered by actual customer orders** (make-to-order is pull). Many chains push up to a point (for example stocking parts) and pull after it (assembling once the order comes).

- **Transportation:** the **movement of goods** from one place to another along the chain (suppliers to firm, firm to customers).
- **The 5 modes of transport:**
  1. **Road:** flexible, door-to-door, good for short and medium distances.
  2. **Rail:** cheap for heavy, bulky goods over long distances; fixed routes.
  3. **Water (sea and inland waterways):** cheapest per tonne for very large volumes; slowest.
  4. **Air:** fastest; most expensive; for urgent, light or high-value goods.
  5. **Pipeline:** for liquids and gases (crude oil, petroleum products, gas); low running cost once built.
- **Factors in choosing a mode:** cost, speed, nature of the goods (perishable, fragile, bulky, hazardous), distance, reliability, safety, flexibility, and availability of routes.
- **Intermodal transport** = using two or more modes in one journey (for example ship to port, then truck to the warehouse).

**Explain more**

- In Nigeria most freight moves by **road**, which explains the heavy truck traffic around the Apapa ports. Rail links such as the Lagos–Ibadan line and the Warri–Itakpe line can carry bulk goods more cheaply. Pipelines move petroleum products, and sea transport brings in most imports through ports in Lagos and elsewhere.
- Match the mode to the goods: fresh flowers or urgent medicines go by **air**; cement and grain in bulk go by **rail** or **water**; a supermarket restock goes by **road**; refined fuel can go by **pipeline** or tanker.
- Production and transportation are tightly linked: a make-to-stock factory needs reliable outbound transport to keep stock moving, while a make-to-order business must deliver quickly once each order is finished.

### One-minute recap

- SCM scope: from sourcing raw materials to delivering to the final consumer, and back (returns).
- Decision levels: strategic (years), tactical (months), operational (days).
- Six elements: customers, planning, purchasing, inventory, production, transportation.
- Customers start and end the chain; planning matches supply to demand (forecasting, S&OP).
- Bullwhip effect: demand swings grow upstream; 4 causes (Lee, Padmanabhan and Whang, 1997); cure is information sharing.
- Purchasing cycle has 9 steps; purchasing is part of procurement; make-or-buy.
- Inventory: 4 types, 3 costs; EOQ = square root of (2DS ÷ H); at EOQ ordering cost = holding cost.
- Production: make-to-stock, make-to-order, assemble-to-order.
- 5 transport modes: road, rail, water, air, pipeline; choose on cost, speed, goods, distance, reliability.

### Theory practice

**Question:** A drinks distributor sells 18,000 crates a year. Each order costs ₦8,000 and holding one crate for a year costs ₦320. Calculate the EOQ, the number of orders a year, and the total annual ordering and holding cost.

*Answer:* EOQ = square root of (2 × 18,000 × 8,000 ÷ 320) = square root of (288,000,000 ÷ 320) = square root of 900,000 ≈ **948.68 crates**, about **949 crates** (rounded to the nearest whole crate). Number of orders = 18,000 ÷ 948.68 ≈ **18.97**, about **19 orders a year**. Annual ordering cost = 18.97 × ₦8,000 ≈ **₦151,789**; annual holding cost = (948.68 ÷ 2) × ₦320 ≈ **₦151,789**. Total ≈ **₦303,579**. As expected at the EOQ, ordering cost and holding cost are equal.
