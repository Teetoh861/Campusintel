## Topic 2: Leadership Approaches and Practices

**What you must know:** what leadership style is and what shapes it; the four traditional styles (plus paternalistic); the contemporary styles, the four dimensions of transformational and three elements of transactional leadership; governance styles and their best contexts; leadership philosophy and the four moral leadership approaches; the seven moral leadership practices; the classical moral philosophies (teleology, egoism, utilitarianism, deontology, virtue ethics, relativism, justice) and ethical dilemmas.

### Lesson 2.1: Leadership style and what shapes it

**Must know**

- **Origin:** leadership as a concept developed in the **20th century** as an offshoot of **scientific management principles** (the idea that workers can be scientifically observed to improve their effectiveness and performance). Every organisation (large or small, public or private, manufacturing or service) needs effective leaders.
- **Leadership style:** the way a leader provides direction, implements plans and motivates people; essentially the way the leader influences followers towards a common goal. Also: the **patterns of thinking, feeling and behaviour** that people in executive positions use to handle the affairs of employees and organisations. Style is synonymous with the leader's behaviour or practices.
- There is **no one best style**. Leaders adjust style to the situation and must know when to use each one.
- **3 issues that influence the style a leader adopts:**
  1. The manager's **individual characteristics** (personality, knowledge, values, ethics, experience).
  2. The **employees** being supervised.
  3. The **organisation** (traditions, values, philosophy).
- **4 factors on which leadership style depends** (the governance view): the **environment**, the **leader**, the **followers**, the **objective or goal**.
- **Task-oriented leadership:** structuring tasks, clarifying roles, setting standards, monitoring performance.
- **Relationship-oriented leadership:** interpersonal support, trust, concern for people's welfare.

**Explain more**

- Things that do **not** shape style in exam options: the company's daily share price, the leader's ethnic origin, age, salary, office size, logo or founding date.
- If a manager gives step-by-step instructions to untrained interns but lets senior engineers decide for themselves, with the same goal and workplace, the factor at work is **the followers**.
- **Situational leadership** (Hersey and Blanchard) assumes leaders can **adjust their style flexibly** to task complexity and follower capability (readiness). It is not a fixed style.
- **Shared (distributed) leadership:** leadership roles are **distributed among group members**, not restricted to top management.
- **Cultural leadership:** leadership influence that **shapes organisational norms**, values and culture.
- Task-oriented maps to Ohio State's "initiating structure"; relationship-oriented maps to "consideration" (Topic 1).

### Lesson 2.2: Traditional leadership styles

**Must know**

**4 traditional styles:** (1) democratic or participative, (2) autocratic or authoritarian, (3) bureaucratic, (4) laissez-faire. Paternalistic is also treated as a traditional style.

- **Democratic (participative):** invites employees into decision-making; keeps subordinates informed; leader is a **coach who has the final say** but collects input first. Largely the **most effective** style, because the leader gives direction and also participates. Yields **high productivity over long periods** because employees value the **trust** they receive and respond with cooperation, team spirit and commitment.
- **Autocratic (authoritarian):** founded on the **classical approach**; leader keeps as much power and decision-making authority as possible; **unilateral decisions** with little or no group input; clear expectations of what, when and how; individual control based on the leader's own ideas, opinions and experience.
- **Bureaucratic:** strict adherence to company **policy and laid-down procedure**; anything outside the policy needs **express approval from management**; the leader acts like a **regulatory or compliance officer**.
- **Laissez-faire (delegative):** French for **"leave it alone"**; hands-off; little or no direction; **maximum autonomy**; also called "non-leadership" or **absent** leadership.
- **Paternalistic:** leader acts like a parent, deciding in what he believes is the staff's best interest and caring for their welfare, in return for loyalty and obedience.

**Bureaucratic style is effective when (5):**
1. Staff perform routine tasks over and over.
2. Employees need to understand certain principles or processes.
3. Employees work with unsafe or delicate equipment needing fixed procedures.
4. Safety training is being conducted.
5. Employees perform tasks that involve handling cash.

**Laissez-faire is characterised by (4):** minimal guidance from leaders; wide-ranging freedom for subordinates to decide; leaders provide the tools and resources required; subordinates solve problems on their own, individually or collectively.

| Style | Who decides | Merit | Demerit |
|---|---|---|---|
| Autocratic | Leader alone; one-way communication | Things get done quickly | Hurts morale |
| Democratic | Leader after consulting; two-way communication | Workers motivated and feel recognised | Slow decisions |
| Bureaucratic | The rulebook; approval from top | Consistency, safety, control | Rigid; poor for new problems |
| Laissez-faire | Followers | Creativity, initiative, entrepreneurial mindset | No direction; drift |

**Explain more**

- Bank tellers in Abuja counting about ₦40 million daily under fixed reconciliation rules suit **bureaucratic** supervision. Bureaucratic style is **not** effective where staff must improvise creative solutions to new, non-routine problems.
- Laissez-faire works when subordinates are **highly skilled, motivated and able to work on their own**, not when they are new or unwilling. In practice many laissez-faire leaders **remain open and accessible** for consultation and feedback; close supervision of every task is the opposite of laissez-faire.
- Autocrats lead by coercion, are fault-finders rather than coaches, and believe in give-and-take (reward and punishment).
- Paternalism is common in Nigerian family-owned businesses where the owner is a "father figure"; its risk is dependence and favouritism.
- Trap: **authentic**, transformational and transactional are **not** traditional styles.

### Lesson 2.3: Contemporary styles: transformational and transactional

**Must know**

**3 contemporary styles in this course:** transformational, transactional, laissez-faire. **Laissez-faire is the one style in both** the traditional and contemporary lists. Other modern approaches: authentic, servant, adaptive, distributed (shared), ethical leadership. Autocratic is **not** contemporary.

**Transformational leadership:** builds strong team performance by **empowering and motivating** subordinates to reach personal and organisational goals; inspires new ways of working, risk-taking and solutions; appeals to **higher values, purpose and a shared vision**; inspires followers to **exceed self-interest** for the good of the group.

**4 dimensions (the "4 I's"):**
1. **Idealised influence (charisma):** the **most vital element**; leader is a **good role model**, becomes valued, appreciated and trusted through personality and the beliefs he upholds.
2. **Inspirational motivation:** engaging, optimistic talk about future goals; challenges staff to high moral standards; clear expression of what must be achieved.
3. **Intellectual stimulation:** encourages followers to question established conventions, reframe problems and try new approaches.
4. **Individualised consideration:** concern for each follower's needs; tailored coaching and a supportive climate.

**Transactional leadership:** based on **exchange**: rewards for good performance, sanctions for poor performance; sets rules, focuses on goals, coordinates effort. Credited to **Max Weber (1947)**, later **expanded by Bernard Bass (1985)**.

**3 elements:**
1. **Contingent reward:** what employees must do to qualify for rewards, with punishment when targets are missed.
2. **Management by exception-active:** leader **continuously monitors** each follower's performance and corrects deviations early.
3. **Management by exception-passive:** leader **intervenes only when problems become serious** or mistakes occur; described as harmful.

**Explain more**

- **Contingent reward** belongs to transactional leadership; it is **never** a transformational dimension. **Intellectual stimulation**, individualised consideration and inspirational motivation are **never** transactional elements.
- Matching scenarios: a Yaba start-up manager urging her team to question assumptions = **intellectual stimulation**; a head of department coaching each person separately = **individualised consideration**; a CEO's optimistic rally speech demanding high moral standards = **inspirational motivation**; a leader trusted as a role model = **idealised influence**.
- A supervisor checking output all day = **MBE-active**; a branch manager who acts only when a complaint reaches head office = **MBE-passive**.
- Influence through inspiration differs from control because it relies on **emotional and value-based appeal**, not procedures or enforcement.
- **James MacGregor Burns (1978)** first contrasted transforming and transactional political leadership; **Bass (1985)** developed both into the full model.

### Lesson 2.4: Leadership styles in governance

**Must know**

| Style | Decision approach | Strengths | Limitations | Best contexts |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Democratic | Consultative, collective | Trust, legitimacy, innovation, civic engagement | Slower; conflict; needs skilled facilitation | Democratic institutions, universities, policy consultations, community governance |
| Transformational | Vision-driven | Drives innovation and reform; strong culture; social change | Vision may overshadow implementation; **overdependence on a charismatic leader** | **National reforms**, organisational change, development initiatives |
| Transactional | Structured, rule-based | Order, efficiency, clear expectations | Discourages innovation; compliance over inspiration; low emotional engagement | **Bureaucratic systems, public administration, regulatory agencies** |
| Authoritarian | Leader-controlled | Fast decisions; clear direction in emergencies; control | Abuse of power; low participation; loss of trust | **Emergencies**, military operations, crisis management |
| Servant | Consultative, value-driven | Trust, ethical governance, community participation, social cohesion | **Perceived as weak in hierarchical settings**; slower | Public service, NGOs, education, community development |

- **Servant leadership** (Robert **Greenleaf**): the leader's primary obligation is **serving the needs of followers and the community above self-interest**; emphasis on **follower development, well-being and empowerment** (including self-leadership).

**Explain more**

- Sudden Lagos flash floods requiring evacuation within hours call for **authoritarian** leadership: speed matters more than consultation.
- Democratic leadership's key features: open communication, collective decision-making, respect for diverse opinions, transparency, delegation.
- Transactional features: performance monitoring, clear rules, reward and punishment, short-term goals.

### Lesson 2.5: Leadership philosophy and moral leadership

**Must know**

- **Moral philosophy** is the **ethical foundation** of leadership: it guides leaders to put **purpose, integrity and service** first in decisions and in how they treat people.
- **Leadership philosophy:** a foundational framework setting out an individual's **core beliefs, values and principles** about leadership. It acts as a **compass** guiding decisions, behaviour and interactions.
- **4 elements of a leadership philosophy:** theory, attitude, guiding principles, behaviour.

**4 leadership approaches rooted in moral philosophy:**
1. **Ethical leadership:** respect, accountability, honesty, justice and service, so decisions benefit employees, customers, investors, suppliers, regulators and communities.
2. **Servant leadership:** puts subordinates' needs and growth first ("people first").
3. **Authentic leadership:** **self-awareness, relational transparency, balanced processing, strong moral perspective**; influence from credibility, personal values and consistency rather than formal authority.
4. **Virtuous leadership:** character traits of **courage, integrity, humility, compassion and wisdom**, guided by moral excellence.

**4 ways moral philosophy influences leadership:**
1. **Decision-making:** a framework (fairness, justice, empathy) for choices aligned with **ethical values rather than expediency**.
2. **Trust and credibility:** ethical, transparent leaders earn deeper trust and respect.
3. **Purpose-driven action:** links leadership to a wider purpose, showing what is right and good.
4. **Human-centred approach:** puts people at the centre, recognising their humanity, aspirations and concerns; teams become more engaged and loyal.

**7 moral leadership practices:**
1. **Start with a pause:** reflect, reconnect with moral commitments, rethink, reimagine.
2. **See the humanity in everyone:** people are **ends, not means**; learn their hopes and struggles.
3. **Foster freedom:** a culture of interdependence and responsibility, shared values, support for informed risks.
4. **Demonstrate humility:** confident when warranted, admit the limits of one's knowledge, make amends for mistakes.
5. **Uphold ethical standards:** model integrity even when unpopular; testing situations are **opportunities to reveal what one most values**, not constraints.
6. **Seek the truth:** sincere, rigorous effort to find the truth; weigh multiple perspectives.
7. **Act with courage:** never trade principles for short-term gain; indifference is the greatest risk.

**Explain more**

- **Bureaucratic** leadership is **not** a moral-philosophy approach. Remuneration is **not** an element of a leadership philosophy.
- A managing director who publicly admits the limits of his knowledge after a failed decision = **demonstrate humility**. A procurement director refusing a ₦30 million "gift" despite angry board members = **act with courage**.

### Lesson 2.6: Moral philosophies and ethical dilemmas

**Must know**

- **Moral philosophy:** the principles or rules people use to decide what is right or wrong.
- **Teleology (consequentialism):** an act is right if it produces a desired result; rightness is judged **solely by consequences**.
  - **Egoism:** right = what maximises **one's own self-interest**. Enlightened egoism also considers others' well-being where it serves long-term self-interest.
  - **Utilitarianism** (Jeremy **Bentham**, John Stuart **Mill**): right = **the greatest good for the greatest number**. **Rule** utilitarians follow rules that usually maximise good; **act** utilitarians judge each act.
- **Deontology** (Immanuel **Kant**): focus on **duties, rights and principles regardless of outcomes**; **fairness and moral duty**. Kant's **categorical imperative**: act only on a rule you would want to become a universal law; treat people always as ends, never merely as means.
- **Virtue ethics** (Aristotle): good conduct flows from the **character and virtues** of the person (courage, honesty, prudence, justice).
- **Relativism:** moral standards are judged by the individual or **cultural context**; no universal rule.
- **Ethical absolutism:** moral rules are **unbreakable in every situation** (the opposite of relativism and situational ethics).
- **Justice:** fairness. **Distributive** (fair share of outcomes), **procedural** (fair process), **interactional** (fair treatment and communication).
- **Ethical dilemma:** a situation with two or more choices, each carrying ethical costs, where no option is clearly right.

| Philosophy | Asks | Key name |
|---|---|---|
| Utilitarianism | Which act gives the greatest good for the greatest number? | Bentham, Mill |
| Egoism | What is best for me? | |
| Deontology | What is my duty, whatever the outcome? | Kant |
| Virtue ethics | What would a person of good character do? | Aristotle |
| Relativism | What does my culture or group accept? | |
| Justice | Is it fair in outcome, process and treatment? | Rawls |

**Explain more**

- **Divine command theory** (right is what God commands) and **care ethics** (right is what preserves caring relationships) are distractors, not the answers to "judged solely by consequences" or "moral duty".
- Dilemma example: a Lagos plant manager can save ₦50 million by quietly skipping a costly safety upgrade. A utilitarian weighs total harm against total gain; a Kantian says protecting workers is a duty whatever the savings; a virtue ethicist asks what an honest, courageous manager would do.
- Resolving a dilemma: recognise the issue, gather facts, identify stakeholders and options, test options against the philosophies, decide, act and review.

### One-minute recap

- Leadership style = how a leader directs and influences; no one best style. Shaped by the leader, followers, environment and goal.
- Traditional: democratic, autocratic, bureaucratic, laissez-faire (plus paternalistic).
- Contemporary: transformational, transactional, laissez-faire (laissez-faire is in both lists).
- Transformational 4 I's: idealised influence (most vital), inspirational motivation, intellectual stimulation, individualised consideration.
- Transactional 3: contingent reward, MBE-active, MBE-passive. Weber 1947, Bass 1985.
- Governance: transformational for reforms; transactional for bureaucracies; authoritarian for emergencies; servant for public service.
- Leadership philosophy = a compass with 4 elements: theory, attitude, guiding principles, behaviour.
- Moral approaches: ethical, servant, authentic, virtuous. Seven moral practices from "start with a pause" to "act with courage".
- Consequences = teleology/utilitarianism; duty = deontology (Kant); character = virtue ethics; culture = relativism.

### Theory practice

**Question:** Distinguish transformational from transactional leadership and list the components of each.

*Answer:* Transformational leadership inspires followers to go beyond self-interest by appealing to shared values and vision. Its four dimensions are idealised influence (charisma, the most vital), inspirational motivation, intellectual stimulation and individualised consideration. Transactional leadership rests on exchange: rewards for good performance and sanctions for poor performance. Its three elements are contingent reward, management by exception-active (constant monitoring) and management by exception-passive (stepping in only when problems are serious). Credit Weber (1947) and Bass (1985).
