## Topic 4: Social Contract and Ethics

**What you must know:** what a social contract is and its examples; Hobbes, Locke, Rousseau and Rawls (books, state of nature, who holds power); social contract ethics and the meaning and principles of ethics; the 8 public sector and 6 private sector ethical issues and their remedies; codes of conduct, corruption and accountability in Nigeria (CCB, CCT, EFCC, ICPC).

### Lesson 4.1: The social contract: meaning and examples

**Must know**

- **Social contract:** when a group of people agree to **give up certain rights** and **accept a central authority** in order to **protect their other rights**.
- It is also an **implicit agreement** among the members of a society to **cooperate for social benefits**, for example by **sacrificing some individual freedom for state protection** (this is the trade-off at its heart).
- The social contract is **what allows any government to work**, but **government and the social contract are different things**.
- **Social contract theory:** people live together in society in accordance with **an agreement that establishes moral and political rules of behaviour**.
- Theories of the social contract became popular in the **16th, 17th and 18th centuries** (Thomas **Hobbes**, John **Locke**, Jean-Jacques **Rousseau**) as a means of explaining **the origin of government and the obligations of subjects**.

**Examples of the social contract** (grouped so you can remember them):

| Group | Examples |
|---|---|
| Political rights and democracy | voting, democracy, right to vote, right to run for political office, political rights |
| Freedoms | constitutional freedoms, freedom of speech, expression, religion, assembly and movement; personal sovereignty; right to privacy |
| Legal protection | legal rights, right to a fair trial, protection under the law, equal protection under the law |
| Economic and social | economic rights, right to education, government services, social safety net |
| Civic duties | civic duty, jury duty, census participation, taxes |
| State authority | laws, regulations, government authority, emergency measures that override freedoms, international agreements |
| Administrative requirements | registering births, obtaining government identification |
| Business expectations | corporate social responsibility, consumer protection, environmental protection |

**Explain more**

- **Rights vs duties:** jury duty, census participation, paying taxes and civic duty are **duties** you owe; a fair trial, privacy and freedom of religion are **rights** you receive. An exam may ask which item is a duty: jury duty.
- **Registering births and getting a National Identification Number** are listed as **administrative requirements**, not freedoms or economic rights.
- Because **CSR, consumer protection and environmental protection** appear in the list, the social contract also shapes **what society expects of businesses**, not only of governments.
- **Curfew example:** during violent unrest a state government imposes a dusk-to-dawn curfew; residents accept a temporary limit on movement so lives and property are protected. That is the social contract (some freedom given up for state protection), and also an example of "emergency measures that override freedoms".
- **Tax example:** a Lagos trader pays ₦250,000 in tax a year and expects security, roads and courts in return. That exchange is the social contract.
- **Breach of the social contract:** a government that **systematically denies citizens basic rights and public services** breaks its side of the bargain. Passing tax laws, holding free and fair elections and setting up an independent judiciary are the contract working, not breaches.
- The contract is **implicit**: it is not a written document signed by every citizen, not a trade agreement between countries, and not an employment contract with the state.

### Lesson 4.2: Hobbes, Locke, Rousseau and Rawls

**Must know**

| Theorist | Main work | State of nature | Who holds power / key idea |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Thomas Hobbes** (English) | *Leviathan* (1651) | "war of all against all"; life is "**solitary, poor, nasty, brutish and short**" | People surrender their rights to a **strong, absolute sovereign** that guarantees peace and security |
| **John Locke** (English) | *Two Treatises of Government* (1689) | governed by **natural law and reason**, fairly peaceful but **insecure** (no impartial judge) | Government by **consent** exists to protect natural rights to **life, liberty and property**; if it violates them, the people may **resist and replace** it |
| **Jean-Jacques Rousseau** (Geneva-born) | *The Social Contract* (1762) | people are **free and equal**, later corrupted by society and private property | Sovereignty lies with the people; legitimate laws must reflect the **general will**; legitimacy comes from the **consent and agreement of the governed** |
| **John Rawls** (American) | *A Theory of Justice* (1971) | replaced by the **original position** behind a **veil of ignorance** | **Justice as fairness**: rules chosen without knowing your own place in society |

- **Rawls' two principles of justice:** (1) **equal basic liberties** for all; (2) social and economic inequalities are allowed only if they are attached to positions open to all under **fair equality of opportunity** and benefit the **least advantaged** (the **difference principle**).
- Classical social contract theorists = Hobbes, Locke, Rousseau. **Not** social contract theorists: Karl **Marx**, Niccolò **Machiavelli**, Max Weber, and leadership scholars such as Kelley, Bass and Burns.

**Explain more**

- **Hobbes vs Locke:** Hobbes believed a **powerful sovereign** was necessary to prevent the brutish state of nature; people keep almost no rights against it. Locke believed people **keep their natural rights** after forming a government. Trap: "Locke saw it as a war of all against all" is reversed; that is Hobbes.
- **Hobbes vs Rousseau:** Hobbes favoured an **absolute sovereign**; Rousseau placed sovereignty in the **general will of the people**.
- Rousseau's famous opening line: "**Man is born free, and everywhere he is in chains.**"
- **Property** as the foundation of political obligation points to **Locke**; the **general will** points to **Rousseau**; **"solitary, poor, nasty, brutish and short"** and *Leviathan* point to **Hobbes**; **veil of ignorance** points to **Rawls**.
- Divine right of kings, military strength and inherited privilege are the very ideas the social contract theorists replaced with **consent**.
- Locke's right to resist is often linked with later constitutional democracy; in Nigeria the idea that power belongs to the people appears in the 1999 Constitution, which says sovereignty belongs to the people of Nigeria, from whom government derives all its powers and authority.

### Lesson 4.3: Social contract ethics, meaning and principles of ethics

**Must know**

- **Ethics:** the moral principles and standards that guide what is right and wrong in behaviour and decisions. Leadership ethics guides decision-making by providing **moral principles and standards**; the ethical dimension of leadership emphasises **moral responsibility and integrity**.
- **Social contract ethics** (contractarian ethics): moral and social rules are justified because rational people **would agree to them**.

**Key ideas of social contract ethics:**

1. **Laws are justified** when they **align with principles citizens would accept**.
2. **Goal:** to make social rules **fair and acceptable**.
3. **Authority** of a leader rests on **collective agreement and legitimacy**.
4. **Rights** = **mutually recognised protections**.
5. **Moral obligation** = a **rationally accepted constraint**.
6. Moral rules gain strength from **shared endorsement**.
7. Moral norms are sustained through **mutual expectation and trust**.
8. **Injustice** occurs when **agreements favour some unfairly**.

**Principles of ethics** (in public and organisational life):

1. **Integrity** 2. **Honesty** 3. **Accountability** 4. **Transparency/openness** 5. **Fairness and justice** 6. **Objectivity** (decisions on merit) 7. **Respect** for persons 8. **Selflessness** (public interest first) 9. **Responsibility/stewardship** of resources.

**Explain more**

- The UK's **Nolan Committee (1995)** set out seven principles of public life widely used in codes of conduct: **selflessness, integrity, objectivity, accountability, openness, honesty, leadership**.
- Social contract ethics is not based on **personal moral intuition, inherited virtue, religious doctrine, tradition, fear or emotion**. Those are the usual wrong options. Nor are laws justified merely because they "promote stability regardless of fairness" or "preserve existing hierarchies".
- Rawls fits here: his veil of ignorance is a test for whether rules are fair enough that everyone would accept them.
- **CSR** reflects a **stakeholder-oriented** view: business has responsibilities **beyond profit**, not pure profit maximisation or tax compliance only.

### Lesson 4.4: Ethical issues in the public and private sectors

**Must know**

- **Public sector** ethical issues centre on **abuse of authority for personal gain, conflicts of interest, corruption and failure to act in the public interest**. Bribery, nepotism, mismanagement of public funds and weak accountability **erode public trust**.

**8 key ethical issues in the public sector** (some lists give 7, leaving out data privacy; learn all 8):

1. **Corruption and bribery:** using public office for private gain: accepting kickbacks, stealing public funds, manipulating contracts.
2. **Conflict of interest:** officials decide to benefit themselves, family or associates rather than the public.
3. **Nepotism and favouritism:** hiring, promoting or awarding contracts to friends or family on relationships, not merit.
4. **Misuse of power/authority:** intimidating, harassing or discriminating against people.
5. **Lack of accountability and transparency:** concealing information, making fraudulent reports, avoiding scrutiny.
6. **Data privacy violations:** improper handling of, or unauthorised access to, confidential citizen or government information.
7. **Ethical dilemmas in decision-making:** choosing between personal values, political pressure and professional responsibilities, often leading to poor administrative judgement.
8. **Low integrity/work ethic:** absenteeism, laziness, lack of dedication to public service.

**Remedies (4):** strong **anti-corruption laws**, **whistleblower protection**, improved **transparency**, **merit-based administration**.

- **Private sector** ethical issues primarily arise from **profit-driven decision-making**, and often stem from **intense pressure for performance, leading to unethical shortcuts** that risk legal consequences and reputational damage.

**6 key ethical issues in the private sector:**

1. **Workplace harassment and discrimination** (race, gender, religion).
2. **Conflicts of interest** (personal interests such as nepotism compromise judgement).
3. **Data privacy and security** (misuse or weak protection of customers' personal and financial data).
4. **Financial integrity and fraud** (dishonest reporting, bribery, manipulation to hit targets).
5. **Environmental and social responsibility** failures (environmental harm; unsafe, unfair labour in the supply chain).
6. **Anti-competitive behaviour** (e.g. price-fixing).

**Explain more**

- **Why the sectors differ:** public officials are **stewards of public resources** and owe **accountability to all citizens**; private firms answer mainly to owners but, through **corporate governance ethics**, must act with **integrity, transparency and accountability to all stakeholders**, beyond mere legal compliance.
- **In both lists:** **conflicts of interest** and **data privacy**. Specific to the public sector: using public office for private gain, absenteeism from public service. Specific to the private sector: price-fixing, fraudulent company accounts, supply-chain exploitation.
- **Classify the case:**

| Case | Issue |
|---|---|
| Works officer demands 10% of a ₦20 million contract in cash | Corruption and bribery |
| Commissioner approves land to a firm he secretly owns shares in | Conflict of interest |
| Director promotes his niece over three better-qualified officers | Nepotism and favouritism |
| Officer intimidates a junior who refused to falsify a report | Misuse of power |
| Registry staff leak citizens' records on social media | Data privacy violation |
| Staff sign in at 8 a.m., leave to run private businesses | Low integrity/work ethic |
| Agency conceals information, files fraudulent reports | Lack of accountability and transparency |
| Three cement makers agree not to sell below ₦9,500 a bag | Anti-competitive behaviour |
| Listed company books ₦800 million of fake sales | Financial integrity and fraud |
| Firm dumps waste in a river and buys from child-labour suppliers | Environmental and social responsibility |
| Employee passed over because of her religion | Harassment and discrimination |
| Fintech app leaks customers' card details and BVNs | Data privacy and security |

- **Worked figure:** the bribe demanded is 10% of ₦20,000,000 = 0.10 × ₦20,000,000 = **₦2,000,000**.
- **Remedy matching:** relatives being given posts → **merit-based administration**. Whistleblower protection works by **encouraging insiders to report wrongdoing without fear of reprisal**. **Restricting citizens' access to information** is never a remedy. Taking approved annual leave is not corruption.

### Lesson 4.5: Codes of conduct, corruption and accountability in Nigeria

**Must know**

- **Code of conduct:** a written set of rules and standards stating how members of an organisation or public officers must behave (e.g. on gifts, conflicts of interest, asset declaration).
- **Corruption:** the **abuse of entrusted power for private gain**. Forms: **bribery, embezzlement, nepotism, fraud, extortion, abuse of resources**. Types: **petty** (small, everyday), **grand** (high-level, large sums), **political** (manipulating policies or elections).
- **Accountability:** the obligation of leaders and institutions to **explain their actions, accept responsibility** for decisions, and **face evaluation or consequences** when performance falls short. Mechanisms: **performance monitoring, legal oversight, public reporting, institutional checks and balances**.

| Body / law | What it does |
|---|---|
| **Code of Conduct for Public Officers** (Fifth Schedule, 1999 Constitution) | Rules for public officers: no conflict of interest, no bribes or improper gifts, asset declaration |
| **Code of Conduct Bureau (CCB)** | Receives and checks public officers' **asset declarations**; refers breaches to the CCT |
| **Code of Conduct Tribunal (CCT)** | Tries breaches of the Code of Conduct |
| **EFCC** (Economic and Financial Crimes Commission; Act of 2004, first set up 2002) | Investigates and prosecutes **economic and financial crimes**: fraud, money laundering, advance-fee fraud |
| **ICPC** (Independent Corrupt Practices and Other Related Offences Commission; Act of 2000) | Fights **corruption, especially in the public sector**: bribery, gratification; also prevention and public education |

**Explain more**

- Public officers declare their assets to the CCB on taking office, every four years, and at the end of their term.
- Other Nigerian accountability tools: the **Public Procurement Act 2007** (Bureau of Public Procurement, "due process"), the **Freedom of Information Act 2011**, the federal **whistleblower policy (2016)**, and legislative oversight by the National and State Assemblies.
- Effects of corruption: **weakens institutions, reduces public trust, limits economic development, undermines fairness and justice**. Leadership response: **strengthen accountability systems, promote transparency, enforce ethical standards**.
- Trap: the **CCB** handles asset declarations; the **CCT** is the court that tries breaches. **EFCC** = financial crimes broadly; **ICPC** = corrupt practices, mainly public sector.

### One-minute recap

- Social contract = give up some rights, accept a central authority, protect other rights; implicit; not the same as government.
- Popular in the 16th-18th centuries; explains the origin of government and the obligations of subjects.
- Hobbes (*Leviathan*): brutish state of nature, absolute sovereign. Locke (*Two Treatises*): life, liberty, property; right to resist.
- Rousseau (*The Social Contract*): general will, "born free... in chains". Rawls (*A Theory of Justice*): veil of ignorance.
- Social contract ethics: rules people would accept; fair and acceptable; shared endorsement; mutual trust.
- Public sector issues: corruption, conflict of interest, nepotism, misuse of power, poor accountability, data privacy, dilemmas, low work ethic.
- Private sector issues come from profit pressure: harassment, conflicts, data, fraud, environment, anti-competitive behaviour.
- CCB (assets), CCT (tries breaches), EFCC (financial crimes), ICPC (corrupt practices).

### Theory practice

**Question:** Compare the social contract theories of Thomas Hobbes and John Locke.

*Answer outline:* Define the social contract (giving up some rights to a central authority to protect others). Hobbes, *Leviathan*: state of nature a war of all against all, life "solitary, poor, nasty, brutish and short"; people surrender rights to an absolute sovereign for peace and security; almost no right to resist. Locke, *Two Treatises of Government*: state of nature ruled by natural law and reason but insecure; government by consent protects life, liberty and property; if it violates them, people may resist and replace it. Conclude: Hobbes stresses order, Locke stresses limited government and rights.
