## Topic 1: An Overview of Leadership

**What you must know:** what leadership means and how it differs from management; power, authority and the five bases of power; the trait, behavioural, contingency and contemporary theories and who proposed each; the qualities and characteristics of effective leaders; the core components, functions and roles of leadership; leadership challenges in governance.

### Lesson 1.1: Meaning of leadership, and leadership versus management

**Must know**

- **Leadership:** the **process of influencing others to achieve shared (common) goals** for collective development. The best one-line answer: "the process of influencing others to achieve a common goal."
- **Leadership (as an art):** the art of influencing others to direct their abilities and efforts towards the group's goals. In business: management's ability to induce subordinates to work towards group goals with confidence.
- The **core element** in almost every definition is the ability to **influence behaviour**. **Influence** = the leader's capacity to **shape attitudes, beliefs and behaviours**.
- **Relational process:** leadership **emerges through interaction between leaders and followers in a specific context**; it is not a one-person activity. It is **dynamic** because of this ongoing leader-follower interaction.
- Effective leadership depends on **4 things:** **trust, communication, legitimacy, shared commitment to common objectives**.
- Leadership is a **social influence process**, not confined to formal positions. Modern thought assumes leadership can be **learned and developed over time**.
- Leadership matters in governance because leaders **shape institutional direction, set ethical standards, coordinate collective action and manage competing interests**. The quality of leadership determines **policy direction, institutional performance and citizens' trust** in institutions.

| Leadership | Management |
|---|---|
| Copes with **change** | Copes with **complexity** |
| Long-term **vision and direction** | Detailed operational control, planning and budgeting |
| Aligns and inspires people | Organises, staffs, controls |
| "Doing the right thing" | "Doing things right" |
| Rests on influence, trust, legitimacy | Rests on formal position and rules |

**Explain more**

- The defining feature that separates leadership from management is leadership's emphasis on **long-term vision and direction** (John Kotter: management copes with complexity, leadership copes with change; Warren Bennis: managers do things right, leaders do the right thing). A good executive needs both.
- Ralph **Stogdill** (1974) observed that there are almost as many definitions of leadership as there are people who have tried to define it. Do not worry about one exact wording: any option that says "influencing others towards a shared or common goal" is the right one. Options about commanding obedience, managing resources for profit or delegating efficiently are traps.
- Leadership as an idea grew in the 20th century as an offshoot of scientific management.

### Lesson 1.2: Power, authority and the bases of power

**Must know**

- A leader needs both **power** and **authority**.
- **Power:** the ability to influence others. It can come from **personal sources** such as **intelligence, wealth, even beauty**.
- **Authority:** the **right to command**. It is conferred by **culture, society and the organisation**; in a company it is conferred through the **board of directors**.
- **Qualities of authority (4):** it must be (1) **clear**, (2) **feasible**, (3) **consistent with individual belief and organisational purpose**, (4) **capable of being carried out**.

**French and Raven's 5 bases of power** (1959):

1. **Legitimate:** from **organisational position** (formal right to give orders).
2. **Reward:** from control of **incentives** (pay rise, bonus, promotion). Appeals to extrinsic motivation.
3. **Coercive:** from the **threat of punishment** (query, demotion, pay cut, dismissal).
4. **Expert:** from **expertise**, special knowledge or skill shown consistently.
5. **Referent:** from **personal attraction and identification**; followers admire the leader.

**Explain more**

- Legitimate, reward and coercive power are **positional** (they come with the office). Expert and referent power are **personal**; they last beyond the job title. Raven later added **information power**.
- A supervisor who says anyone who misses the quota gets a query and may lose part of a ₦80,000 allowance is using **coercive** power; offering the ₦80,000 as a bonus for hitting the quota would be **reward** power.
- A trap swaps the definitions: power is **not** "the right to command conferred by the board"; that is **authority**. Authority should never be kept secret from subordinates.
- Missing clarity example: a branch manager told to "handle staff matters as necessary" with no word on which leave, discipline or hiring decisions she may take lacks **clarity** of authority.

### Lesson 1.3: Trait and behavioural theories

**Must know**

- **Great Man Hypothesis:** leaders are **born, not made**. It is the basis of the **trait approach**.
- **Trait theory:** leaders are **born with inherent personal attributes** that distinguish them from non-leaders.

**Trait groups (4):**

1. **Physical:** height, strength, vigour, appearance.
2. **Intelligence:** ability to comprehend, conceptualise, plan, think.
3. **Task-related ability:** initiative, administrative ability.
4. **Personality:** interpersonal skills, aggressiveness, adaptiveness, quiet, introvert and extrovert.

**Limitations of trait theory:** (1) **no basis for comparing the traits**; (2) **ignores situational factors**; also, researchers never agreed on a consistent list of traits that guarantees effective leadership.

- **Behavioural theory:** studies **what leaders actually do** in practice, not their innate qualities. Limitation: it has **not found one style that works in every situation**.

| Behavioural type | What it looks like | Merit | Demerit |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Autocratic** | No participation; subordinates passive; believes in transaction (reward and punishment); leads by coercion; a fault-finder, not a coach; **one-way** communication | Things get done **quickly** | Hurts workers' **morale** |
| **Democratic** | Trusts and involves subordinates; a coach; **two-way** communication; expects feedback | Workers motivated and feel recognised | **Slow** decision-making |
| **Laissez-faire** | Lets things drift; sets no goals or directives; leader absent; individuals decide alone | Boosts **creativity and personal initiative**; entrepreneurial mindset | No direction; group easily **drifts** |

**Classic behavioural studies:**

1. **University of Iowa (Kurt Lewin, 1939):** autocratic, democratic, laissez-faire.
2. **Ohio State studies (late 1940s):** **initiating structure** (organising tasks, roles, deadlines) and **consideration** (friendship, respect for ideas, concern for welfare).
3. **University of Michigan (Rensis Likert):** **employee-centred** versus **job (production)-centred**.
4. **Managerial Grid (Blake and Mouton, 1964):** concern for production (first number) and concern for people (second number), each 1 to 9.

| Grid position | Style |
|---|---|
| 1,1 | Impoverished |
| 9,1 | Authority-compliance (task) |
| 1,9 | Country club |
| 5,5 | Middle-of-the-road |
| 9,9 | Team management (the ideal) |

**Explain more**

- Stogdill's review of trait studies (1948) found no trait set that made someone a leader in every situation; this pushed research towards behaviour and situations.
- Trap: "social-class traits such as family name and inheritance" is **not** a trait group. "Anyone can become a leader through training" is **not** a limitation of trait theory; it is the opposite of what trait theory claims.
- Pairing traps: initiative = task-related (not personality); aggressiveness = personality; vigour = physical; ability to plan = intelligence.
- Case: a Lagos MD who did well in stable years but failed in a currency crisis with the same qualities shows that trait theory **ignores situational factors**. Two consultants unable to agree how to measure who has more "vigour" shows **no basis for comparing traits**.
- A friendly Ikeja supervisor who respects workers' ideas and cares about their welfare scores high on **consideration**. A manager with relaxed rules and parties but missed targets is **country club (1,9)**.
- The Managerial Grid is **behavioural**, not contingency.

### Lesson 1.4: Contingency and situational theories

**Must know**

- **Contingency view:** **no single style is universally effective**; effectiveness depends on the **match between leadership style and situational factors**.

**Fiedler's contingency model (Fred Fiedler, 1967):**

- The **Least Preferred Co-worker (LPC) scale** shows whether a leader is **task-oriented** (low LPC) or **relationship-oriented** (high LPC).
- **3 situational factors:** (1) **leader-member relations**, (2) **task structure**, (3) **position power**.
- Task-oriented leaders do best in **very favourable or very unfavourable** situations; relationship-oriented leaders do best in **moderately favourable** ones.
- Style is **fixed**, so **match the leader to the situation or change the situation**.

**Hersey and Blanchard's Situational Leadership Theory (1969):** adapt style to followers' **maturity (readiness)** and task demands.

| Follower readiness | Style | Task behaviour | Relationship behaviour |
|---|---|---|---|
| R1: unable and unwilling or insecure | **S1 Telling** (directing) | High | Low |
| R2: unable but willing or confident | **S2 Selling** (coaching) | High | High |
| R3: able but unwilling or insecure | **S3 Participating** (supporting) | Low | High |
| R4: able and willing, confident | **S4 Delegating** | Low | Low |

**Path-goal theory (Robert House, 1971):** the leader's job is to **clarify the paths to goals and remove obstacles** for followers. **4 styles:** **directive, supportive, participative, achievement-oriented**. The right one depends on follower characteristics and the task environment.

**Vroom-Yetton normative decision model (1973):** how far to let followers **participate in a decision**, from deciding alone (autocratic) through consulting to group decision, chosen by answering questions about decision quality and follower acceptance.

**Explain more**

- "Follower maturity or readiness" is a Hersey and Blanchard idea, **not** one of Fiedler's three factors.
- A fresh graduate trainee in a Port Harcourt firm, unable to do a new task and unsure of himself, is R1, so use **telling**. A skilled, confident senior accountant keen to own month-end close is R4, so use **delegating**.
- Path-goal styles are **not** coercive, autocratic or bureaucratic. Path-goal is **not** about applying reward and punishment.
- Classification trap: Fiedler, Hersey-Blanchard, path-goal and Vroom are contingency theories; Ohio State, Michigan, Iowa and the Grid are behavioural; Great Man is trait.

### Lesson 1.5: Contemporary theories of leadership

**Must know**

- **James MacGregor Burns (1978)**, a political scientist, first distinguished **transforming** from **transactional** leadership. **Bernard Bass (1985)** expanded both. Max **Weber (1947)** is credited with bringing the transactional notion into the leadership field.
- **Transformational:** empowers and inspires followers to go beyond self-interest and achieve personal and organisational goals. **4 I's:** (1) **idealised influence (charisma)**, (2) **inspirational motivation**, (3) **intellectual stimulation**, (4) **individualised consideration**.
- **Transactional:** based on **exchange**: rewards for good performance, sanctions for poor. **3 elements:** (1) **contingent reward**, (2) **management by exception-active**, (3) **management by exception-passive**.
- **Laissez-faire:** "leave it alone"; non-leadership.
- **Charismatic:** influence through the leader's personality, vision and role-modelling (Weber's idea of charisma; developed by Robert House, 1976).
- **Servant:** puts followers' needs and growth first ("people first"). **Robert Greenleaf (1970)**.
- **Authentic:** self-awareness, relational transparency, balanced processing, strong moral perspective.

**Explain more**

- Transformational leadership drives reform and builds a strong culture, but risks over-dependence on charismatic leaders. Transactional leadership gives order and clear expectations but discourages innovation and is short-term. Management by exception-passive (acting only when problems become serious) is harmful.
- Do not confuse Burns (transforming leadership, 1978) with **Robert Kelley** (followership), **Fred Fiedler** (contingency) or **Robert Blake** (Managerial Grid).

### Lesson 1.6: Qualities, characteristics, functions and roles

**Must know**

**Qualities of an effective leader:** decisive; has a **vision**; empathetic, with conceptual skills, understandable; interpersonal skill.

- **Vision:** a **mental picture** of what someone aims to achieve within a time frame, given constraints. In leadership, vision serves to **provide long-term direction** for the group. From broadest to most concrete: **vision, goal, objective**. Example: vision = quality education; goal = university education; objective = a four-year course.

**Characteristics of effective leaders (6):**

1. **Vision and strategic thinking:** long-term goals, anticipating risks, spotting trends, aligning resources.
2. **Integrity and ethical responsibility:** the foundation of **credibility**; avoiding corruption and abuse of power.
3. **Communication skills:** enhances cooperation and reduces misunderstandings.
4. **Emotional intelligence:** **understanding and managing one's own emotions while recognising the feelings of others**; handles stress, resolves conflict constructively, shows empathy.
5. **Adaptability and innovation:** supports **institutional resilience**.
6. **Accountability and responsibility:** evaluating outcomes honestly, admitting mistakes.

**Core components (functions) of leadership (5):**

1. **Providing direction:** vision, achievable objectives, clarifying roles, aligning efforts.
2. **Motivating followers** (the motivating and inspiring function: getting people to work willingly towards a shared vision): recognising achievements, meaningful goals, supportive environment, empathy and fairness, trust.
3. **Making decisions:** critical thinking, stakeholder consultation, evaluating alternatives, risk assessment, accountability.
4. **Maintaining ethical standards:** transparency, fairness and justice, no corruption, respect for diversity and human rights, responsible use of resources.
5. **Managing change and conflict:** clear communication, dialogue, addressing resistance, negotiating, stability.

- **Roles of a leader:** be **firm and decisive**; **bring people together**. **Leadership role** = the **behaviours expected of a leader** in an organisational or social context. The **directing** function = **motivating and guiding** subordinates.

**Leadership challenges in governance (5):**

| Challenge | Meaning or effect | Leadership response |
|---|---|---|
| Corruption and misuse of power | Authority used for personal gain | Accountability, transparency, ethics |
| Lack of transparency | Suspicion, low participation | Open channels, **public reporting**, inclusive talks |
| Weak institutions | Poor service; reliance on **informal networks** | Reform, capacity building |
| Political interference | Partisan pressure; lower autonomy | Professional ethics, safeguards, **merit-based decisions** |
| Low citizen trust | Caused by scandals, poor service, no accountability | Integrity, **tangible results**, participation |

**Explain more**

- Without direction, organisations suffer **confusion, inefficiency and conflict**. Good conflict management **prevents social unrest and promotes inclusive decision-making**. Ethical failures cause **governance crises and institutional breakdown**.
- Odd-one-out traps: preparing statutory financial statements is not a core component; relying only on instructions is not motivating; choosing the first option to save time is not good decision-making; hiding decisions is not ethical; shifting blame under stress is not emotional intelligence; authoritarian imposition of views or keeping away from subordinates is not a quality of a leader.
- Matching cases: a Lagos MD setting a five-year vision and quarterly objectives = **providing direction**. A commissioner holding town halls and negotiating a phased levy compromise = **managing change and conflict**. An Ibadan CEO studying trends before investing ₦500 million = **vision and strategic thinking**. A registrar admitting a portal crash was poorly planned = **accountability and responsibility**. A head deciding firmly the same afternoon = **decisiveness**. An Abuja MD ending a feud between sales and finance = **bringing people together**. A senator pushing party loyalists for agency jobs = **political interference**. A council hiding its ₦2 billion budget needs **public reporting systems**. Delivering tangible results **builds** trust; it is never a cause of low trust.

### One-minute recap

- Leadership = the process of influencing others to achieve shared goals; relational and dynamic; rests on trust, communication, legitimacy, shared commitment.
- Leadership = vision and change; management = control and complexity.
- Power (intelligence, wealth, beauty) versus authority (right to command, conferred by the board); authority must be clear, feasible, consistent, capable of being carried out.
- French and Raven: legitimate, reward, coercive, expert, referent.
- Trait (Great Man): physical, intelligence, task-related, personality; limits: no basis for comparison, ignores situation.
- Behavioural: Iowa (Lewin), Ohio State (initiating structure, consideration), Michigan (employee- vs job-centred), Grid (9,9 team; 1,9 country club).
- Contingency: Fiedler (LPC; 3 factors), Hersey-Blanchard (telling, selling, participating, delegating), House path-goal (4 styles), Vroom.
- Burns 1978, Bass 1985: transformational (4 I's) and transactional (3 elements); Greenleaf servant.
- 5 core components; 6 characteristics; roles: decisive, bring people together; 5 governance challenges.

### Theory practice

**Question:** A Nigerian bank manager has excellent relations with her team, the task is highly structured, and she holds strong formal power to hire, reward and discipline. Using Fiedler's model, which type of leader suits this situation best, and why?

*Answer outline:* All three of Fiedler's factors are good (leader-member relations good, task structure high, position power strong), so the situation is **very favourable**. Fiedler found that **task-oriented (low-LPC)** leaders perform best in very favourable and very unfavourable situations, while relationship-oriented leaders do best only in moderately favourable ones. Because Fiedler treats style as fixed, the bank should place a task-oriented leader here, or change the situation to fit the leader it has.
