## Topic 8: Motivating and Rewarding Employees

**What you must know:** the theories of motivation, how they fit together, and how to design rewards.

### Lesson 8.1: What motivation is

**Must know**

- **Motivation:** the process by which a person's efforts are energized, directed and sustained toward attaining a goal; put simply, **encouraging employees to work effectively**. It belongs to **leading**.
- **3 elements:** 1. **Energy** (intensity or drive); 2. **Direction** (effort channelled toward organizational goals); 3. **Persistence** (effort continues until the goal is achieved).
- **Process, 6 steps:** unsatisfied need → tension → drives → search behaviour → satisfied need (also worded "satisfying behaviour" or "satisfied behaviour") → reduction of tension.
- Motivating employees effectively requires **understanding their individual needs and goals**; one size does not fit all.
- **Intrinsic:** from inside (achievement, enjoying the work). **Extrinsic:** external rewards (pay, bonuses, promotion, recognition).
- **Content theories (what motivates):** Maslow, Herzberg, McClelland. **Process theories (how motivation works):** Vroom, Adams, Locke and Latham.

**Explain more**

- Satisfaction is not an element. Vroom is process, not content.

### Lesson 8.2: Early theories of motivation

**Must know**

**1. Maslow's hierarchy of needs (Abraham Maslow),** lowest to highest:

1. **Physiological:** food, water, shelter ("primary needs").
2. **Safety:** protection from harm, job security, health insurance, pension.
3. **Social:** affection, belonging, acceptance, friendship.
4. **Esteem:** internal (self-respect, autonomy, achievement); external (status, recognition, attention).
5. **Self-actualization:** growth, achieving one's potential.

- **Lower-order:** physiological, safety; satisfied **externally** (pay, contracts, tenure). **Higher-order:** the other three; satisfied **internally**.
- **Assumptions:** behaviour is based on needs; needs come in a fixed order; a satisfied need no longer motivates, so the person moves up.

**2. McGregor (Douglas McGregor).** **Theory X** (negative): little ambition, dislike work, avoid responsibility, need close control. **Theory Y** (positive): self-direction, accept and seek responsibility, see work as natural.

**3. Herzberg's two-factor (motivation-hygiene) theory (Frederick Herzberg).**

| Hygiene factors (**extrinsic**) | Motivators (**intrinsic**) |
| --- | --- |
| Remove dissatisfaction; do not motivate | Create satisfaction; motivate; concern the job itself |
| Salary, company policy, supervision, working conditions, relations with others, job security | Achievement, recognition, the work itself, responsibility, advancement, growth |

**4. McClelland's three needs (David McClelland):** **Achievement** (drive to succeed and excel in relation to a set of standards); **Power** (need to make others behave in a way they would not have behaved otherwise); **Affiliation** (desire for friendly and close interpersonal relationships).

**Explain more**

- **Maslow:** helps managers understand what motivates each employee. Status and recognition are esteem, not social.
- **McGregor** favoured Theory Y: participation in decisions, responsible and challenging jobs, good group relations. No evidence either set is valid or that Theory Y always raises motivation.
- **Herzberg:** opposite of satisfaction = **no satisfaction**; opposite of dissatisfaction = **no dissatisfaction**. Fixing hygiene makes workers calm, not motivated. His work led to **job enrichment**.
- **McClelland:** high achievers want personal responsibility, fast feedback, **moderate** risk. Best managers tend to be **high in power, low in affiliation**.

### Lesson 8.3: Goal-setting and reinforcement

**Must know**

- **Goal-setting theory (Edwin Locke, later with Gary Latham):** specific and challenging goals motivate. **Clear**, **difficult but attainable** goals **with feedback** raise performance. Specific beats "do your best"; accepted difficult goals beat easy ones.
- **SMART:** **S**pecific (clear, well defined); **M**easurable (progress tracked with numbers); **A**chievable/attainable (realistic, within reach; goals that are too difficult cause **discouragement**); **R**elevant (fits objectives and values); **T**ime-bound (deadline).
- **Reinforcement theory (B. F. Skinner):** behaviour is a function of its consequences. **Reinforcers:** consequences that, coming immediately after a behaviour, increase the chance it will be repeated.

**Explain more**

- **Self-generated feedback** (tracking own progress) beats feedback from others.
- Participation does not always beat assigned goals, but makes people **accept** a hard goal.
- Other conditions: **goal commitment**, **self-efficacy** (belief one can do the task), task type, national culture. **MBO** applies goal-setting.
- Reinforcement ignores inner needs, goals and expectations. **Ignore** rather than punish unwanted behaviour: punishment works faster but is often temporary, with unpleasant side effects.
- **Trap:** goal-setting = goal **before** the action; reinforcement = consequence **after** it.

### Lesson 8.4: Job design and the job characteristics model

**Must know**

- **Job design:** the way tasks are combined to form complete jobs. **Job scope:** number of different tasks and how often repeated. **Job depth:** degree of control over the work.
- **Job enlargement:** **horizontal** expansion, increasing scope (more tasks of the same kind). **Job enrichment:** **vertical** expansion, adding planning and evaluating responsibilities (increases depth).
- **Job characteristics model (Hackman and Oldham): 5 core dimensions:**
    1. **Skill variety:** range of activities, skills, talents.
    2. **Task identity:** completing a whole, identifiable piece of work.
    3. **Task significance:** effect on the lives or work of others.
    4. **Autonomy:** freedom and discretion in scheduling and choosing methods.
    5. **Feedback:** direct, clear information on performance.
- **3 critical psychological states:** skill variety + task identity + task significance → **experienced meaningfulness**; autonomy → **experienced responsibility**; feedback → **knowledge of actual results**.

**Explain more**

- Outcomes: high internal motivation, performance quality, satisfaction, **low** absenteeism and turnover. Works best for high **growth-need strength** (a trait, not a dimension). Job enrichment is a **non-financial** incentive.

### Lesson 8.5: Equity, expectancy and integration

**Must know**

- **Equity theory (J. Stacy Adams):** an employee compares their **input-outcome ratio** with that of relevant others and acts to correct inequity. **Inputs:** effort, experience, education, skill. **Outcomes:** pay, recognition, promotion.
- **3 referents:** 1. **Other** (similar jobs in the organization, friends, neighbours, professional associates); 2. **System** (pay policies and procedures); 3. **Self** (own past experience).
- **Expectancy theory (Victor Vroom):** a person acts based on the expectation that the act will bring a given outcome and on how attractive that outcome is. **3 linkages:** 1. **Expectancy** (effort-performance); 2. **Instrumentality** (performance-reward); 3. **Valence** (attractiveness of reward).
- **Integrated model:** expectancy theory is the base. Chain: individual effort → individual performance → organizational rewards → individual goals. **Opportunity** to perform also affects performance. Goal-setting: goals direct effort. Reinforcement: rewards reinforce behaviour. Equity: rewards must feel fair. Needs theories: rewards meet needs. JCM: a well-designed job raises motivation directly.

**Explain more**

- Ratio lower than referent's = **under-rewarded**; higher = **over-rewarded**.
- **Responses to inequity:** distort own or others' inputs or outcomes; make others change theirs; change own inputs or outcomes (work less); choose a different referent; quit.
- **Distributive justice:** fairness of the **amount** and allocation of rewards; affects **satisfaction** more. **Procedural justice:** fairness of the **process**; affects commitment, trust in the boss, intention to quit.
- Motivation is low if **any one** linkage is weak. What counts is **perception**. A reward staff do not want = low valence; doubting a promised bonus will be paid = weak instrumentality.

### Lesson 8.6: Current motivation issues and designing rewards

**Must know**

- **Tough economic times:** find creative ways to keep efforts energized, directed and sustained. **Cross-cultural challenge:** most theories were developed in the United States, so do not fit every culture.
- **Flexible work arrangements:** **compressed workweek** (longer days, fewer days); **flextime** (set weekly hours, start and end times vary within limits around common core hours); **job sharing** (two or more people split one full-time job); **telecommuting** (working from home, linked by computer).
- **Unique groups:** **professionals** want job challenge, keeping up with their field, support and recognition; **contingent workers** want a chance to become permanent and training; **low-skilled, minimum-wage workers** respond to recognition programmes and sincere praise.
- **3 ways to design rewards:**
    1. **Open-book management:** financial statements shared with all employees, who learn to read them and see how their work affects results.
    2. **Employee recognition programmes:** personal attention; interest, approval, appreciation for a job well done.
    3. **Pay-for-performance programmes:** variable pay based on a performance measure: piece-rate pay, wage incentive plans, profit-sharing, lump-sum bonuses.

**Explain more**

- **Culture:** Maslow's order fits American culture; where people strongly avoid uncertainty **safety** can top it, where caring and relationships matter most **social** can. McClelland's high achiever fits cultures like the United States. Interesting work, growth and achievement appear across cultures.
- Professionals' loyalty is often to their **profession** more than their employer. Watch **equity** problems when contingent workers sit beside better-paid permanent staff. Pay-for-performance fits **expectancy theory**.
- **Financial incentives:** pay rises, bonuses, profit-sharing, piece-rate pay, perquisites, co-partnership. **Non-financial:** praise, recognition, job enrichment, flexible hours.

### One-minute recap

- Motivation = efforts energized, directed, sustained toward a goal. Content: Maslow, Herzberg, McClelland. Process: Vroom, Adams, Locke and Latham.
- Maslow has 5 needs; McClelland 3. Herzberg: hygiene (incl. salary) vs motivators.
- SMART: specific, measurable, achievable, relevant, time-bound. Reinforcement (Skinner): consequences shape behaviour.
- Enlargement = horizontal, scope; enrichment = vertical, depth. JCM: 5 dimensions, 3 states.
- Equity: other, system, self. Expectancy: expectancy, instrumentality, valence.
- 3 reward designs: open-book management, recognition programmes, pay-for-performance.

### Theory practice

**Question:** Explain Herzberg's two-factor theory and state its implications for managers.

*Answer outline:* Hygiene factors (extrinsic: salary, policy, supervision) prevent dissatisfaction only. Motivators (intrinsic: achievement, recognition, responsibility, growth) create satisfaction. Opposite of satisfaction is no satisfaction. Implication: enrich jobs to motivate.
