## Topic 1: Managers and Management

**What you must know:** managers and their three levels; efficiency, effectiveness and the functions; Mintzberg's roles and Katz's skills; why study management; science or art; history.

### Lesson 1.1: Organizations, managers and levels

**Must know**

- **Organization:** a deliberate arrangement of people to accomplish a specific purpose. Not a building, a mere group, or only profit-makers.
- **3 characteristics:** **goals** (purpose), **people**, a deliberate **structure** (rules, regulations, values) that defines how members relate to one another at work. A student fraternity qualifies.
- **Nonmanagerial employees** do not direct or oversee others.
- **Manager:** works with and through others, directing and overseeing their activities. **Coordinating and integrating others' work** makes a job managerial. Managers may also do ordinary tasks. Managers get the chance to think creatively and use their imagination.

1. **Top (strategic):** whole-organization decisions: vision, mission, long-term goals, policy, budgets. Board of Directors, chairperson, CEO, COO, executive vice president.
2. **Middle (tactical):** between first-line and top; the link. Interpret directives, implement policies, turn goals into action, coordinate departments. Department head, division manager, plant manager, agency head, project leader, functional manager.
3. **First-line (lower, supervisory, operational):** supervise workers: daily operations, routine decisions, training, performance. Report to middle managers. Focus on employee efficiency and motivation. Day-to-day administration of policies is operational (first-line) management's work. Supervisor, team leader, shift manager.

**Explain more**

- First-line is the lowest level of management; where it is not offered, a middle manager is the lowest level listed (nonmanagerial staff are not a management level). Coordinating departments is **not** a top-level job.
- Newer organizations are more **dynamic**; managers are more varied in age, background and gender.

### Lesson 1.2: Management, efficiency, effectiveness and the functions

**Must know**

- **Management:** the process of getting things done effectively and efficiently, with and through people. Also: coordinating resources (planning, organizing, leading, controlling) to achieve goals. Organizational success depends on efficient management of resources; the main aim is organizational goals. It helps organizations achieve goals with minimal resources.

- **Efficiency:** doing things **right**; the **means**. Relation of inputs to outputs: most output from least input; cutting cost while reaching goals.
- **Effectiveness:** doing the **right things**; the **ends**. Attaining goals, e.g. delivering before the deadline.

- Good management needs **both** high. Same sweets at lower cost = efficient, **not** more effective. Better sweets at lower cost = both. Effective management achieves organizational goals efficiently, never wasting resources. More cars at the same cost but full of defects = efficient only.
- **Henri Fayol** (French industrialist) gave **5 functions:** planning, organizing, commanding, coordinating, controlling. Today **4 (POLC):**

1. **P**lanning (first): setting goals and how to reach them: objectives, strategy, forecasting, decision-making, timelines, mission and vision.
2. **O**rganizing: structuring work: grouping tasks, allocating resources, assigning duties, delegating authority, organizational chart.
3. **L**eading (= directing): influencing, motivating, communicating, mentoring, settling conflict. **Motivation** = encouraging employees to work. **Delegation** = assigning duties and responsibilities to employees.
4. **C**ontrolling (last): monitoring, comparing, correcting; evaluating results; ensures all activities are aligned with organizational objectives and tasks are done correctly and on time.

- **Not** functions: innovating; departmentalization (part of organizing).

**Explain more**

- **Directing** initiates action (other functions prepare the setting). It is continuous and flows top to bottom: a manager directs his subordinate and takes instructions from his boss.
- **Staffing:** recruiting, selecting, training, appraising, retaining: the right number and mix of people in the right positions. Budgeting is **not** staffing.
- **Coordinating:** synergizing the efforts of separate units to reach goals. It **is** a management function, relates to organizing, needs leadership. Needed because of task specialization, human differences, task dependency. Mechanisms: rules and procedures, plans and goals, management hierarchy ("organization" is not one). **External** coordination is with outside stakeholders. Problems: poor communication, differing leadership styles, differing formality of structure; not acceptable plans. Lack of commitment causes failure.
- Management is **continuous**; a good manager communicates well. Management thought grew with socio-economic development, managers' experience and thinkers' foundations.

### Lesson 1.3: Roles, skills and universality

**Must know**

**Mintzberg's 10 roles** (from closely studying **5 chief executives**), in 3 groups:

- **Interpersonal (3):** figurehead, leader, liaison.
- **Informational (3):** monitor, disseminator, spokesperson.
- **Decisional (4):** entrepreneur, disturbance handler, resource allocator, negotiator.

- Interpersonal = dealing with people, ceremonial and symbolic duties. Informational = receiving, gathering, spreading information. Decisional = decisions. "Technical" is not a role.
- Dinner speech = figurehead. Attending a professional or scientists' meeting = liaison. Reading business newspapers = monitor. Negotiator is decisional: decides when to compromise.

**Katz's skills:** technical, human (interpersonal), conceptual. The four-skill version adds **political**.

- **Technical:** job-specific (building codes, computer programming); matters most at first-line.
- **Human:** getting along with and motivating people; needed at all levels.
- **Conceptual:** abstract thinking, seeing the whole, applying information to solve problems; matters most at top.
- **Political:** networking, building a power base; for those moving up.

**Universality:** all managers make decisions and do the 4 functions, but not identically. Emphasis varies with **level**, **profit vs nonprofit**, **size**, **country**. Almost every task involves a decision.

**Explain more**

- Mintzberg's surprise: managers spent only a short time on each activity.
- All three interpersonal roles fit **leading**; resource allocator fits **planning**. Higher levels stress figurehead, liaison, disseminator, spokesperson, negotiator; lower levels stress **leader**.
- As managers rise, technical skill matters less; human skill stays needed. A president needs technical skill least. Managers with strong **human skills bring out the best in their people**.
- Political skill: networking, social astuteness, sincerity; not a dominant personality.
- **Nonprofits** differ in how they **judge success**; tight budgets demand efficiency.
- **Small business:** under 500 staff and no major industry influence. Manager is a **generalist**; **spokesperson** matters more. Informal, simpler design; control by direct observation, not computerised monitoring. Large firms focus internally, on **resource allocation**.

### Lesson 1.4: Why study management; science or art

**Must know**

- **2 reasons:** (1) we deal with organizations every day; (2) you will manage or be managed. Well-managed organizations prosper in hard times through a **loyal customer base**.
- **Universality of management:** needed in every type and size of organization, level, work area and country.
- **4 forces:** changing workplaces, ethics and trust (including **mistrust between managers and employees**), economic and political uncertainty, changing technology. Key areas: **customers**, **innovation**. Employees who are unwilling to work is **not** one of these forces.
- Management is **both** a science and an art.

- **Science** (objective): systematic knowledge and universal principles; clear guidelines; systematic planning; decision-making based on data; experimentation, observation, facts; measured and tested; removes bias; predictable, consistent results; applies to any organization; e.g. time and motion study.
- **Art** (subjective): practical skill and personal judgement; creativity, innovation, intuition, vision, experience, talent; improves with continuous practice; develops unique solutions; matters most in strategic decision-making; motivation, leadership style, conflict resolution. "An art of getting things done" means it requires human skills.
- **Both** because it combines creativity (art) and structure (science).

**Explain more**

- It is an **inexact science** because it deals with human behaviour. **Mintzberg** stressed the art side. Trial and error is **not** scientific.
- Scientific management (productivity, standard work methods, data) is criticised as **mechanistic and rigid**, ignoring human aspects.
- Management is often hard and thankless; success depends on how well **others** perform. Managers create an environment where members work to their best, think creatively, and rely on **every member** to raise productivity. The direct-supervisor relationship is the biggest factor in productivity and loyalty.
- Hardest ethical challenge: laying off hardworking, productive staff.
- Customer satisfaction is increasingly a key goal; a prompt, helpful human answer shows responsiveness. **Innovation** gives an advantage over competitors.
- **C.K. Prahalad** introduced "core competency". A football fan gains least.

### Lesson 1.5: History of management

**Must know**

Management is ancient; its study as a discipline began in the **20th century**. Five eras:

1. **Early:** Arsenal of Venice, **Adam Smith** (*Wealth of Nations*, division of labour), Industrial Revolution (machine power replaced human power).
2. **Classical:** **Taylor** (scientific management, "one best way"; focus on process efficiency), **Gilbreths**, **Fayol**, **Weber** (bureaucracy).
3. **Behavioural (OB):** **Owen** (working conditions), **Munsterberg** (industrial psychology), **Follett** (individual and group behaviour), **Barnard** (cooperative social systems; first to call organizations open systems), **Hawthorne Studies** (Mayo; the most important contribution to the growth of organizational behaviour).
4. **Quantitative:** operations research / management science, from World War II; **TQM** (total quality management, the quality revolution of the 1980s-90s).
5. **Contemporary:** systems, contingency ("it depends"), information technology.

Four approaches of the early 20th century: scientific management, general administrative, quantitative, OB. Systems is **not** one.

**Fayol's 14 principles:** division of work · authority · discipline · unity of command · unity of direction · subordination of individual interest · remuneration · centralization · scalar chain · order · equity · stability of tenure · initiative · esprit de corps. "Equality" and "monopoly" are **not** principles.

- **Division of work:** tasks by skill (specialization); raises efficiency.
- **Authority:** right to give orders and expect obedience; balanced with responsibility.
- **Discipline:** respect for agreements and rules. **Remuneration:** fair pay.
- **Unity of command:** one boss; gives clear lines of authority and reduces confusion. **Unity of direction:** common objectives.
- **Scalar chain:** line of authority top to bottom. **Esprit de corps:** team spirit.

**Explain more**

- **Smith:** division of labour raises productivity: more skill and dexterity, less time lost switching tasks, labour-saving inventions (not strict control of time).
- **Taylor:** Pennsylvania steel companies; shocked that workers used different methods, took it easy, produced only about a third of what they could, and were placed regardless of ability. Concern: **worker efficiency**. Study: **pig iron**. Recommended **incentive pay**.
- **Gilbreths:** began with **bricklaying**; used motion pictures to study hand and body motions; **therbligs** = basic hand motions.
- **General administrative theory** (Fayol, Weber): the whole organization. Fayol studied **macro** issues, Taylor **micro**.
- **Weber's bureaucracy:** division of labour, hierarchy, detailed rules, impersonality, **formal selection** (choosing people for jobs on the basis of their **technical qualifications**), career orientation.
- **OB** is linked with motivation. Munsterberg links with scientific management; work groups trace to Follett.
- **Hawthorne:** began as **lighting** tests; group norms drive individual behaviour; productivity and human relations.
- **Quantitative:** from WWII military problems. **Linear programming** allocates resources; **EOQ** sets inventory levels. Motivating individuals is **not** quantitative.
- **TQM** (quality management; **Deming and Juran**): continual improvement, customer needs; costs can fall while productivity rises.
- **Contingency:** flexible, no one best way.
- **Trends:**
    - **Workforce diversity:** gender, race, age and more. **Learning organization:** keeps learning, adapting, changing. **Knowledge management:** members systematically gather and share knowledge.
    - **Entrepreneurship:** exploiting opportunities others have not noticed. 3 themes: pursuing opportunities, innovation, growth.
    - **E-business:** the organization's work through internet links; **e-commerce** is its sales and marketing side. Levels: **enhanced**, **enabled** (internet improves traditional work, no online selling), **total** (revolves around the internet). **Intranet:** internal network for employees only.

### One-minute recap

- Organization = goals + people + structure.
- Management = efficient (means), effective (ends) work through people.
- Fayol's 5 functions became **POLC**; directing = leading.
- Mintzberg: 10 roles (3 interpersonal, 3 informational, 4 decisional).
- Katz: technical (first-line), human (all levels), conceptual (top), plus political.
- Management is both a science and an art. History: early, classical, behavioural, quantitative, contemporary.
