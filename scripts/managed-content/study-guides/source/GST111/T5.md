## Topic 5: Reading Skills II: Comprehension and Summary

**What you must know:** how to study a comprehension passage in stages (preview, first reading, second reading); the two major question types (MCQ and Traditional) and how to answer each; the four categories of comprehension questions; what a summary, synopsis and abstract are; the rules and the four steps for writing a summary.

### Lesson 5.1: Studying a comprehension passage

**Must know**

- **Reading** involves the eyes and the brain and is fundamental to acquiring knowledge. To understand a comprehension passage fully, study its components carefully and methodically.
- **Preview first:** previewing prepares you to study the passage in a "questioning" manner, as if searching for something. Turn the title into a question: "The Causes of Election Violence" becomes "What are the causes of election violence?"
- **Read the passage more than once.** This is the key habit for studying a comprehension passage effectively. A passage is best understood when it is read **more than once** (not simply "once", "twice" or "thrice").
- **Match your speed to the passage:** your reading speed should correspond to how easy or difficult the language is.

| Passage language | Speed | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Technical or difficult | Slow | To assimilate the peculiar vocabulary |
| Fairly familiar | Faster | Easier to follow |

The two stages of reading:

1. **First reading:** done **rapidly**, whatever the nature of the passage, to get a **general impression of the central ideas**. You gain insight into:
   1. What the writer's **tone** suggests about his or her **attitude** to the topic.
   2. What the writer is trying to **achieve**: to persuade, inform, educate, frighten or annoy the reader (the purpose).
   3. The **theme** or central idea of the passage.
2. **Second reading:** done at a **much slower pace**. Here you isolate the **main ideas from the minor ones**, the **subjective from the objective**, and the **direct from the indirect**. It demands careful attention to details of the general ideas found in the first reading. Focus on:
   1. Sentences which **summarise** an idea or ideas.
   2. **Examples or illustrations**.
   3. Statements which give a clue to the writer's **viewpoint**.
   4. Statements which tend to **suggest ideas** to you.

**Explain more**

- First reading = fast, big picture (tone, purpose, theme). Second reading = slow, details (main vs minor, salient points). Exams swap these: if an option says the first reading is "slow and detailed", it is wrong.
- A passage on "The Michael Jackson Saga" can be raced through; "Globalist Action and the African Psyche" must be read slowly. The difference is the difficulty of the language, not the length.
- The four focus points of the second reading help you recognise the **salient points** (the most important ideas) of the passage.
- Order: preview, first (rapid) reading, second (slow) reading, then answer the questions.

### Lesson 5.2: Answering the questions: MCQ and Traditional Type

**Must know**

Three things comprehension passages do:

1. They require you to **analyse** the contents of the passage.
2. They test your understanding by demanding a **summary of the salient points** or ideas.
3. They demand an **in-depth study** so that you can **infer** information that is not presented directly (implied, suggested or subtly indicated).

**Two major types of comprehension question:**

1. **Multiple Choice Questions (MCQ):** a **fast mode** of testing understanding. The alternatives are carefully designed to **disguise** the answer, which you obtain through a **process of elimination**.
2. **Traditional Type Questions:** questions which require you to give answers **in your own words**, e.g. "What do you suppose is the writer's attitude towards the subject matter?", "What is your impression of the writer's viewpoint?", "In one sentence, summarise the central idea of the passage."

**4 clues to answering MCQ:**

1. **Never mark more than one answer.**
2. Ensure that **all other options are incorrect** before marking your answer.
3. Attempt the question through a **process of elimination**, discarding the incorrect answers.
4. There is **usually a correct answer** unless otherwise stated.

**4 features of MCQ options:**

1. Some answers are **suggested by the writer but not confirmed** in the text.
2. Some answers are **only partly correct**.
3. Some answers are **logically true but not specifically mentioned** in the text.
4. Some contain **zero answers**, e.g. "none of the above", "don't know".

**4 features of Traditional Type questions:**

1. Some demand answers **obtainable only in the passage**, e.g. "According to the passage, what does globalisation mean?"
2. Some require you to explain the **meaning of specific words in context**, e.g. "cat" could be an animal or a metaphor (a cat with nine lives).
3. Some demand your **personal opinion** about the contents, as long as your inferences are relevant to the text. These test your **imagination**.
4. Some demand your **personal deductions** based on what is implied, suggested or clearly stated.

**Explain more**

- **MCQ** = Multiple Choice Questions. If an exam lists "OPQ", "PVQ", "TDQ" and "MCQ" as question types asked in comprehension passages, only **MCQ** is real.
- "Zero answer" is the name for an option such as "none of the above" or "don't know". It carries no content of its own.
- An MCQ needs only a choice; a Traditional question needs a written response in **your own words**, so it demands in-depth understanding of the passage.
- Word-in-context questions (feature 2) test whether you can tell the literal meaning from the figurative one. Answer with the meaning the **passage** uses, not the dictionary's first meaning.

### Lesson 5.3: The four categories of comprehension questions

**Must know**

MCQ and Traditional Type describe the **format** of questions. When you consider the **tools examiners use to test understanding**, there are **four categories**:

1. **Direct reference questions:** require you to **refer directly to the text**, e.g. "With reference to the passage, what are the major types of masquerades in Africa?"
2. **Inference questions:** require you to **infer** from the text, i.e. find out or establish the **relationship between two or more sentences or parts** of the passage, e.g. "What do you consider the difference between the view expressed in sentence 1 and sentence 3 of paragraph four?"
3. **Supposition questions:** require you to **think about ideas which extend beyond the passage**, relating aspects of the passage to the **outside world**, e.g. "What do you suppose is responsible for the negative attitude of labour leaders to most undemocratic governments in the world today?"
4. **Evaluation questions:** require an **evaluation of the passage** in terms of the **information provided** and the **effectiveness of the writer's language** in presenting the main idea, e.g. "In your opinion, what is the motivation of the writer in this passage?"

**Explain more**

- There is **no** "affirmation question" among the four. If an exam asks which is NOT one of the categories, the odd one out is whichever name is not direct reference, inference, supposition or evaluation.
- Trap: "two types" and "four categories" are different lists. Two **types** (format): MCQ, Traditional. Four **categories** (examiner's tools): direct reference, inference, supposition, evaluation.
- Signals in the stem: "with reference to the passage" = direct reference; "difference between sentence 1 and sentence 3" = inference; "what do you suppose" = supposition; "in your opinion ... the writer's language" = evaluation.
- Supposition takes you **outside** the text to the world; evaluation keeps you **on** the text but asks you to judge it.

### Lesson 5.4: Summary, synopsis and abstract

**Must know**

- **Summary (also synopsis or recap):** a **condensed version of an original work**, usually about **one-third of the length** of the original. It highlights the **major points**, with the sole purpose of helping the reader get the message in a more concise manner.
- A **paragraph** can often be summarised in a **single sentence**; a **three or four paragraph passage** can usually be summarised in a **single paragraph**.
- **Synopsis:** a **summary of a plot**. When you return from a movie and say what it was about, naming the main characters and what happened to them, that is a synopsis. Its use is **limited to novels, plays and stories**. For all other reading materials, the **summary** is used.
- **Abstract:** a **shorter, more concise non-fiction summary**.

**Written summary of a comprehension passage:**

1. Begin with a **lead** which states the **title, author, text type and main idea** of the text.
2. Arrange the ideas **clearly**.
3. **Paraphrase** expressions with new words; **no quotations** from the text.
4. No dramatic structure is needed, because a summary does not retell a story.
5. Use the **present tense** or the **historic present**.
6. If the material is a **book or dissertation**, present the **major facts** in common scientific language.

**Summary in non-fiction:** non-fiction portrays **actual events**. You highlight the **author's point of view** on the subject matter **without offering analysis or assessment**. In addition to the guidelines above, you provide **numerical ratings and evaluations** of the titles covered.

**Explain more**

- Trap question: "Which is NOT true about writing a summary?" The false statement is "quotations from the text are included". True statements: expressions are paraphrased with new words; the major facts are included; the present tense is used.
- "A ____ is a condensed version of an original work" = **summary**. Not "abstract" (a specific short non-fiction summary), "preface" (an introduction before a book's main text) or "blurb" (the promotional text on a book's cover).
- "A ____ is a summary of a plot" = **synopsis**. Not "storyline" (the plot itself), "prologue" (an introductory section) or "episode" (one part of a work).
- Why present tense? A summary describes what the text says now, so "the writer argues", not "the writer argued". **Historic present** = present tense used for past events ("In 1349 the king issues an ordinance"); it is accepted in summaries.

### Lesson 5.5: Strategies and steps for writing a summary

**Must know**

Summary writing requires you to distinguish **important, less important and trivial** information, and to judge **main ideas** against **supporting details**. The strategy depends on the type and length of the text.

**3 recommended strategies:**

1. **Paragraph summarisation:** focus on **one paragraph at a time**.
2. **Section summarisation:** focus on the **multi-paragraph section** that covers the topic and **integrate the multiple ideas**.
3. **Multi-section summarisation** (e.g. report writing): read each section or chapter and make **at least three summarising statements** out of each. This creates a paragraph with a **topic sentence, at least three supporting sentences and a closing sentence**.

**Summarising a paragraph:** condense the whole paragraph into a few sentences. **Two results:** (1) you prove you understand it by translating it into your own words; (2) you reduce it to a more convenient form for review.

**The 4 steps of summarising, in order:**

1. **Read** the paragraph to determine its **basic idea**.
2. **Read it again** to pick out the **essentials**, summarising mentally as you read; make notes if necessary.
3. **Without consulting the paragraph**, express its content **in your own words**. If you get stuck, stop and repeat step 2.
4. **Compare** your summary with the original and make any necessary **revisions**.

**Tense in summaries of literature:**

| Text | Tense |
|---|---|
| Drama | Present tense is most appropriate |
| Poems, stories, novels | Present preferred; past allowed if it seems more natural |

Whichever tense you choose, **use it throughout**. Shifting tense gives the appearance of **uncertainty**.

**Explain more**

- Most paragraphs develop **one simple idea** by **repetition, explanation or illustration**. If the idea is not stated openly, **distil** it from the sentences that come closest to stating it.
- The same four steps in other words: (i) find the basic idea; (ii) eliminate whatever is not essential; (iii) write the summary, safest done with the original put aside; (iv) compare the digested form with the original for accuracy and revise.
- Ordering trap: exams jumble the steps. The correct order is always **read for basic idea, read for essentials, express in own words, compare with original**. Writing can never come before reading, and comparing can only come last.
- **Keep to the point:** no new opinions, no needless examples, no quotations.
- Worked example. Original: "Many students fail examinations not because they are unintelligent but because they prepare badly. They start reading a few days before the paper, read everything at the same speed, and never test themselves. Those who plan their reading, vary their speed and check their understanding usually do far better." One-sentence summary: "Examination failure usually comes from poor preparation, so students who plan, vary their reading speed and test themselves do better." Check: basic idea kept, examples condensed, own words, present tense, under half the length.

### One-minute recap

- Preview (turn the title into a question); read **more than once**, at a speed matching the difficulty of the language.
- First reading: **rapid**, for tone, purpose, theme. Second reading: **much slower**, for main vs minor ideas, summarising sentences, examples, viewpoint clues.
- Two question **types**: **MCQ** (fast; elimination; never mark more than one) and **Traditional Type** (your own words).
- MCQ options may be partly correct, logically true but not in the text, suggested but not confirmed, or **zero answers**.
- Four **categories**: **direct reference, inference, supposition, evaluation**. No "affirmation question".
- **Summary** = condensed version, about **one-third** the length. **Synopsis** = summary of a **plot** (novels, plays, stories). **Abstract** = short, concise non-fiction summary.
- Written summary: lead (title, author, text type, main idea); paraphrase; **no quotations**; **present tense**.
- Four steps: read for basic idea, read for essentials, write in own words, compare and revise. Three strategies: paragraph, section, multi-section.
- Keep one tense throughout; shifting tense looks like uncertainty.
