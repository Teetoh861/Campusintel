## Topic 11: Grammar II: Clauses and Sentences

**What you must know:** what a clause is; main versus subordinate clauses; the three clause types by form (finite, non-finite, verbless) and three by function (adjective/relative, adverb, noun); defining versus non-defining relative clauses and their commas; the sentence, major and minor sentences and fragments; simple, compound, multiple, complex and compound-complex sentences; the four sentence types by function; subject and predicate, S, V, O, C, A and the seven clause patterns; subject-verb agreement; the seven common sentence faults.

### Lesson 11.1: The clause and its forms

**Must know**

- **Hierarchy of grammatical units:** morpheme, word, phrase, clause, sentence. The clause is next in rank after the phrase; the **sentence is the highest unit of grammatical analysis**.
- **Clause:** a group of words that contains a **subject and a predicate**.
- **Main (independent) clause:** can stand by itself and make sense. It identifies what the sentence is about (the subject) and gives the essential information about it (the predicate).
- **Subordinate (dependent) clause:** cannot stand by itself or make complete sense; it needs the main clause. It works inside the main clause like a **noun** or a **modifier**.
- **A simple sentence occurring by itself can also be called a clause.** When simple sentences combine into a larger sentence, each becomes a clause: *Olu kicked the ball and Ade caught it* has two clauses linked by *and*.

**3 types of subordinate clause by form** (structure):

1. **Finite clause:** contains a **finite verb**, a verb form that **changes** with **number, person and tense** (*visit, visits, visited, am visiting, is visiting*).
2. **Non-finite clause:** contains a **non-finite verb**, a form that **remains unchanging** whatever the number, person or tense. Often has **no subject**: *It will be safer [for us to leave the scene]*; *The car [being repaired] is mine*; *[The applause having died down,] she began to sing*.
3. **Verbless clause:** has **no verb**, and usually no subject; both are understood from context: *He became a drug addict [while in prison]*; *[When in his famous sad mood,] he will not talk to anyone for days*.

**Explain more**

- Main + subordinate: *We had to abandon our home* | *as the flood waters rose higher*; *They found out* | *that he had lied to them*.
- The subordinate clause may come **first** (*As the flood waters rose higher, we had to abandon our home*) or be **embedded** in the main clause (*The boy [who came here today] is my cousin*; *My sister, [who lives in Warri,] is visiting us today*).
- Finite verb changes: *They visit* (plural, present); *Mary visits* (singular); *Mary visited* (past); *I am visiting*.
- **Verbless clause rule:** only clauses whose main verb is **to be** (*is, am, are, was, were, be, been, being*) can be verbless. The missing subject is usually the subject of the main clause: *while (he was) in prison*.
- **Trap:** a finite clause contains **a verb that changes**; "a verb that remains unchanging" is non-finite and "no verb at all" is verbless. There is no **"infinite clause"**.

### Lesson 11.2: Clauses by function: adjective, adverb and noun clauses

**Must know**

Subordinate clauses do the work of adjectives, adverbs and nouns, so there are **3 types by function**:

- **Adjective (or relative) clause:** modifies a **noun** (or pronoun) like an adjective. The other name for the adjective clause is **relative clause**. Introduced by the **relative pronouns** *who, whom, whose, which, that* (occasionally *when, why, where*). The relative pronoun is the **subject** or **object** of its clause: *The man [who called you up this morning] is my uncle* (*who* = subject); *This is the man [whom the police questioned yesterday]* (*whom* = object).
- **Adverb clause:** usually modifies the **verb** (time, place, manner, cause, condition, degree, comparison, concession), or an adjective, another adverb or the rest of the sentence. Introduced by **subordinating conjunctions**: *when, if, since, before, as, because, although, while, wherever, after, unless, whether*.
- **Noun clause:** sits in noun positions and does noun jobs: **subject, direct object, indirect object, subject complement, object complement, prepositional complement, appositive**. Usually introduced by **that**; also *what, where, who, whoever, whether, why, when, whatever*.

**Relative clauses: 2 major functions**

1. To **identify** the particular noun meant; they answer **"which one?"** This is the **defining (restrictive)** clause.
2. To give **additional information** about a noun already known. This is the **non-defining (non-restrictive)** clause.

| | Defining (restrictive) | Non-defining (non-restrictive) |
|---|---|---|
| Job | Identifies: "which one?" | Adds new, extra information |
| Essential to meaning? | Yes; fused into the sentence | No; can be left out |
| Commas | **None** | **Paired commas**, one before and one after |
| *that* | **Accepted** | **Never** used |
| Pronoun omitted? | Yes, if not the subject (*the man we have been looking for*) | No |

**Explain more**

- **Defining:** *The man who lent you a book this morning is our Use of English instructor* (without the clause: "which man?"); *The banks which had failed to comply were sanctioned*.
- **Non-defining:** *My cousin Miriam, who was runner-up last year, won the first prize this year*; *Chike, who had never been to Lagos before, was excited about the trip.* Miriam and Chike are already known.
- **Commas change meaning:** *The students who arrived late for the lecture were locked out* = **some** students arrived late. *The students, who arrived late for the lecture, were locked out* = **all** students arrived late.
- ***That*:** *The students that arrived late were locked out* is correct (restrictive). *\*Chike, that had never been to Lagos before, ...* is unacceptable. American English permits only *that* in defining clauses; use this as a guide to punctuating.
- **Trap:** "restrictive clause" is only a **sub-type** of relative clause, not another name for the adjective clause.
- **Adverb clauses:** *She hung onto his feet [as if her life depended on it]* is an **adverbial clause of manner** modifying *hung* (not adjectival; "relative clause of manner" and "verbal clause" are not clause types). *[Whenever he is upset,] he goes out and gets drunk* is an adverbial clause of **time**. *If you study very hard* = condition; *Wherever they went* = place; *Although the two countries had signed a peace treaty* = concession; *because he was afraid* = cause.
- **Noun clauses:** *[That she was the best suited for the job] was pretty obvious* (subject); *We found out [that he was not telling the truth]* (direct object); *He told his pathetic story to [whoever he met on the way]* (indirect object); *The question now is [how we can solve this problem]* (subject complement); *Our reaction will depend on [what he does next]* (complement of the preposition *on*); *The fact [that she is not here now] speaks for itself* (appositive). A **nominal clause** = noun clause; **adjectival clause** = adjective clause.

### Lesson 11.3: The sentence and its types by form

**Must know**

- **Sentence, 2 definitions:** (1) a group of words that **begins with a capital letter and ends with a full stop**; (2) a group of words that **expresses a complete thought**.
- **Sentence fragment:** does **not** express a complete thought; essential parts are missing: *\*Because I was hungry*; *\*While Dad was driving across town.* **Unacceptable.**

**By form, first distinguish:**

1. **Major sentences:** complete; contain the **subject and the predicate**: *The carpenter completed the job this afternoon.* The vast majority of sentences are major.
2. **Minor sentences:** **meaningful but grammatically incomplete**; they lack an essential component, but the omitted parts are **recoverable from context**: *No fuel. Gatekeeper wanted. For sale. No vacancy. No smoking. Look out! Taxi, Surulere! Better luck next time!* **Acceptable.**

**Major sentences are further divided into 5 kinds:**

| Kind | Clauses | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Simple** | **one clause**, **only one finite verb** (a single statement) | *The boy killed the snake.* |
| **Compound** | **two** clauses, each independent and equal; two finite verbs | *Ngozi was at the scene, but she didn't witness the accident.* |
| **Multiple** | like compound, but **three or more** clauses | *The fire was raging, the police kept the anxious crowd at bay, and the firefighters were dashing around.* |
| **Complex** | **one** independent (main) + **one or more** dependent (subordinate) | *She met her husband while she was studying abroad.* |
| **Compound-complex** | **two or more** main + **at least one** subordinate | *Olu kicked the ball as it rolled towards him and Ade caught it.* |

**Explain more**

- **Fragment vs minor sentence:** a fragment leaves the reader wondering; a minor sentence is clear: *(There is) no fuel. (A) gatekeeper (is) wanted. (You should) look out! (I wish you) better luck next time!*
- **Trap:** *God bless you* is **not** a minor sentence: it has its own subject (*God*) and verb (*bless*). *No smoking* is a minor sentence, not a simple one (no subject, no finite verb).
- **Simple sentence trap:** one clause **and** one finite verb. Two clauses or two finite verbs means it is not simple. *The teacher asked a difficult question and no student could answer it* is compound.
- **Compound sentences** use **coordinating conjunctions** (*and, but, or, nor, for, yet*) and **conjunctive adverbs** (*however, moreover, nevertheless, therefore, still, so*).
- **Complex:** the subordinate clause is joined by a **subordinating conjunction** (*They returned home after the war ended*) or by **embedding** (*The escaping robber, who was shot in the head last night, died this morning*). **Trap:** one independent clause and **one or more** dependent clauses, not exactly one. Two independent clauses plus a subordinate one = compound-complex (*He washed the clothes when it stopped raining and ironed them later*).

### Lesson 11.4: Sentence types by function and the elements of a sentence

**Must know**

**4 sentence types by function:**

1. **Declarative:** makes a **statement**; ends with a **full stop (period)**. *Tomorrow is my birthday.*
2. **Interrogative:** **asks a question**. *What were you doing this morning?*
3. **Imperative:** **gives directives**. *Get out! Have a drink. Look out!*
4. **Exclamatory:** expresses **strong emotion**; usually ends with an **exclamation mark**. *Oh, my goodness! I'll be damned!*

**12 kinds of imperative directive:** commanding (*Get out!*), inviting (*Have a drink.*), warning (*Look out!*), pleading (*Have mercy.*), permitting (*Suit yourself.*), suggesting (*Let's go by bus.*), advising (*Take the first flight.*), requesting (*Open the windows, please.*), meditating (*Let me see.*), expressing an imprecation (*Damn you!*), expressing good wishes (*Have a lovely day.*), instructing (*Stir for five minutes.*).

**Elements of a sentence:**

- **2 major components:** the **subject** (names the person or thing about which something is said) and the **predicate** (what is said about the subject). *Birds* | *fly*. *The happy children* | *poured out of the classroom*.
- **5 components:** **subject (S), verb (V), object (O), complement (C), adverbial or adjunct (A)**.
- **7 basic clause patterns** of the English simple sentence:

1. **SV:** *Jesus wept.*
2. **SVO:** *The guards caught the thief.*
3. **SVC:** *Kofi became the president.*
4. **SVA:** *The plane took off at eight o'clock.*
5. **SVOO:** *The man bought his wife a present.*
6. **SVOC:** *The priest pronounced the couple man and wife.*
7. **SVOA:** *The tribunal conducted the hearing behind closed doors.*

**Explain more**

- **Traps:** "interlocutory" is not a sentence type. *Oh, my goodness!* and *I don't believe that!* are exclamatory, **not** imperative. The plainest imperative is a bare verb with *you* understood: *Take the bus home.*
- **Trap:** the **major components of a sentence are the subject and the predicate**, not main and subordinate clause, not independent and dependent clause, not nouns and adjectives.
- **Subject:** in *That boy is my cousin*, *boy* is the **subject of the verb** *is*.
- **Direct object:** receives the action; answers "what/whom?" *Mr Dokunboh thanked the lady* (*the lady* = direct object); *The young lady thanked Mr Dokubo* (*Mr Dokubo* = direct object); *Mr Dokunboh donated some books* (*some books* = direct object).
- **Indirect object:** the receiver, "to or for whom?", normally present only when the verb has two objects (SVOO). *We bought John a guitar*: *John* = indirect object, *a guitar* = **direct object**. *The man bought his wife a present*: *his wife* = indirect, *a present* = direct.
- **Complement:** (i) a noun or noun phrase that is **the same as** the subject or object (*Nosa is my cousin*; *Kofi became the president*, so SVC; *man and wife* = *the couple*, so SVOC); or (ii) an **adjective describing** the subject or object (*The panel considered his remarks misleading*).
- **Adverbial (adjunct):** like an adverb, tells **time, place, manner**: *at eight o'clock* (SVA), *behind closed doors* (SVOA).
- **Trap:** **seven** patterns, not 4, 6 or 8; **SVCC is not one**. *Jesus wept* = SV.

### Lesson 11.5: Subject-verb agreement (concord)

**Must know**

- **Concord** = grammatical agreement. **4 types:** subject-verb, pronoun-antecedent, determiner-noun head, tense concord. Focus: **subject-verb agreement in number** (singular/plural).
- **Rule:** **singular subject, singular verb; plural subject, plural verb.** *The girl lives in Surulere. The girls live in Surulere.*
- **The -s inconsistency:** plural **nouns** take -s; **singular verbs** take -s. *The car drives. The cars drive.*

**7 rules for doubtful subjects:**

1. **Joined by *and*:** **plural** (*The man and his wife were present. The manager and all his staff are here.*), **unless** the members form a **single unit** (*Hot lemonade and honey is good for a cold. Waiter, is the rice and beans ready? The MD and Chief Executive Officer of the company was introduced.*).
2. **Singular members joined by *(either...) or* / *(neither...) nor*:** singular (*Either the security man or the suspect is lying*). Plural members: plural (*Neither the apprentices nor their supervisors were available*).
3. **Concord of proximity** (nearness): mixed *or*-compounds agree with the **nearest** member: *Either Janet or her parents visit...*; *Either you or Ibrahim is...*; *Either you or I am...*
4. **Plural in form, singular in meaning:** always singular: *There is no news yet. Two hundred pounds is all he managed to save. Statistics is not as difficult as many think.*
5. **Linking expressions** (*with, together with, accompanied by, as well as, along with, in collaboration with, in conjunction with, flanked by, escorted by, in addition to*): the verb **stays singular** with a singular subject: *The Manager, together with all his staff, was outside. The governor, accompanied by his aides, is walking down the street.*
6. ***The number of*** (a specific figure) = **singular**; ***a number of*** ("several") = **plural**: *The number of reported cases has dropped. A number of women and children have been treated.*
7. **Collective nouns** (*class, committee, crew, team, congregation, audience, herd, jury*): singular if viewed as a **unit**, plural if viewed as **individuals**. This is **notional concord**: *The jury has reached a verdict. The jury are still arguing over that point.*

**Explain more**

- **Trap:** only *The man and his wife were present* has a **plural compound subject**; lemonade and honey (one drink), rice and beans (one dish) and the MD and CEO (one person) are singular units.
- ***And* vs a linking expression:** *The captain and several crew members were injured* gives both equal attention; *The captain, along with several crew members, was injured* focuses on the captain. *Organisation A in collaboration with Organisation B* = B played a subsidiary role.
- Any **pronoun** referring to a collective noun must agree with it too. Faulty: *\*The committee has concluded its deliberations and their report is expected* (*its* then *their*); *\*The cabinet is divided, but it is hoped that they will soon agree* (*is* then *they*).

### Lesson 11.6: Common errors in sentence construction

**Must know**

**7 sentence faults:**

1. **Empty sentences:** grammatically correct but **say nothing**: *\*I want to acquire university education because acquiring university education will satisfy my long-felt desires.* Cancel or revise.
2. **Padded sentences:** full of **unnecessary words**. Two kinds: **unnecessary repetition** and **wordiness**. Sentences should be **accurate, brief and clear**.
3. **Overloaded sentences:** **too many facts and ideas** crowded in. Split them. Rules: (i) **say one thing at a time**; (ii) **say it simply and directly**.
4. **Dangling modifiers:** modifiers whose word is **missing**, so they have **nothing to modify**: *\*Returning home late one night, the gate was locked.*
5. **Run-on sentences:** two or more sentences **collapsed into one with no punctuation** between them: *\*Anne looked everywhere for her missing phone unfortunately she did not find it.*
6. **The comma splice:** related to the run-on; a **comma weakly separates** sentences run together: *\*Anne looked everywhere for her phone, unfortunately she did not find it, she reported the loss to the hostel warden.*
7. **The sentence fragment:** a **phrase or subordinate clause** punctuated as a sentence: *A nominal clause functions like a noun. \*While an adjectival clause modifies a noun.*

**Explain more**

- **Padding:** *\*The modern businessman of today must completely master all of the principles of computerization*: "of today" repeats "modern"; "completely" and "all of" repeat "master". *\*...as a result of the fact that our computers broke down*: seven words replaced by **because**.
- **Overloading:** one long sentence about a large university was split into three; one about Chike's parents into four.
- **Dangling modifiers:** correct use: *While climbing a palm tree, Amodu lost his grip* (the modifier attaches to *Amodu*). Faulty: *\*Driving around Greensville a year later, it's clear the town will never be the same*; *\*Having said that, there are other points to consider*; *\*Returning home late one night, a snake bit Olu* (the modified word must be **right next to** the modifier, since modifiers attach to the **closest** noun or pronoun). **Two corrections:** put the doer next to the modifier (*Returning home late one night, Olu was bitten by a snake*) or make the modifier a full clause (*As Olu returned home late one night, he was bitten by a snake*).
- **Run-ons:** break into sentences or link with suitable conjunctions.
- ***While*** and other **subordinating conjunctions** (*when, although, as, because, since, whenever, wherever*) make a clause subordinate, so it cannot stand alone. Correct: *A nominal clause functions as a noun, while an adjectival clause modifies a noun.*
- **Trap:** run-on = **no** punctuation; comma splice = a **comma** where a full stop or conjunction is needed. Minor sentence = acceptable; fragment = not.

### One-minute recap

- Clause = subject + predicate; main stands alone, subordinate does not. A simple sentence alone is also a clause.
- Form: finite (verb changes), non-finite (unchanging), verbless (no verb; *to be* only).
- Function: adjective/relative (noun; *who, whom, whose, which, that*), adverb (verb; subordinating conjunctions), noun (S, DO, IO, complement, appositive; *that*).
- Defining: "which one?", no commas, accepts *that*. Non-defining: extra information, paired commas, never *that*.
- Major (subject + predicate) vs minor (incomplete but recoverable); fragments unacceptable.
- Simple (1 clause, 1 finite verb), compound (2), multiple (3+), complex (1 main + 1 or more subordinate), compound-complex (2+ main + 1 or more subordinate).
- Declarative (statement), interrogative (question), imperative (directive), exclamatory (emotion).
- Subject + predicate; S, V, O, C, A; 7 patterns: SV, SVO, SVC, SVA, SVOO, SVOC, SVOA.
- Concord: *and* plural unless one unit; *or/nor* nearest member; *together with* keeps singular; *the number of* singular, *a number of* plural; collective nouns notional.
- 7 faults: empty, padded, overloaded, dangling modifier, run-on, comma splice, fragment.

### Theory practice

**Question:** Distinguish between defining and non-defining relative clauses, with one example of each, and show how punctuation affects meaning.

*Answer outline:* Define the relative (adjective) clause. Defining: identifies the noun ("which one?"), essential, no commas, accepts *that* (*The students who arrived late were locked out*: some students). Non-defining: adds information about a known noun, non-essential, paired commas, never *that* (*The students, who arrived late, were locked out*: all students). Conclude that the commas change "some" to "all".
