## Topic 8: Writing Skills II: Essay Writing

**What you must know:** why writing matters to scholarship; what an essay is and its thesis; the four essay types (narrative, analytical, descriptive, argumentative/persuasive) and what each demands; the COEM factor (Content, Organisation, Expression, Mechanics); introduction, body and conclusion; linking paragraphs; the punctuation marks and their functions; capitalisation, spelling, reports and the parts of a formal letter.

### Lesson 8.1: Writing, the essay and its parts

**Must know**

- **Writing** is an indispensable part of scholarship: about the most **permanent record** of your view on an issue. It requires **training, tact, imagination and rigour**.
- Academic activity consists of observing phenomena and reporting on them, listening to lectures, group projects and, most important, **reading and reviewing**. Through writing (term papers, research reports) you are tested on how much you have learned.
- **Essay:** a written paper of **several paragraphs** addressing a **central subject** clearly specified in the **thesis statement**. An essay is a combination of ideas from one paragraph to another that creates a **united whole**.
- **Thesis:** the **major point an essay seeks to prove**; the central idea and organising principle. In a scholarly (expository) essay it is stated in the **first (introductory) paragraph**. In imaginative writing (a short story) it is implied in the **conflict** and need not be in the first paragraph.
- **The thesis statement is to an essay what the topic sentence is to a paragraph.**
- **4 types of essay** (by content, goal or strategy): **narrative, analytical, persuasive (argumentative), descriptive**. Keep them distinct; do not confuse the method of one with another.

1. **Introduction:** states the **thesis**, sets the **tone**, gives the **plan** of the essay; arouses interest.
2. **Middle (main body):** develops the thesis, one idea per paragraph.
3. **Conclusion:** signals the **idea of concluding**, looks back over the argument (a **flashback** or recap) and gives a **resolution of the thesis**.

- **3 main features of a full essay:** (1) appropriate choice of words and acceptable sentences; (2) good paragraphing and grammatical sentences; (3) coherence and use of standard writing conventions.
- **3 stages of the writing process:** **pre-writing** (planning, **brainstorming**: jotting down every idea on the topic before organising them), **writing** (drafting), **proof-reading** (checking and correcting). **Publishing is not a stage.** Brainstorming is a fundamental of writing a good essay.

**Explain more**

- Learning takes place when you can compare issues, define, classify, analyse and illustrate phenomena; your writing shows your competence and comprehension.
- Trap: the three parts of good writing are **introduction, middle, conclusion**, not "introduction, summary, conclusion" (the summary belongs in the conclusion).
- Trap: a **proposal** and an **experimental** essay are **not** essay types. "Expository" is another name for the factual, analytical essay; "argumentative" is another name for persuasive.
- A **synopsis** is a summary; a **theme** is the general subject area; the **thesis** is the point you prove.

### Lesson 8.2: The four types of essay

**Must know**

**1. Narrative essay**

- A **sequential or chronological account** of an experience, procedure or incident; largely **reportorial**. Ranges from a field-trip account to biography, autobiography and fiction. A **police statement** (what happened, in order) is narrative writing.
- **Chief aim:** to **arouse and sustain interest**; to make the reader feel a good story has been told.
- Features: **story line, plot, suspense** and other rhetorical devices. It does **not** need a strong argument.
- It **emphasises character and setting** so the reader identifies with the subject. It should be **racy** (lively), **captivating** and **plausibly handled**. It lacks the complexity of other types.

**2. Analytical essay**

- The type a university student writes most. The writer identifies an issue, **examines** it in detail and **assesses its implications for human existence**. It does **not** give a chronological account.
- **2 forms (by strategy of rendition):** **rationalisations** (explain the state of things by interpreting facts or data so the reader can form a judgement) and **critiques** (review an issue, experiment or creative work by making **statements on its quality**; must rest on reliable and verifiable authority, fact, logic, clear thinking and valid opinion). **Criticism = the informed evaluation of imaginative writing.** Critique suits the humanities and social sciences.
- **Empirical method:** self-evident or verifiable facts; results come instantly without controversy (an object thrown up falls). Suits the **natural sciences**.
- Interpreting the results of experiments may need **rationalisation, critique and empirical methods** combined (all three).
- A good analytical essay rests on **clear thinking, accurate or verified data, reliable authority and a clear conclusion** from the evidence, and separates the writer's opinion from facts.

**3. Descriptive essay**

- A **detailed and often dispassionate account of people, incidents, scenes and situations** so the reader can imagine and visualise them as a participant/observer; it **creates a mental picture**.
- Often part of fiction, biography or a **travelogue** (account of a travel). Objective: an overall impression that aligns the reader with the writer's view, by appealing to **visual and sensory perceptions**.
- **2 requirements:** (1) **appropriate wording**: a large stock of visual and sensory vocabulary; the essay is **shorn of personal feelings** so the writer stays invisible; (2) **keen observation**: authentic details; noticing what is uncommon to the general eye.

**4. Argumentative (persuasive) essay**

- The writer tries to get readers to **accept a point of view**. Goal: to **sell an idea**, **convince readers** to make up or change their minds, **persuade the audience** (all three). The writer **takes a stand**. Argument is at the core of most university writing.
- **2 strategies:** (1) **the use of details**: reasoning that is **inductive** (particular to general) or **deductive** (general to specific), using statistics, illustrations, justifications, cause/effect relations, analogies and appeals to authorities; (2) **appeal to the emotion**: logic alone cannot win a reader; build a friendly disposition, show authority, arrange ideas for emotional impact.
- **Not** necessary in an argument: creating a mental picture (that is description).

**Explain more**

- Narrative: a narration fails when the subject is commonplace or has a trite moral; the reader says "and so what?" Fantasy works (science and detective fiction) if plausibly handled. Sample: "A Face in the Night" by Ruskin Bond (Mr Oliver and the faceless boy at Simla); its force lies in character, word choice and brevity.
- Analytical example: an essay on a major decision (postgraduate study versus paid employment): state the decision in the first paragraph; give each reason its own paragraph. The sample on a mother giving up her baby for adoption (reasons: her age, her parents' problems, her own future) illustrates **rationalisation**.
- Descriptive samples: "In the Jungle" (Gerald Durrell) emphasises **size** with visual words ("tremendous", "choked", "ran riot"); "jungle" appears only in the title. "The Open Boat" (Stephen Crane) uses colour, shape, sound and smell to evoke doom; the mood is **dejection and despair**.
- Descriptive trap: words like "dirty", "clean", "untidy" (describing a slum) and superlatives like "wonderful", "excellent", "best" rob the essay of **detachment**; use them only where justified.
- Persuasive sample: "The Crime of Imprisonment" uses **reasoning by analogy** (prisoner compared with a pneumonia patient punished and cured at once). Prisons aim to be **retributory, deterrent and reformative**, yet men are not improved by injuries. The analogy works as an extended metaphor, appealing to emotion and logic.
- Trap: a **police statement** is narrative in method (events in order), but a police report, an annual general meeting report and a sports report count as **expository** (they set out facts). A **newspaper article** is not classed as expository because it may narrate, describe, argue or entertain.

### Lesson 8.3: The COEM factor and connecting paragraphs

**Must know**

- **COEM = Content, Organisation, Expression, Mechanics:** the four quality-control components that separate a well-executed essay from a poor one.

1. **Content:** the **depth of treatment** of the subject; the authority or knowledge brought to it. Called the **"meat of the essay"**. Two rules: (a) **limit or narrow the subject** to achieve depth, since content is a function of size: "population growth" is too large; "Africa's Demographic Multiplications: Implications for the Continent's Economic Development" is manageable; (b) **maintain a central idea** (the thesis), which guides selection of material and keeps you focused.
2. **Organisation:** arranging materials in a **logical order** to create the desired effect. Develop (a) a suitable **introduction**: perhaps the **most important part**; arouses and sustains interest; may range from a definition to general ideas; rests on a strong thesis; (b) the **main body**: at the writer's discretion, but move from **familiar to unfamiliar**, simple to complex; (c) a fitting **conclusion**: inferences drawn from linking the facts presented; **striking, memorable and definitive**.
3. **Expression:** discriminating choice of **suitable words** in simple, clear, unambiguous language. Writers lack the speaker's feedback (questions, gestures, tone of voice), so the key is to be **precise, concise and economical**. Two steps: (a) **avoid jargon, deodorant language and highfalutin expressions**; (b) **be concrete** ("Mrs Babatunde mailed the agreement to me" names the performer; "The agreement was mailed to me" is non-concrete). Use clear, concise, forceful sentences and a friendly, natural, courteous tone.
4. **Mechanics:** meaningful items in a sentence expressed in **symbols rather than words**; the technical term is **punctuation**. Mechanics of writing also covers **capitalisation** and **spelling and letter shapes** (all of these).

**Connecting paragraphs**

- **Vary paragraph length.** Short paragraphs let the reader grasp facts quickly but become **choppy** if overused; long ones become **boring**. A good essay has **a variation of long and short paragraphs**, which improves continuity and the logic of thought.
- Each paragraph communicates a **single idea** stated in a **topic sentence**, clarified by examples, details and descriptions.
- Paragraphs are related by **linking devices (linking expressions)**: first; second; finally; in contrast with; compared to the above; following upon; furthermore; in addition; however. Their function is to specify **continuity** by establishing a clear relationship with the idea already expressed.

**Explain more**

- COEM traps: not "Entertainment", "Comment" or "Orientation". Content is what the essay says; expression is how; organisation is the order; mechanics is the symbols.
- Depth trap: to achieve depth you **narrow** the subject, never expand it. A broad subject is merely skimmed.
- The inflated passage on "organisational behaviour" says, in fanciful words, only that the field has grown greatly and is now a separate discipline. Inflated vocabulary conceals near emptiness of idea and draws attention to the writer, not the subject.

### Lesson 8.4: Punctuation marks and their functions

**Must know**

- **Punctuation marks:** symbols used to organise and structure expressions so as to convey the right meaning.
- The frequently used marks: full stop (.), question mark (?), exclamation mark (!), quotation marks (" "), square brackets [ ], comma (,), semi-colon (;), colon (:), capital letters, hyphen (-), dash (–), ellipsis (...), apostrophe ('), parentheses or round brackets ( ).
- **4 general functions:** (a) integral to the meaning of the sentence; (b) signify completion or absence of completion of thought; (c) delineate attitudes or idiosyncrasies of speakers or writers; (d) demonstrate variations of pitch or intonation. Punctuation also separates subordinate ideas from major ones, marks stretches of direct speech and shows the possessive case. It does **not** indicate a writer's background.

1. **Full stop (period):** ends a complete **declarative** or **imperative** sentence. Also marks off **abbreviations and initials** (Mr., Prof., Ph.D., E. A. Adedun). Omitted in **acronyms** (UNICEF, NEPA, FIFA, ASUU).
2. **Exclamation mark:** ends a structure expressing **surprise, horror, disappointment, anger, indeed all forms of excitement**; strong feeling, a warning or forceful command; punctuates **interjections** (Move! Look out! Oh, boy!). Hardly used in formal business writing.
3. **Question mark:** ends a **direct question** (interrogative sentence). **Never** after an **indirect (reported) question**: "The manager demanded to know why production was slow."
4. **Colon:** introduces a **list**, especially after "these", "namely", "the following", "as follows". Also introduces **direct speech** after a speaker's name in dialogue ("Okon said: 'I am hungry.'") and the **publisher's name** in a reference entry (Ibadan: Heinemann).
5. **Semi-colon:** (a) **separates independent clauses**, replacing a comma plus coordinating conjunction ("Supply us with three cars; they must all be black"); (b) separates **phrases and clauses in a series** that already contain commas; (c) sets off conjunctive adverbs (however, therefore, moreover) joining independent clauses.
6. **Comma:** separates words, phrases, clauses, items and non-essentials; sets off a state from a town, a street from a city, the day from the month. The comma before "and" in a list is the **serial comma**. Never put a comma between a **subject and its verb**.
7. **Dash and hyphen:** the difference is **context, not length**. The **dash** works in **syntactic** constructions and introduces **additional information** ("All managers – I mean most managers – look forward to vacations"). The **hyphen** works in **lexical** constructions (ex-senator, sister-in-law, bye-laws).
8. **Parentheses (round brackets):** mark off **additional or explanatory information** the sentence could do without ("management (the decision-making body)"). Brackets also enclose a **reference**. Commas, dashes and parentheses differ in **degree**: commas for closely related material, dashes to emphasise interruptions, parentheses for the least essential extras.
9. **Square brackets:** enclose information **inserted into a quotation** for clarity.
10. **Apostrophe:** shows **omission (contractions)**: won't; and **possession**: Ade's salary, Moses' allowance. **Never** with possessive pronouns: theirs, yours, **its** ("the dog wagged its tail"; "it's" means "it is"). Never used simply to form plurals.
11. **Quotation marks (inverted commas):** used **in pairs** to enclose a third party's exact words (direct speech): Okon said: "I am hungry." Not used for indirect speech: Okon said that he was hungry.
12. **Ellipsis:** three dots showing items omitted from a quotation; **four dots** if the omission is at the end of a sentence (the fourth is the full stop).
13. **Capitalisation:** see Lesson 8.5.

- **Terminal punctuation marks** (they end a sentence): **full stop, question mark, exclamation mark**. The comma is **not** terminal.

**Explain more**

- Unpunctuated: "william shakespeare 1564-1616 married a woman eight years his senior when he was eighteen." Punctuated: "William Shakespeare (1564-1616) married a woman eight years his senior when he was eighteen." Likewise: "...his collection, *Girls at War*, took place on a cold December day in Lagos."
- In Oswald Mtshali's poem a dead mother tells her son "There is no heaven / Above the clouds"; his reply "WHAT!" is neither question nor statement. Capitals plus exclamation mark pack in his surprise, horror and shock: mechanics introducing figurative language.
- Serial comma: "The General Manager, Production Manager and Operations Manager are going" could mean the GM is being told two people are going; "...Production Manager, and Operations Manager..." makes three travellers clear. Insert it for clarity.
- Paired-mark trap: only **quotation marks** come in pairs; question mark, hyphen and apostrophe are single.
- Comma-splice trap: a comma alone cannot separate two independent clauses; use a semi-colon.
- Colon-use trap: the colon's three uses are the list, direct speech and the publisher's name in a reference entry. Breaking a title into two parts is **not** one of them.
- Poor punctuation distorts meaning; attention to these "minor details" can determine an employee's career.

### Lesson 8.5: Mechanics, spelling and other kinds of writing

**Must know**

**Capitalisation rules.** Use a capital letter:

1. at the **beginning of a sentence** and after every terminal mark (full stop, question mark, exclamation mark);
2. for the pronoun **"I"**, wherever it occurs (an isolated "I");
3. for **proper nouns** (John, Oshogbo, Africa) and **proper adjectives** of nationality and language (Chilean, Guatemalan, Gaelic, French);
4. for names of **deities and sacred contexts** (God, Allah, the Bible);
5. for **principal words in titles, headings and sub-headings** (*Elements of English Grammar and Usage*), and named courses (History);
6. for **acronyms** (NFL, PTA, UNICEF) and letter-based words (U-turn).

- **Do not** capitalise **common nouns** (guesstimates, parsnip) unless they begin a sentence, and do not capitalise after a comma unless the word is a proper noun.

**Spelling.** Learn these correctly spelled words: **harassment** (one r, two s), **embarrassment** (two r, two s), **accommodation** (two c, two m), **sheriff** (one r, two f), **souvenir** (not -ier), **chauffeur**, **parachutist**, **adolescence** (-esc-, -ence), **shrivel** (-el), **clammy** (two m), **sphinx**, **repertoire**, **vagrant**, **resinous**, **communication**, **swinging**.

**Reports and technical writing**

- **Report:** a faithful account of an activity in which the writer participated; it provides **information to influence decisions and actions**. A report must be based on **facts**, not hearsay or arguments.
- **Objectivity:** the highest degree is expected in **scientific writing**.
- **Technical writing:** factual, practical, specialised writing: a **car manual, police reports, law reports**. **Not** love poems. **SME = Subject Matter Expert**, the specialist whose knowledge ensures the content is accurate.
- Not reports: a **memorandum** (brief internal message), an **agenda** (list of meeting items), **anecdotes** (short stories), **prolegomena** (preliminary introduction to a book), an **episode** (one event in a story).

**Parts of a formal letter**

1. Writer's address and **date**.
2. **Inside address:** the addressee's name, **designation** and address.
3. **Salutation:** Dear Sir / Dear Madam / Dear Mr Okon.
4. **Heading (subject line)**.
5. **Body** of the letter.
6. **Subscription** (complimentary close): **Yours faithfully** (after Dear Sir/Madam) or **Yours sincerely** (after a named person).
7. **Signature** and the writer's name.

**Explain more**

- Spotting a wrongly capitalised sentence: check the first letter ("a parsnip is a root vegetable..." fails), then proper nouns and acronyms ("The nfl will repeat..." fails; it should be NFL).
- Spelling traps: "harrassment" copies the double r of "embarrassment"; "souvenier" adds an unneeded e; "Adolescense" ends wrongly in -ense; "adolescents" are the people, "adolescence" is the stage (the awkward stage); "parachutis" has lost its final t; "clamy" has lost an m.
- Letter trap: the "subscription" is the close before the signature, not the salutation, the addressee's designation or the body.

### One-minute recap

- Writing is the most permanent record of your view; it needs training, tact, imagination, rigour.
- Essay = several paragraphs on a central subject; the thesis is the point to prove, stated in the first paragraph; thesis : essay :: topic sentence : paragraph.
- Four essay types: narrative (chronological, suspense, character and setting), analytical (rationalisations and critiques), descriptive (dispassionate, visual and sensory), argumentative/persuasive (details and appeal to emotion).
- COEM: Content (meat; narrow the subject), Organisation (introduction, body, conclusion), Expression (precise, concise, concrete), Mechanics (punctuation).
- Vary paragraph length; link paragraphs with linking expressions.
- Full stop/period ends statements and marks initials; colon introduces lists and direct speech; semi-colon separates independent clauses; exclamation mark for interjections and excitement; question mark only for direct questions; apostrophe for contractions and possession (never its/theirs/yours); quotation marks in pairs; dash for added information; parentheses for explanatory extras.
- Terminal marks: full stop, question mark, exclamation mark.
- Capitalise sentence starts, "I", proper nouns and adjectives, deities, titles, acronyms; not common nouns.
- Reports rest on facts and inform decisions; scientific writing is most objective; SME = subject matter expert; the subscription is "Yours faithfully".

### Theory practice

*Answer outline:* Define COEM as the four quality-control components of an essay. Then 2-3 lines each: Content (depth of treatment, the "meat"; narrow the subject; keep a central idea or thesis), Organisation (logical ordering; suitable introduction, body from familiar to unfamiliar, striking conclusion), Expression (precise, concise, concrete words; avoid jargon and highfalutin language), Mechanics (punctuation and related symbols that convey the right meaning). Close by saying that together they distinguish a well-executed essay from a poorly rendered one.
