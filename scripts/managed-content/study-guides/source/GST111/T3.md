## Topic 3: Speaking Skills II: The Syllable, Stress and Intonation

**What you must know:** what a syllable is and its structure (onset, peak, coda; C0-3VC0-4); acceptable and unacceptable consonant clusters; stress, the four factors of prominence and the three levels of stress; predicting stress phonologically, syntactically and morphologically; intonation, its three functions and the uses of the four tunes.

### Lesson 3.1: The English syllable

**Must know**

- **Segmentals:** the individual consonant and vowel sounds.
- **Suprasegmentals:** features that apply to units higher than the segments (the syllable and beyond), such as **stress** and **intonation**. They are "above the segments" but still need them to function.
- **Syllable:** a distinct group of sounds that can be pronounced in one moment of uninterrupted breath. It usually consists of a vowel which may be preceded or followed by one or more consonants. Easy to identify, hard to define: *education* has four syllables.
- **Two phonetic theories of the syllable:**

1. **Chest Pulse Theory:** the syllable equals a single puff of air by the intercostal muscles of the chest cavity.
2. **Prominence Theory:** the syllable is the most prominent, relatively loudest or most sonorous sound in a phonetic environment.

- **Phonological definition:** looks at how English phonemes combine. A syllable has three parts:

1. **Onset:** all the consonants before the vowel (optional).
2. **Peak:** the vowel itself (compulsory; the only obligatory element).
3. **Coda:** all the consonants after the vowel (optional).

- **Formula:** **C0-3VC0-4**. Onset: 0 to **3** consonants (maximum 3 at the beginning). Peak: an obligatory vowel. Coda: 0 to **4** consonants (maximum 4 at the end).
- **Consonant cluster:** two or more consonants at the beginning or end of a syllable with no vowel between them, e.g. *spray* /spreɪ/, *street* /striːt/, *tasks* /tɑːsks/, *bagged* /bægd/.
- **Open syllable:** no coda (ends in the vowel), e.g. *slow* /sləʊ/, *tea* /tiː/. A CV syllable is open.
- **Closed syllable:** has a coda (ends in a consonant), e.g. *slap* /slæp/, *tag* /tæg/.
- **Minimal syllable:** a single sound preceded and followed by silence, e.g. *are* /ɑː/, *or* /ɔː/, *err* /ɜː/; also /m/ (agreement) and /ʃ/ (asking for silence). The dominant single-sound syllable is the **schwa /ə/**, an unstressed vowel, as in *about* /əbaʊt/, *amiss* /əmɪs/.

**Explain more**

- Phonetically, a syllable has a **centre** with little or no obstruction to airflow and relative loudness (the vowel), surrounded by greater obstruction and less loudness (the consonants).
- Syllable structures (C = consonant, V = vowel):

| Structure | Examples |
|---|---|
| V | *I* /aɪ/, *eye*, *oh* /əʊ/ |
| CV | *boy* /bɔɪ/, *no* |
| VC | *in* /ɪn/, *up* |
| CVC | *pick* /pɪk/, *sock* |
| CCV | *slow* /sləʊ/, *glue* |
| CCVC | *prayed* /preɪd/, *glued* |
| CCVCC | *prank* /præŋk/, *cracked* /krækt/ |
| CCCV | *spree* /spriː/, *straw* |
| CCCVC | *stretch* /stretʃ/ |
| CCCVCC | *sprint* /sprɪnt/ |
| CVCCCC | *waltzed* /wɒltst/ |
| CCCVCCCC | *strengths* /streŋkθs/ |

- Count sounds, not letters: *eye* is one vowel sound (V); *cracked* ends in /kt/; *axe* is VCC; *through* is CCV.
- **Restrictions on onset and coda:**
  - /ŋ/ and /ʒ/ do **not** occur in the onset (so /ŋɔɪz/ is unacceptable).
  - /h/, /r/, /w/ and /j/ do **not** occur in the coda (so /lɔːw/ and /hɑːsh/ are unacceptable).
  - A **three-consonant onset** always has **/s/** as the first consonant, **/p/, /t/ or /k/** as the second, and **/l/, /r/, /w/ or /j/** as the third: *strike* /straɪk/, *square* /skweə/, *stew* /stjuː/, *sprite* /spraɪt/. Any other three-consonant onset is deviant and unacceptable in English.
- Trap: the compulsory constituent of a syllable is the **vowel**, not the consonant, the schwa or a pause. The maximum onset is **3**, not 4; four consonants are possible only in the coda (*strengths*).

### Lesson 3.2: Stress, prominence and levels of stress

**Must know**

- **Stress:** the relative force of articulation or degree of auditory prominence of one syllable over others in a polysyllabic word. The prominence of one syllable over the others in a word is a function of **stress**.
- Stress can fall on any syllable. First (initial): *REAson, MESsage, JAnitor*. Second: *anNOUNcer, inTENtion, creAtion*. Final: *reCORD* (verb), *agGRIEVED, reLENT*.
- **Two perspectives on stress:**

1. **Production:** the speaker uses more muscular energy for stressed syllables.
2. **Perception:** all stressed syllables share one thing, **prominence**.

- **Four factors of prominence:**

1. **Loudness:** stressed syllables are louder.
2. **Length:** a longer syllable is heard as stressed.
3. **Pitch:** the rise and fall of the tone of the voice, from the speed of vocal cord vibration. A syllable said on a noticeably higher pitch than its neighbours is heard as prominent.
4. **(Vowel) quality:** a syllable with a **strong** vowel quality attracts stress.

- **Pitch** produces the **strongest effect** on stress placement of all four factors.
- **Strong vowels** (usually stressed): long vowels /iː, ɜː, ɑː, uː, ɔː/, diphthongs and triphthongs. **Weak vowels** (usually unstressed): /ə/, /ɪ/, /ʊ/. Examples: *aBOUT, rePORT, reUnion, FURther, anNOYance, TEAcher*.
- **Marking stress:** a mark (ˈ) at the beginning of the stressed syllable, or capital letters: *ˈrebel* REbel, *acˈcount* acCOUNT, *ocˈcasion* ocCAsion, *biˈnoculars* biNOculars.
- **Three levels of stress:**

| Level | Mark | Description | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| Primary | raised stroke (ˈ) | the strongest stress; results from pitch movement within a word | withDRAWal, condemNAtion, POSTman, toGEther, inviTAtion |
| Secondary | subscript (ˌ) | weaker than primary, stronger than unstressed | first syllable of *millionaire* /ˌmɪljəˈneə/, *cigarette* /ˌsɪgəˈret/; second syllable of *congratulation*, *responsibility* |
| Zero (unstressed) | none | no recognisable prominence; weak vowels | first syllable of *retain, opinion*; middle of *intimate, custody*; last of *flavour, mother* |

- A word usually has **one** primary-stressed syllable; longer words may add one secondary-stressed syllable and several unstressed ones.

**Explain more**

- **Syllable and stress:** (a) stressed/accented = said with greater energy or breath force; unstressed/unaccented = not; (b) a stressed syllable is more prominent than the sounds around it; (c) word stress operates only on words of **more than one syllable**. The schwa /ə/ and the **syllabic consonants /l, m, n/** usually occur in unstressed syllables (*about, bottle, cotton*).
- **Diphthongs** do **not** usually occur in unstressed syllables; the schwa, short vowels and syllabic consonants do.
- The four factors usually work together, but prominence can come from only one or two.
- Trap: **voice** (vocal cord vibration that separates /b/ from /p/) is **not** a factor of prominence; the four are loudness, length, pitch, quality.
- Trap: zero stress means **unstressed** syllables. There is no "tertiary" stress.
- Secondary stress often sits on the syllable that carried primary stress **before** a stress-moving affix was added: *reˈsponsible* becomes /rɪˌspɒnsɪˈbɪlɪti/, correctly marked **responsiˈbility**, not reˈsponsibility.
- **Sentence stress:** content words (nouns, main verbs, adjectives) are usually stressed; **function words** (*the, of, and, a*) are usually **unstressed** in connected speech, never "always stressed".

### Lesson 3.3: Predicting stress: phonological and syntactic criteria

**Must know**

- English stress is **free** (not fixed): it can fall on the first, second, third or last syllable, so many learners learn each word individually. Still, **three criteria** help predict it:

1. **Phonological:** the number of syllables and their internal constitution (syllable structure, strong or weak vowels).
2. **Syntactic:** the word class (part of speech).
3. **Morphological:** the internal structure of the word (simple, compound or complex; affixes).

- Stress is **not** predicted historically or semantically.
- **Phonological criterion:** weak syllables (/ə, ɪ, ʊ/) are unstressed; strong syllables (long vowels, diphthongs, triphthongs, consonant clusters) are stressed. With two strong vowels, the stronger gets primary stress, the other secondary. Six rules:

1. **Disyllabic nouns:** short vowel in the final syllable → stress the **first** syllable: *TIcket, ASset, PREmise, FOcus, MOney, PIDgin, TODdler*.
2. **Disyllabic nouns:** long vowel or diphthong in the final syllable → stress the **final** syllable: *esTATE, balLOON, canTEEN, maCHINE, rouTINE*.
3. **Trisyllabic nouns:** a final syllable with a short vowel or the diphthong /əʊ/ does **not** attract stress: *toMORrow, anNOUNCEment, reJOINder, acCEPTance*.
4. **Trisyllabic nouns:** a penultimate (second) syllable with a long vowel, a diphthong other than /əʊ/, or a consonant-cluster coda **is** stressed: *poTAto, syNOPsis, diSASter, torNAdo, arRIval, conJUNCtion*.
5. **Disyllabic adjectives:** open final syllable or consonant-cluster coda on the final syllable → stress the **final** syllable: *seVERE, corRECT, diRECT, adHERE, aGHAST*.
6. **Trisyllabic verbs:** final syllable with a short vowel or a one-consonant coda → stress the **second (penultimate)** syllable: *enCOUNter, imPREGnate, maRAUder, conSIder, soLIcit*.

- **Syntactic criterion:** in noun-verb (and noun-adjective) pairs, the **noun** takes stress on the **first** syllable and the **verb** on the **second**:

| Noun (first syllable) | Verb/Adjective (second syllable) |
|---|---|
| EXport | exPORT (V) |
| REcord | reCORD (V) |
| ABstract | absTRACT (V) |
| AUgust (N) | auGUST (Adj) |
| PREsent (Adj) | preSENT (V) |

- **Exceptions** (same stress in more than one class): *PREsent* (N/Adj), *INterest* (N/V), *CHALlenge* (N/V), *miSTAKE* (N/V), *COMfort* (N/V), *reSPECT* (N/V), *HARvest* (N/V); *adVICE* (N), *adVISE* (V). So *challenge* is **CHALlenge/CHALlenge** as noun and verb.
- **Compound noun vs noun phrase:** a **compound noun** is stressed on the **first** element; a **noun phrase** is stressed on the **second (rightmost)** element.

| Compound noun (first element) | Noun phrase (second element) |
|---|---|
| GREENhouse (glass building for plants) | a green HOUSE (a house that is green) |
| BLACKbird | a black BIRD |
| ENGLISH-teacher | an English TEACHER |
| WHITE house | a white HOUSE |

**Explain more**

- A compound noun is a **single entity** whose meaning may or may not relate to its parts (*Facebook*). A noun phrase is **two separate syntactic elements**, preceded by an article (*a/an*), in which the first element modifies the second.
- Trap: "based on syllable structure" = **phonological**; "based on word class" = **syntactic**; "based on internal structure/affixes" = **morphological**.
- **Matching stress patterns** (a common exam task: pick the word with the same stress as the given one):
  - First syllable (like *ˈhumble*): *STANdard, SADdle, PUnish, MARvel, PREtext, COMpact* (noun), *ACtivist, COMpany*.
  - Second syllable (like *comˈplaint*, *conˈcern*): *sucCESS, rePORT, reGRET, reTURN, comBINE* (verb), *ocCURrence, inSURance, amBItion, reACtion*; like *proˈfessor*: *creAtor*.
  - Third syllable (like *indiˈcation*): *eduCAtion*. Words ending in **-tion** stress the syllable **just before** the ending.
  - Like *ˈcapitalism*: *REgionalism*. The suffix **-ism** does not move stress, so the word keeps the stress of its base (*CApital, REgion*).

### Lesson 3.4: Predicting stress: the morphological criterion

**Must know**

- Words are morphologically **simple**, **compound** or **complex**; each structure guides stress.
- **Simple words** (single-base morphemes): one meaningful unit, e.g. *table, glass, window, boy*. Their stress is found phonologically or syntactically.
- **Compound words:** two words that can each exist independently. Written three ways:

1. One word: *suitcase, blackboard, windscreen*.
2. Hyphenated: *fruit-cake, baby-sitter, mother-tongue*.
3. Two words with a space: *child care, credit card, income tax, heart attack*.

- **Compound stress rules** (determined by word class):

1. **Compound nouns** (especially noun + noun): stress on the **first** element: *ˈhandbag, ˈguest house, ˈtea-party, ˈbookshelf, ˈcity mall*.
2. **Compound adjectives** with an adjectival first element and **-ed** at the end: stress on the **second** element: *ill-ˈmannered, well-ˈbred, heavy-ˈhanded*.
3. Compounds whose first element is a **number** (ordinal or cardinal): stress on the **second** element: *two-ˈseater, five-ˈstar, second-ˈhand*.
4. **Compound adverbs** (adverbial element + noun): stress on the **second** element: *up-ˈcountry, down-ˈstream, inside-ˈout*.
5. **Compound verbs** with an adverbial first element: stress on the **final** element: *down-ˈsize, back-ˈpedal, ill-ˈtreat*.

- **Complex words:** two or more elements, formed by **affixation**, e.g. *boys, unrepentant, disloyal, international, endearment*. **Prefixes** go before the base (*un-* in *unpleasant*); **suffixes** go after (*-ness* in *fairness*).
- **Three types of affix by effect on stress:**

1. **Stress-bearing affixes:** the affix itself carries primary stress: *semi-* (*ˈsemicircle*), *-eer* (*engiˈneer*), *-aire* (*millioˈnaire*), *-ette* (*cigaˈrette*).
2. **Stress-neutral affixes:** the word keeps its original stress: *un-* and *-ing* (*ˈpleasant* / *unˈpleasant*; *ˈmarket* / *ˈmarketing*).
3. **Stress-moving affixes:** stress stays on the stem but shifts to a different syllable: *-ic* and *-ity* (*ˈmagnet* / *magˈnetic*; *ˈinfidel* / *infiˈdelity*).

**Explain more**

- Trap: "stress-conserving" is **not** a type of affix. The three types are bearing, neutral and moving.
- *-ism* behaves as stress-neutral (*CApitalism, REgionalism*); *-ity* and *-tion* move stress to the syllable before them (*responsiˈbility*, *indiˈcation*).
- Compound nouns are the only compound type stressed on the first element; adjective, number, adverb and verb compounds all stress the **second** element.

### Lesson 3.5: Intonation and the four tunes

**Must know**

- **Intonation:** the use of the **pitch of the voice** to convey meaning or syntactic information; the distinctive use of patterns of pitch or melody in an utterance; the rise and fall of the tone of the voice. Intonation in English is a matter of **sentence stress**. It is the melody of speech.
- No language is spoken in a monotone. **Tone:** the overall behaviour of pitch in speech.
- Pitch changes are linguistic only when they affect meaning (the pitch changes of someone speaking while running are not): *You are travelling today* on a **falling** tone = statement; on a **rising** tone = question or doubt/surprise. Intonation in speech equals **punctuation** in writing.
- **Three functions of intonation:**

1. **Grammatical:** shows the communicative function of a sentence (statement, question, exclamation).
2. **Attitudinal:** betrays the speaker's emotion or attitude (disinterest, sarcasm, boredom; anger, excitement, impatience).
3. **Discourse:** guides turn-taking in conversation (end-of-utterance patterns differ from mid-utterance ones), and points to the focus and implied meanings. *Yes/no* on a falling tone = definiteness or finality; on a rising tone = interest or continuity.

- **Four intonation tunes:** falling, rising, falling-rising, rising-falling.
- **Falling tune**, five uses:

1. **Declarative statements:** *We shall travel tomorrow.*
2. **Imperatives** (commands, instructions, advice): *Look at the blackboard.*
3. **Wh-questions:** *What did you see? Where is your sister?*
4. **Tag questions seeking confirmation:** *It is true, isn't it?*
5. **Exclamations:** *How brilliant he is! What a horrible accident!*

- **Rising tune**, four uses:

1. **Polar (yes/no) questions:** *Do you know his house? Can we go now?*
2. **Polite requests** (interrogative or imperative form): *Kindly shut the door. Could you lend me some cash?*
3. **Requests for repetition** (wh-question or echo question): *What did she say? Bisi has travelled?*
4. **Tag questions seeking information:** *He has left, hasn't he?*

- **Falling-rising tune**, two uses:

1. **Counting or listing:** each item takes a fall-rise until the last, which falls: *We can invite students, lawyers, soldiers, politicians and workers too.*
2. **Sentence modifiers (adverbials):** *Fortunately, it did not rain. Clearly, she cannot drive well.*

**Explain more**

- Trap: wh-questions take a **falling** tune; yes/no questions take a **rising** tune. A wh-question rises only when asking for repetition.
- Trap: tag questions take **both** tunes. Falling = seeking confirmation (you already believe it). Rising = seeking information (you really do not know).
- Trap: "statement, question or exclamation" = **grammatical** function; "emotion or attitude" = **attitudinal**; "turn-taking" = **discourse**. "Accentual" refers to stress placement, not sentence type.
- Trap: stress is prominence of a syllable within a **word**; intonation (not "rhythm") is pitch movement over an **utterance**.
- The **rising-falling** tune is the fourth tune; it is typically used to show strong feeling, such as approval, disapproval or surprise. Declaratives take the falling tune, not rise-fall.

### One-minute recap

- Suprasegmentals = stress and intonation, above the segments (consonants and vowels).
- Syllable = onset (optional) + peak (compulsory vowel) + coda (optional): **C0-3VC0-4**.
- Three-consonant onset = /s/ + /p, t, k/ + /l, r, w, j/. /ŋ, ʒ/ never start; /h, r, w, j/ never end a syllable.
- Open syllable = no coda (CV); closed = has coda. Minimal syllable = one sound.
- Stress = prominence of one syllable; four factors: loudness, length, pitch (strongest), quality. Not voice.
- Three levels: primary (ˈ), secondary (ˌ, subscript), zero (unstressed). One primary stress per word.
- English stress is free; predict it phonologically, syntactically, morphologically.
- Noun = first syllable, verb = second (EXport/exPORT); exception: CHALlenge (N/V). Compound noun = first element; noun phrase = second.
- Affixes: stress-bearing (-eer, -aire, -ette), stress-neutral (un-, -ing, -ism), stress-moving (-ic, -ity).
- Intonation = pitch for meaning; functions: grammatical, attitudinal, discourse. Falling: statements, commands, wh-questions, confirming tags, exclamations. Rising: yes/no questions, polite requests, repetition, information tags. Fall-rise: lists, adverbials.
