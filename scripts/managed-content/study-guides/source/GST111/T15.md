## Topic 15: Style Manual, Citation and Referencing

**What you must know:** copyright, fair use and unfair use; what plagiarism is (including self-plagiarism and cyber plagiarism), how to avoid it and its consequences; why we cite and what can be cited; the two parts of referencing and the five elements to record; which disciplines use APA, MLA, Harvard and Chicago; the in-text and reference-list rules of APA, Harvard and MLA with worked examples.

### Lesson 15.1: Copyright, fair use and unfair use

**Must know**

- **Copyright (author's right):** the law that says you **cannot use any creative work without the permission of the owner**. Copyrighted works are protected by **law**. Anyone found guilty of breaking it is **liable to prosecution under the law**.
- **Fair use:** "a judicial doctrine that refers to a use of copyrighted material that does not infringe or violate the exclusive rights of the copyright holder". In plain terms: fair use allows copyrighted works to be used **without permission, under certain conditions**, for **academic purposes, to a certain limit**.
- **Purposes allowed under fair use:** criticism, comment, news reporting, teaching and similar **non-commercial** purposes.
- **Works eligible for copyright** (World Intellectual Property Organization): books, music, paintings, sculpture, films, computer programs, databases, advertisements, maps, technical drawings.
- **Unfair use:** when a student or researcher **copies any copyrighted work indiscriminately, with or without acknowledgement**. Fair use has a limit; crossing it is unfair use.

**Explain more**

- As a student you rely on the documented knowledge of other people. Fair use makes that legal; acknowledgement keeps it honest.
- Trap: fair use is for **academic** purposes, not business, publishing or commercial purposes.
- Trap: the punishment for violating copyright law is **prosecution under the law**, not "jail the offender".
- Trap: **complimentary cards** are not on the WIPO list; books, music and paintings are.
- **Paraphrasing correctly**, with acknowledgement, is fair use. Copying indiscriminately is unfair use; using without acknowledgement is plagiarism.

### Lesson 15.2: Plagiarism and how to avoid it

**Must know**

- **Plagiarism:** from the Latin *plagiarius*, meaning **"kidnapper"**. It is the **act of using copyrighted work without permission or acknowledging the source**. In the academic environment it is called **"academic theft"** and **academic fraud**. A scholar who does not acknowledge all quoted and borrowed ideas as he reads and writes is guilty of plagiarism.
- **It covers:** other people's copyrighted work, an organisation's copyrighted work, and even your **own** work.
- **Self-plagiarism:** copying from your **previous assignment for a new assignment without acknowledgement**.
- **Cyber plagiarism:** using the **Internet** to appropriate the ideas or expression of another.
- **Rules for avoiding plagiarism** (any copyrighted work used must be duly acknowledged):

1. Internal citation or footnotes must be done **properly according to the prescribed reference style**.
2. Internal citation or footnotes must be **listed in the reference list or bibliography**.

- **Three things a student must learn** to avoid plagiarism:

1. How to **manage time** to leave enough time for academic work.
2. How to **paraphrase correctly**.
3. The **skill of citation**.

- **Paraphrase:** restating another writer's idea **in your own words**. A **direct quotation** uses the author's **exact words** and is shown with **quotation marks**. Both must be cited.

**Explain more**

- In one line: to avoid plagiarism, other people's ideas must be **paraphrased and cited**, and **all used works must be cited**. Consulting a lecturer, using the library or reading a manuscript are good habits but do not by themselves stop plagiarism.
- Trap: copying from a lecturer's handout or from the internet without acknowledgement is ordinary plagiarism, not self-plagiarism. Self-plagiarism is only about **your own** earlier work.
- Trap: paraphrasing is **not** quoting verbatim and **not** translating into another language.
- When taking notes, mark a direct quotation with **quotation marks** so you can tell it from your paraphrase later. Underlining or capitals are not the recognised way.
- Citation is a skill learnt for **academic purposes**.

### Lesson 15.3: Consequences of plagiarism and reasons for citing

**Must know**

- **5 consequences of plagiarism** (personal, professional and legal):

1. The **academic reputation** of the offender could be destroyed.
2. **Suspension or rustication** of the affected student.
3. **Summary dismissal** of an affected member of staff.
4. It can affect the **reputation of the institution**.
5. It can lead to **prosecution**.

- **6 reasons for citing** published works:

1. Acknowledging people's works and ideas is essential for **moral and legal** reasons.
2. To **give credit** to work done by others.
3. To **give direction** to prospective researchers on resources to consult.
4. **Verification** of citations in support of the author's arguments.
5. It **reveals the scope and quality** of the researcher's work.
6. Positions in a research work are **strengthened** when previous good sources are cited.

- **Sources that can be cited:** secondary sources (books, encyclopaedias, dictionaries, scholarly journals, newspapers, magazines), electronic resources, institutional sources, websites, and **grey literature** (theses and dissertations, technical reports, conference proceedings).

**Explain more**

- Trap: a student who plagiarises can be **dismissed from school** (suspension or rustication). "Jailed" is tempting because prosecution is possible, but the direct student penalty is removal from school.
- A **quotation** is used in a research write-up to **support an argument** with the author's own words (also to disagree with an author, compare views or note existing research), not to show off writing skills or vocabulary.
- Trap: **every** type of source listed can go in a reference list: books, chapters or articles in edited books, web pages, journal articles, newspapers, theses. None is excluded.

### Lesson 15.4: Referencing styles and what to record

**Must know**

- **Referencing style:** the **various ways of acknowledging literature sources consulted** in a piece of writing; a **set of rules** telling a researcher how to acknowledge the thoughts, ideas and works of others in a particular way. A crucial part of successful academic writing.
- **Style manual:** each style has a manual that guides its acceptable manner of citation and referencing.
- **Referencing has 2 parts:**

1. **In-text referencing (internal citation):** the brief citation in the **body of the manuscript**, right where the idea or words are used.
2. **Reference list (bibliography):** the full details, placed **at the end** of the assignment.

- **Rule:** any citation given in-text must appear in the reference list, **and vice versa**. **Only exception:** **personal communications** (conversations, lectures, email). They are non-print and are cited **in the body of the text only**.
- **5 key elements to record** for every source:

1. **Author** (how authors are cited varies by style).
2. **Title** (book: the title on the title page; anthology: the work's name and the book's title; periodical: the **article** name, not the periodical name).
3. **Publication information** (book: publisher and place; periodical: journal or magazine name, volume and issue numbers; website: the organisation that created the content).
4. **Dates** (record all date information).
5. **Access** (Web or print; URL or **Digital Object Identifier, DOI**).

- **Styles and disciplines:**

| Style | Full name | Used mostly by |
|---|---|---|
| **APA** | American Psychological Association | **Social sciences** (and sciences) |
| **MLA** | Modern Language Association | **Composition** and language courses |
| **Chicago** | Chicago style | **Humanities** |
| **Harvard** | Harvard (author-date) | University students generally |

**Explain more**

- A full bibliography entry shows **all** of: author's name and year, title and place of publication, and publisher. A bibliography is arranged **alphabetically** and sits at the **end** of the work. A **keyword** never appears in a reference entry; author, title and page numbers do.
- Trap: "NLA", "ALA" and "MPA" are **not** referencing styles. The four to know are **APA, MLA, Harvard, Chicago**.
- Trap: "In-text citation appears in the body of the manuscript" is **true**, fully, not partially.
- You will likely use different styles during your degree; the style depends on your **discipline**, so always follow the citation guide your lecturer assigns.

### Lesson 15.5: APA style

**Must know**

- **APA** is most commonly used in the **social sciences**, popular worldwide, has changed often and (as taught here) is in its **6th edition**.
- **In-text:** author's **surname, year of publication**, and **page number only for a direct quotation**.

1. Quotations of **less than 40 words**: inside the text, in **double quotation marks " "**.
2. Quotations of **more than 40 words**: a **block** on a new line, **indented 5 spaces** from the left margin, **no quotation marks**.
3. **No page numbers** when you summarise or paraphrase.

- **Reference list** rules:

1. **Alphabetical order** by the author's **surname**.
2. **No author:** use the **title** in the author's place.
3. **Italics** for book and journal titles, websites and the **volume number** of a journal. The **issue number** is **not** in italics.
4. Lines after the first line of an entry are **indented 5 spaces** (hanging indent).

- **Authors:** 2 authors: use **&** inside parentheses and **and** in the sentence. 3 to 5 authors: all surnames the first time, then **et al.** 6 or more: **et al.** from the **first** in-text citation. In the reference list, with **more than 6 authors**, list the first six and then write **et al.** in place of the rest. The exam's wording for this rule is: after the sixth author, do **not** list any more names; write **et al.** in their place.

| Source | In-text citation | Reference list entry |
|---|---|---|
| Book | John states "all of us need reassurance occasionally" (2002, p. 77). *or* (John, 2002, p. 77) | John, P. (2002). *Mental health and mental illness* (7th ed.). Philadelphia: Lippincott. |
| Journal article | Gregory and Verdouw (2005) explain… *or* (Gregory & Verdouw, 2005) | Gregory, S., & Verdouw, J. (2005). Therapeutic touch: Its application for residents in aged care. *Australian Nursing Journal, 12*(7), 23-25. |
| Web document | (Diabetes WA, 2006) | Diabetes WA. (2006). *Gestational*. Retrieved October 17, 2008, from http://diabeteswa.com.au/Default.aspx?f=pv&ctr=Default&mid=133&pid=433&page_id=436 |

- Order: **Author. (Year). Title. Place: Publisher.** For a journal: **Author. (Year). Article title. *Journal, volume*(issue), pages.** For a web document: **Author. (Year). *Title*. Retrieved [date], from [URL].**

**Explain more**

- **Web document with no date:** write **(n.d.)** for the year and still give **both the retrieval date and the URL**: WA Deaf Society. (n.d.). *You have rights!* Retrieved October 17, 2008, from http://www.wadeaf.org.au/index.php?id=45. In text: (WA Deaf Society, n.d.).
- **Book without an author:** the **title** (its first few words, in italics, with capitals) takes the author's place in text: (*Nursing Procedures*, 2002, p. 17). Reference list: *Nursing procedures made incredibly easy*. (2002). Springhouse: Springhouse. Exam answer: a book without an author is cited in text using **the title**.
- **Journal article with no author:** the first few words of the article title in **double quotation marks**, with the comma inside the closing mark: ("Mobile Phones," 2007).
- **Editor:** Macpherson, G. (Ed.). (1995). *Black's medical dictionary* (38th ed.). London: A & C Black.
- **Secondary source:** Palfrey (2000, as cited in Talbot & Verrinder, 2005, p. 79). Only the book you actually **read** (Talbot & Verrinder) goes in the reference list.
- **Several authors at once:** (Hamilton, 2005; Stevens, 2003), in **alphabetical order**, separated by a **semicolon**.
- **Database article or eBook:** end the entry with "Retrieved from" and the database or eBook library name.
- **Newspaper:** O'Leary, C. (2005, June 11). Painkillers raise heart attack risk. *The West Australian*, p. 1.
- Trap: in the reference list the **volume** is italic and the **issue** is not: *12*(7).

### Lesson 15.6: Harvard and MLA styles

**Must know**

**Harvard**

- Primarily used by **university students**. An **author-date** style with two types of citation: **in-text citations** (when quoting or paraphrasing; a fragment of the full citation in the body) and the **reference list**.
- In-text format: **(Author's Last Name Year, Page Number)** with **no comma** between name and year: **(Austen 1813, p. 64)**. Initials have **no full stops** and the year has **no brackets** in the reference list. Article titles go in **single quotation marks**; use **vol.**, **no.**, **pp.**; publisher comes **before** place.

| Source | In-text citation | Reference list entry |
|---|---|---|
| Book | (Holt 1997) *or* Holt (1997) wrote that… | Holt, DH 1997, *Management principles and practices*, Prentice-Hall, Sydney. |
| Journal article | (Conley & Galeson 1998) | Conley, TG & Galeson, DW 1998, 'Nativity and wealth in mid-nineteenth century cities', *Journal of Economic History*, vol. 58, no. 2, pp. 468-493. |
| Web page | (Weida & Stolley 2013) | Weida, S & Stolley, K 2013, *Developing strong thesis statements*. Available from: https://owl.english.purdue.edu/owl/resource/588/1/. [13 October 2015]. |

- Order: **Author, Initials Year, *Title*, Publisher, Place.** Electronic: add **Available from: [source]. [date accessed]**.

**MLA**

- A widely used system that helps an author acknowledge others' ideas, lets a reader **quickly locate** the source, and shows the **scope and depth** of the research.
- **In-text:** author's **last name and page number**, **no year** and **no comma**: **(McDonagh 80)**. MLA is the style that **excludes the year** from the in-text citation.
- **Reference list order:** **Author's last name, first name. Title. Place of publication: publisher, year.** Full first names, not initials; the year comes **last**.

| Source | In-text citation | Reference list entry |
|---|---|---|
| Book | In another study (McDonagh 80)… *or* McDonagh has discussed (80)… | McDonagh, Sean. *Why Are We Deaf to the Cry of the Earth*. Dublin: Veritas, 2001. |
| Journal article | Mann (850) argues… | Mann, Susan. "Myths of Asian Womanhood." *Journal of Asian Studies* 59.1 (2000): 835-62. |
| Web page | Harris argues… *or* (Harris) | Harris, Robert. "Evaluating Internet Research Sources." *VirtualSalt*. 15 June 2008. Web. 27 April 2011. |

**Explain more**

- **Harvard variants:** 2 or 3 authors: (McCarthy, William & Pascale 1997). More than 3: (Bond et al. 1996) in text, but **all names** in the reference list. No author: (*A history of Greece* 1994). Editor: Jones, MD (ed) 1998; two editors: (eds). Chapter in an edited book: Milkman, R 1998, 'The new American workplace: High road or low road?' in P Thompson & C Warhurst (eds), *Workplaces of the future*, pp. 22-34, Macmillan Press, London. Edition: 10th edn. Newspaper: Devlin, M 2011, 'Keys found to success by the less privileged', *The Australian*, 16 November, p. 32.
- **MLA variants:** two or three authors: McLean, Bethany and Peter Elkind. *The Smartest Guys in the Room*. New York: Portfolio, 2004; in text, McLean and Elkind (122). More than three: McGeady, Thomas et al.; in text, McGeady et al. (155). Editor: Booth, David. Ed. *Rethinking Social Development*. Essex: Longman, 1994. E-journal: add the database, "Web." and the access date. Print newspaper: Gleeson, Jim. "Towards Holistic Approach to School Discipline." *Irish Times* 10 February 2006, p. 16.
- **Quick comparison of the three in-text forms** for the same quotation on page 77 of a 2002 book by John: APA **(John, 2002, p. 77)**; Harvard **(John 2002, p. 77)**; MLA **(John 77)**.
- Trap: APA and Harvard are both **author-date** styles; only MLA leaves the year out of the in-text citation. In APA the year is in brackets in the reference list, (2002); in Harvard it is bare, 1997; in MLA it comes at the very end.

### One-minute recap

- Copyright law protects creative works; violation means prosecution. Fair use = academic, non-commercial use within a limit; unfair use = indiscriminate copying.
- Plagiarism (Latin *plagiarius*, "kidnapper") = using copyrighted work without permission or acknowledgement; "academic theft". Self-plagiarism = reusing your own old assignment unacknowledged; cyber plagiarism = via the Internet.
- Avoid it: cite properly in the prescribed style, list every citation in the reference list; learn time management, correct paraphrasing and the skill of citation.
- 5 consequences: reputation, suspension/rustication, dismissal of staff, institutional reputation, prosecution. 6 reasons to cite.
- Referencing = in-text citation (body) + reference list (end); every in-text entry appears in the list and vice versa, except personal communications.
- Record 5 elements: author, title, publication information, dates, access (URL/DOI).
- APA: social sciences; MLA: composition; Chicago: humanities; Harvard: author-date, university students.
- APA: (Surname, Year, p. x); quotes under 40 words in double quotation marks, over 40 as an indented block; list alphabetical, title replaces a missing author, italics for titles and volume but not issue; more than 6 authors: six then et al.; no-date web: (n.d.) plus retrieval date and URL.
- Harvard: (Holt 1997, p. 64), no comma; MLA: (McDonagh 80), no year.

### Theory practice

**Question:** You have consulted a book written by O. P. Maxwell and I. Daniel, published in 2017 by Oxford in England, titled *Introduction to Geodesy*. Show how you would enter it in the reference list in (a) APA and (b) Harvard style, and explain what plagiarism is and two ways to avoid it.

*Answer outline:* (a) APA: Maxwell, O. P., & Daniel, I. (2017). *Introduction to geodesy*. England: Oxford. (b) Harvard: Maxwell, OP & Daniel, I 2017, *Introduction to geodesy*, Oxford, England. Then define plagiarism (using copyrighted work without permission or acknowledging the source; "academic theft") and give the two rules: cite properly in the prescribed style, and list every citation in the reference list or bibliography.
