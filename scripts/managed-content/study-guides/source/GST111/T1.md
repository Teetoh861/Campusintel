## Topic 1: Listening Skills

**What you must know:** what listening is and why it matters in lectures; the three conditions that cause ineffective listening; the seven strategies for better listening; formal versus informal listening; how listening links to note-taking (requirements, strategies, before/during/after a lecture, symbols and abbreviations).

### Lesson 1.1: Listening and the lecture method

**Must know**

- **Listening:** the ability of a person to **receive, perceive and decode** the meaning of spoken words. In class, it is the ability to receive, perceive and decode the meaning of what the lecturer is disseminating as knowledge. In short: the ability to identify and understand what a speaker is saying.
- Listening is a **receptive skill** in language and communication. You must acquire it **consciously** to reach **communicative competence**, which leads to excellence in your studies.
- **Why it is important:** without listening, communication is impossible.
- Knowledge in a university is communicated through **lectures**. The main goal of a lecture is to **impart knowledge** to students. Your duty is to capture the **essence** of what the lecturer says in your notes, which is impossible without effective listening.
- **A lecture has three parts:** introduction, main lecture (body) and conclusion.
- **Course outline:** a listing of the relevant **topics to be treated in a particular course**, provided by the **lecturer**.
- This course (GST 111, formerly GST 105 Use of English) covers listening, speaking (the sounds of English, i.e. phonology), reading, writing, grammar, vocabulary and use of the library. Epistemology is not part of it.

**Explain more**

- The four language skills divide into two groups. **Receptive:** listening and reading (you take language in). **Productive:** speaking and writing (you produce language). When a question in this topic asks for the receptive skill, the answer is **listening**.
- Trap: listening is **not** predicting what the speaker will say, guiding the speaker's speech, or simply keeping quiet. Keeping quiet is good manners; listening is understanding.
- Trap: listening is the ability to receive, perceive and decode meaning. "Denounce" (to condemn publicly) is not part of the definition.
- The skills involved in listening include understanding the speaker's **accent, vocabulary and presentation** of the topic. Reading the topic beforehand and preparing for the lecture are pre-lecture activities, not listening skills themselves.
- A lecture is mostly one-way delivery, not an equal trade of information. Giving a course outline or introducing the lecturer is not its goal.
- The course outline is prepared before teaching begins, so it lists topics, not the specific or main points raised in a lecture. Those belong in your notes.

### Lesson 1.2: Factors responsible for ineffective and unproductive listening

**Must know**

Three conditions make listening ineffective:

1. **Physical condition of the environment** where you receive the lecture. A **noisy** or **ill-ventilated** room inhibits listening.
2. **Physiological condition of the lecturer:** his **speech defects, pronunciation problems and intonation**. These are parts of communication skills, but when the speaker is deficient in them they become **barriers to listening**.
3. **Psychological condition of the listener:** mostly the **emotional state** of the listener. It has a great impact on the result of listening.

Situations that make listening unproductive: noise, speech defects, pronunciation problems, intonation, the mind of the listener.

**Explain more**

- Match the condition to the person or thing: **physical = environment**, **physiological = lecturer (speaker)**, **psychological = listener**. A speech defect is a **physiological** condition. Noise is **physical**. Grief or excitement in the listener is **psychological**.
- Trap: a "spiritual" condition is not one of the three.
- Other barriers: an unfamiliar accent or speech affectation, and difficult vocabulary. **Organised and cohesive speech** helps the listener, so it does not harm listening.

### Lesson 1.3: Strategies for enhancing listening skills

**Must know**

Seven strategies, in this order:

1. **Eye contact:** maintaining eye contact with your lecturer is the **beginning** of any attempt at effective listening. It links your mind with the speaker's and establishes your physical readiness to process the message.
2. **Identify and concentrate on the content of the lecture:** first identify what the lecture is about, then focus your mind on the content.
3. **Repress all forms of emotional attachment:** suppress your emotion so that your interest is a product of genuine interest to know.
4. **Avoid distractions:** noises inside and outside the lecture room.
5. **Listening as a mental challenge:** see listening as a challenge you can overcome; prepare psychologically by stimulating your interest in the lecture.
6. **Ask mental questions** as the lecture progresses.
7. **Avoid hasty generalisations and conclusions:** be patient enough to let the speaker finish the point before you react.

Effective listening requires that you pay **total attention** to what the speaker is saying.

**Explain more**

- **Eye contact** also lets you focus on the lecturer's actions in laboratory demonstrations or step-by-step explanations. Exam wording: maintain **eye** contact (not "close", "full" or "virtual").
- **Emotional attachment** to the subject leads to **prejudices**, which may come from religious, cultural, economic, tribal or financial bias against the topic or the lecturer. Personal emotions such as grief, happiness or love also hinder listening.
- **Distractions** include music, friends and classmates talking, spectacles created by people's manner of dressing, and humorous remarks by the lecturer. Admiring the lecturer's outfit is a distraction, not listening.
- **Mental questions** to ask: What is the main thrust of the lecture? How does it relate to my past knowledge of previous topics? What other course in my field has said something about this topic?
- **Hasty generalisation:** drawing your conclusion on the content before the lecture ends. However attractive, agitating or irritating the delivery, there may still be something in it you will benefit from. Listen to the end, or until the lecturer pauses for your reaction.
- Trap: effective listening does **not** mean dictating the speaker's pace, or judging his attitude and comportment. Critical thinking is useful, but it comes after you have fully taken in what was said.

### Lesson 1.4: Formal and informal listening

**Must know**

There are **two types** of listening: **formal** and **informal**.

| | Formal listening | Informal listening |
|---|---|---|
| Where | Specific formal environments, e.g. a university lecture, and all formal contexts | Normal, regular conversations with anyone we talk to or who talks to us |
| Purpose | Specific formal purposes, e.g. to pass exams | Not to pass exams; to improve our relationships with people |
| Strategies | Deploy the seven strategies above for maximum results | Still essential to improve our capacity to listen to others |

In the classroom we engage in formal listening for any of **six purposes**:

1. To **comprehend** or gain a general understanding of the lecture.
2. To **remember** a specific equation, formula or concept so you can define it when the time arises.
3. To **evaluate or criticise** a theory, a point of logic, the weakness or strength of an argument.
4. To **analyse** a step or method of arriving at an answer or the result of an experiment.
5. To **learn** so as to outline, account for and discuss the historical development of an event, theory or concept.
6. To learn the **critical tools** used in practical analysis of literary texts and how to deploy them to evaluate the texts' categories and functions.

**Explain more**

- These purposes are **not mutually exclusive**: one lecture may need you to comprehend, criticise and evaluate a concept at the same time.
- Lecturers try to focus on a specific topic, but not in every lecture. What you need most is **awareness of the situation**, so you can relate it to former knowledge.
- Exam wording: during lectures we engage in **formal** listening; regular conversations attract **informal** listening. "Total" and "partial" are not types of listening.
- Informal listening still matters: listening skills shape your relationships in the larger society, not only your academic performance.

### Lesson 1.5: Listening and note-taking

**Must know**

- **Why take notes:** you cannot remember everything you listen to, however ingenious you are. Even verbatim recall is **tentative**; after a period you forget part or even the entire main gist. Hence the necessity of taking notes while listening.
- **Note-taking:** the aspect of communication skills that involves your ability to **keep a record of a speaker's message** and to **record the essential points from textbooks or written materials**. Through it you engage actively in learning and produce your own written record of main ideas and important details for revision and examinations.

**Six requirements of effective note-taking.** You must be able to:

1. **Recognise the main ideas** in a speaker's message, lecture or reading material.
2. **Identify only relevant information** from the lecture or reading.
3. **Devise a personal system** of recording information that works for you (e.g. a format with columns: Heading, Source, Reference, Content, Details).
4. **Reduce the content** of the lecture to note form, possibly with a diagram format.
5. Possess the skills of **sentence reduction, summary, paraphrasing**, etc.
6. **Correctly cite and reference** sources when making notes from a textbook.

**Five strategies for effective note-taking:**

1. **Define the purpose** for listening or reading; have a firm grasp of the message or topic before you begin. For written material, skim and highlight the relevant parts.
2. **Identify the function and purpose** of the text or lecture: make out the potentially important information; overview the outline as you listen or read. For a book, read the title, preface, foreword, abstract, table of contents and introduction, and skim the material.
3. Know the **organising principles** by which the lecturer or textbook presents the information.
4. **Include your own thoughts** in the note, in a separate column or on the margin.
5. **Do not write verbatim.** Use **shorthand**: symbols, abbreviations, concept maps or diagrams.

**Before, during and after a live lecture:**

- **Before:** revise previous related lectures; read about the topic (a **pre-lecture survey**); check the meaning of new words that are vital terms in the course; prepare the format for note-taking; arrive in time to sit close to the lecturer.
- **During:** distinguish the lecture's **main points** from elaborations, examples, repetitions and re-statements; listen for **transition cues** (in addition, however, moreover, consequently) that mark movement from introduction to body to conclusion or summary; look out for **non-verbal cues** (facial expressions, gesticulations, body movement); observe **illustrations and demonstrations** with visual materials (diagrams, tables, drawings and their labels); listen for **phonological cues** (voice changes, accent, word stress, speed, emotional nuances).
- **After:** **revise or review** the notes as soon as possible and compare notes with course-mates; **tidy up** handwriting and fill in missing bits; try out a **summary** of the lecture in your own words.

**Explain more**

- **Organising principles** a lecture or text may follow: steps or stages of a process or event; general to specific ideas; simple to complex ideas; problems and solutions; causes and results; largest parts to smallest parts; well-known ideas to least known; most important point to least important; past ideas to present ones.
- Note-taking from a text differs from note-taking in a live lecture because you cannot stop the lecturer to ask for a review (unless it is a recording). Hence the before, during and after activities.
- **Good notes are:** brief and concise, legible, systematic and orderly. They are **not** all that is said in class.
- **Good habits:** record the main points with a **selection** of illustrations and examples; summarise main points; think logically about ideas; disregard digressions; use personal abbreviations **consistently**.
- **Bad habits:** verbatim recording; recording all the lecturer says; recording main points with **all** the illustrations; using **inconsistent** personal abbreviations.
- **Pre-lecture preparation:** surveying the course outline and topic beforehand, reading introductory surveys of an unfamiliar topic, and arriving before the lecturer are all good. Speculating about the lecturer's sources is **not** preparation. A **pre-lecture survey** means reading up on a topic before the lecture.
- Post-lecture activities are review, revision and summary. "Disposal" (throwing notes away) is not one.
- Note-taking skills are sentence reduction, summary and paraphrasing. **Sound articulation** is a speaking skill, not a note-taking skill.
- Abbreviation trap: **NEC** means "necessary", not National Executive Council or National Electoral Commission.

### One-minute recap

- Listening = ability to receive, perceive and decode the meaning of spoken words; a receptive skill; without it communication is impossible.
- A lecture imparts knowledge: introduction, main lecture, conclusion. A course outline lists a course's topics; the lecturer provides it.
- Three conditions hinder listening: physical (environment), physiological (lecturer), psychological (listener).
- Seven strategies: eye contact; concentrate on content; repress emotional attachment; avoid distractions; mental challenge; mental questions; no hasty generalisations.
- Formal listening (lectures; six purposes) versus informal (normal conversations).
- Six requirements and five strategies of note-taking; never verbatim, use shorthand consistently.
- Before: revise, read up, check new words, prepare format, arrive early. During: main points, transition, non-verbal, visual and phonological cues. After: review, tidy up, summarise.
- Good notes: brief, legible, systematic, orderly. NEC = necessary, c.f. = compare, NB = note well.
