## Topic 2: Speaking Skills I, the Sounds of English

**What you must know:** the organs of speech and what each does; the 44 phonemes of English (24 consonants, 20 vowels); how consonants are described by place, manner and voicing; how vowels are described by tongue height, tongue position, lip shape and length; the 12 pure vowels and 8 diphthongs; IPA symbols and how to transcribe short words.

### Lesson 2.1: The organs of speech

**Must know**

- **Organs of speech:** all the parts of the body involved in producing speech sounds, from the **lungs** to the **lips and nose**. About half the body, from the head to the abdomen, is needed for speech. The organs modify the airstream from the lungs to produce different sounds.
- **Lungs:** sponge-like tissues housed within the **rib cage**. They provide the airstream for speech. Air pushed out from the lungs is the **pulmonic egressive airstream**, the airstream used for English sounds.
- **Trachea:** the windpipe; air passes up through it from the lungs.
- **Larynx:** the voice box; it contains the **vocal folds**, two elastic tissues.
- **Glottis:** the aperture (opening) between the vocal folds. It decides voicing:
  1. Glottis held **open**, air passes without obstruction = **voiceless** sound.
  2. Glottis relatively **closed** so the vocal folds **vibrate** under air pressure = **voiced** sound. Voicing is caused by the **vibration** of the vocal folds.
- **Vocal tract:** the air passages above the larynx. Two parts: the **oral cavity** (mouth and pharynx) and the **nasal cavity** (nose).
- **Articulators:** parts of the vocal tract used to form sounds (tongue, lips and so on). **Articulation** is the contact of two or more speech organs.
- **Active articulators** move (lips, tongue, soft palate). **Passive articulators** are fixed (teeth, hard palate). The **tongue** is the most flexible of all the organs of speech.

Other articulators and their functions:

1. **Soft palate (velum):** a muscular flap at the back of the roof of the mouth. Raised, it presses against the back wall of the pharynx and shuts off the nasal tract so no air leaves through the nose. Lowered, air escapes through the nose (nasal sounds).
2. **Hard palate:** a bony structure at the front part of the roof of the mouth.
3. **Alveolar ridge:** the rough surface just behind the upper teeth; you can feel it with the tip of the tongue.
4. **Upper and lower teeth.**
5. **Lips:** brought together, rounded or spread; for some consonants the lower lip touches the upper teeth.

**Explain more**

- Parts of the tongue used in articulation: **tip (apex), blade, front, centre (middle), back, root and body**.
- Locations: lungs in the chest (rib cage); larynx and vocal folds in the neck; pharynx above the larynx; velum, hard palate, alveolar ridge and teeth in the mouth; nasal cavity in the nose. The **uvula** hangs at the end of the soft palate.
- Trap: the lungs are housed in the **rib cage**, not the larynx. The larynx houses the vocal folds.
- Trap: "separation" of the vocal folds gives voicelessness; **vibration** gives voicing.
- Trap: pulmonic (lungs) is the English airstream; glottalic and velaric airstreams belong to some other languages, and "laryngeal" names a place, not an airstream.

### Lesson 2.2: Consonants

**Must know**

- **Phoneme:** a sound a language selects as significant, one that can keep meanings apart (distinguish words). The speech organs can make unlimited sounds; each language uses only a few.
- English has **44 phonemes: 20 vowels and 24 consonants**. (26 is the number of letters, not sounds.)
- **Consonant:** a speech sound produced when at least **two articulators come in contact**, obstructing the flow of air from the larynx to the lips or nostrils.
- Consonants are described by **three parameters**:
  1. **Place of articulation:** where the contact takes place.
  2. **Manner of articulation:** the nature of the obstruction.
  3. **State of the glottis (voicing):** whether or not the vocal folds vibrate.

**Places of articulation** (9):

| Place | Organs involved | Sounds |
|---|---|---|
| Bilabial | the two lips | /p/ /b/ /m/ /w/ |
| Labio-dental | lower lip and upper front teeth | /f/ /v/ |
| Dental | tongue tip or blade and upper front teeth | /θ/ /ð/ |
| Alveolar | tongue blade or tip and alveolar ridge | /t/ /d/ /s/ /z/ /n/ /l/ |
| Post-alveolar | tongue raised towards the ridge but no contact | /r/ |
| Palato-alveolar | blade on alveolar ridge plus front of tongue raised to the palate | /ʃ/ /ʒ/ /ʧ/ /ʤ/ |
| Palatal | front of tongue and hard palate | /j/ |
| Velar | back of tongue and soft palate | /k/ /g/ /ŋ/ |
| Glottal | vocal folds, friction but no vibration | /h/ |

**Manners of articulation** (7 groups):

1. **Stops (plosives)** /p t k b d g/: total closure of the airflow in the mouth, then sudden release.
2. **Fricatives** /f v θ ð s z ʃ ʒ h/: two articulators come close; air escapes through a small passage with friction or a hissing sound.
3. **Affricates** /ʧ ʤ/: begin as plosives, end as fricatives (total closure, then gradual release). They combine the manner of stops and fricatives, which their two-part symbols show.
4. **Nasals** /m n ŋ/: complete closure in the mouth, the soft palate is lowered, so air escapes through the nose.
5. **Approximants** /r w j/: a narrowing, but the articulators are not close enough to produce audible friction. /w/ and /j/ are a sub-group called **glides**.
6. **Lateral** /l/: the centre of the tongue touches the alveolar ridge and air passes along the sides of the mouth.
7. **Liquids:** /l/ and /r/ together form the group called liquids.

**Voicing:**

- **Voiceless (9):** /p t k s f ʃ θ h ʧ/.
- **Voiced (15):** /b d g z v ʒ ð ʤ m n ŋ l r w j/.

**The 24 English consonants**

| Symbol | Voicing | Place | Manner | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|---|

| /z/ | voiced | alveolar | fricative | zoo, rise, rays, maize |

| /ʒ/ | voiced | palato-alveolar | fricative | genre, seizure, garage, casual |

**Explain more**

- The slants / / around a symbol mark it as a phoneme, not a letter of the alphabet.
- The voiceless th symbol is sometimes printed as /ɵ/; the correct IPA symbol is /θ/ (thing, youth, mouth). In typed questions it may come out as an odd character; any voiceless th option still means /θ/. The voiced one is /ð/ (this, mother).
- Sounds that share a **place** but differ in manner: /s/, /t/ and /n/ are all **alveolar** (/s/ fricative, /t/ stop, /n/ nasal). Sounds that share a **manner** but differ in place: /p/, /t/ and /k/ are all voiceless stops.
- Trap: /f/ is **not** bilabial; it is labio-dental. Bilabials are /p b m w/.
- Trap: the **lateral** is /l/ only. /r/ is the post-alveolar approximant (it is sometimes classed as a trill); /w/ and /j/ are glides.
- Trap: the only **glottal** sound is /h/. /k/ and /g/ are velar.
- Feel voicing: fingertips on your larynx while saying a long "zzzz" (vibration) then "ssss" (none). Or block your ears and contrast [s z f v ʃ ʒ]; you hear buzzing in /z v ʒ/.
- Nasals and stops both start with complete closure; in nasals the velum drops and air leaves through the nose.

### Lesson 2.3: Vowels: the 12 pure vowels

**Must know**

- **Vowel:** a sound produced **without any obstruction** to the airflow from the larynx to the lips. The articulators do not come close; there is **no contact**. The vocal folds vibrate, so **all vowels are voiced**.
- **20 vowels: 12 pure vowels (monophthongs) and 8 diphthongs.**
- Because the articulators never meet, vowels are **not** described by place or manner. Four parameters are used instead:
  1. **Tongue height** (vertical distance from the tongue to the palate): **high (close)** vowels /i: ɪ u: ʊ/; **low (open)** vowels /æ ɒ ʌ ɑ:/; **mid** vowels /ɛ ɜ: ə ɔ:/ in between.
  2. **Tongue horizontality** (which part of the tongue is raised highest): **front** /i: ɪ ɛ æ/; **back** /u: ʊ ɒ ɔ: ɑ:/; **central** /ɜ: ə ʌ/.
  3. **Lip rounding** (the easiest to observe): **spread** /i: ɪ ɛ æ/; **neutral** /ʌ ɜ: ɑ: ə/; **rounded** /ɒ ʊ ɔ: u:/.
  4. **Duration:** long or short. Because context can lengthen or shorten a vowel, the terms **tense** (= long: /i: ɜ: ɔ: u: ɑ:/) and **lax** (= short: /ɪ ɛ æ ʌ ə ɒ ʊ/) are preferred, stressing quality over quantity.

**The 12 pure vowels**

| Symbol | Description | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| /i:/ | high, front, tense | seat, seed, receive, be, machine, key |
| /ɪ/ | mid-high, front, lax | rich, basket, symbol, village, ladies |
| /ɛ/ | mid, front, lax | bed, breath, many |
| /æ/ | low, front, lax | man, back, hat, badge, land |
| /ɑ:/ | low, back, tense | card, heart, clerk, pass, half, laugh |
| /ɒ/ | low, back, lax | cot, was, cough, knowledge, because |
| /ɔ:/ | mid-low, back, tense | war, four, door, board, cause, water, jaw, ought, broad |
| /ʊ/ | mid-high, back, lax | full, book, woman, could |
| /u:/ | high, back, tense | rule, food, who, soup, chew, blue, juice |
| /ʌ/ | mid-low, central, lax | cup, son, young, blood, does |
| /ɜ:/ | mid-high, central, tense | serve, nurse, bird, myrrh, work, search, journey |
| /ə/ | mid, central, lax | woman, oblige, figure, mother, possible, suppose, colour |

**The vowel chart** (tongue height down the side, tongue position across):

| | Front | Central | Back |
|---|---|---|---|
| High (close) | i: ɪ | | u: ʊ |
| Mid | ɛ | ɜ: ə | ɔ: |
| Low (open) | æ | ʌ | ɒ ɑ: |

**Explain more**

- Trap: "high" vowels are also called **close** vowels (tongue close to the roof of the mouth); "low" vowels are **open**. "High" does not mean long or rounded.
- Real vowel distinctions: high/low, close/open, front/back, long/short (tense/lax), spread/neutral/rounded. "Rounded/around" is not a distinction, and "pursed" is not a lip position.
- Trap: what is **not** involved in vowel articulation is **contact** between articulators. Tongue movement, lip shaping and length all are.
- /u:/ is the high, back, tense vowel; /i:/ is high and tense but **front**; /ɜ:/ is central; /ɑ:/ is low and back. In typed questions /ɜ:/ sometimes appears as /3:/ and /ɑ:/ as /a:/.
- Schwa /ə/ is the mid central lax vowel of unstressed syllables (the "a" in woman, the "er" in mother).
- The vowel of bed is written /ɛ/ here; some dictionaries write it /e/. Same sound.
- Length pairs differ in quality too: /i:/ high front vs /ɪ/ mid-high front; /u:/ high back vs /ʊ/ mid-high back; /æ/ low front vs /ɑ:/ low back; /ɒ/ low back vs /ɔ:/ mid-low back.

### Lesson 2.4: Diphthongs and the spelling problem

**Must know**

- **Diphthong:** a vowel sound that consists of a **movement or glide from one vowel position to another**. A pure vowel is static in phonetic space. Diphthongs contain two elements but are **equal in length to the long pure vowels**. Their symbols show both elements.
- The 8 diphthongs are grouped by the **direction of the glide**:
  1. **Closing (rising) diphthongs (5):** first element a mid or low vowel, second element a **high** vowel.
  2. **Centring diphthongs (3):** first element a front or back vowel, ending in the central vowel **schwa /ə/**.

| Group | Symbol | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Closing | /eɪ/ | gate, waist, play, eight, they, great |
| Closing | /aɪ/ | time, pie, fly, type, light, dye, eye |
| Closing | /ɔɪ/ | boil, boy, joint, joy, oyster, void, voyage |
| Closing | /əʊ/ | so, hoe, own, toast, soul, go, pillow |
| Closing | /aʊ/ | house, how, loud, crowd, shout, owl |
| Centring | /ɪə/ | here, cheer, media, idea, museum |
| Centring | /ɛə/ | care, chair, bear, parent, aerial |
| Centring | /ʊə/ | poor, gourd, endure, during |

- **No sound-letter correspondence:** the same letters can be pronounced in many ways. The letter "a" is /eɪ/ in gate, /ə/ in woman, /ɛ/ in many, /ɪ/ in village, /æ/ in man, /ɑ:/ in pass and /ɒ/ in was. Never use spelling as a guide to pronunciation.

**Explain more**

- Closing diphthongs end in /ɪ/ (eɪ, aɪ, ɔɪ) or /ʊ/ (əʊ, aʊ). Centring diphthongs all end in /ə/.
- The vowel classes you may meet are **monophthongs** (12), **diphthongs** (8) and **triphthongs** (a glide through three positions, e.g. fire /faɪə/, formed by a closing diphthong plus schwa). There is no such thing as a **quadriphthong**.
- Trap: diphthongs are single vowel phonemes, not two vowels. "Care" /kɛə/ has two sounds in total, not three.
- Trap: "pure vowel" = monophthong = one steady position; "diphthong" = glide. Tense does not mean diphthong: /i:/ is a long pure vowel.

### Lesson 2.5: Symbols and transcription

**Must know**

- The symbols for English sounds are adapted from the **International Phonetic Association's alphabet (IPA)**, not from the 26 letters. They represent the **phonemes**, the sounds that differentiate words.
- **Transcription:** capturing an utterance or word as a linear sequence of sound symbols. It mirrors pronunciation. **Orthography** is the conventional spelling system. Phonetics is to pronunciation what orthography is to spelling.
- **Minimal pairs** show phonemes at work: /kʌt/ cut, /kɒt/ cot, /kɑ:t/ cart, /kæt/ cat, /kɔ:t/ caught, /kɪt/ kit. Change one vowel phoneme and the word changes.
- The **length mark (diacritic) ':'** after a vowel symbol shows the vowel is **long**: /i:/ seat vs /ɪ/ sit; /u:/ fool vs /ʊ/ full; /ɔ:/ caught vs /ɒ/ cot; /ɑ:/ cart vs /æ/ cat. It does not change the perceptual quality of the long vowel; it marks length only.
- Number of letters is not number of sounds:

| Word | Transcription | Sounds | Letters |
|---|---|---|---|
| source | /sɔ:s/ | 3 | 6 |
| fall | /fɔ:l/ | 3 | 4 |
| shirt | /ʃɜ:t/ | 3 | 5 |
| tough | /tʌf/ | 3 | 5 |
| care | /kɛə/ | 2 | 4 |

**Explain more**

- How to transcribe a monosyllabic word: say it aloud, ignore the spelling, write one symbol per sound. Worked examples: youth /ju:θ/ (glide /j/ + long /u:/ + voiceless /θ/; never written with the letter "y", never with /ð/); mouth /maʊθ/; state /steɪt/; love /lʌv/; teach /ti:ʧ/; care /kɛə/; proud /praʊd/; street /stri:t/.
- Counting sounds: cheap /ʧi:p/ has **3** sounds (ch is one affricate). Trust /trʌst/ has 5; frog /frɒg/ has 4; thoughts /θɔ:ts/ has 4 (th is one sound, "ough" is one vowel).
- Trap: silent letters (knowledge, half, queue) and double letters (hammer, rubber) get one symbol or none. /ŋ/ in "song" is one sound spelt with two letters.
- Pronounce correctly to transcribe correctly, and use the transcription your dictionary gives for every entry.
- Trap: APA and MLA are referencing styles; the symbol source is the **IPA**.

### One-minute recap

- Organs of speech run from the lungs (in the rib cage) to the lips and nose; the pulmonic egressive airstream powers English speech.
- Glottis open = voiceless; vocal folds vibrating = voiced. The tongue is the most flexible articulator.
- Active articulators move (lips, tongue, velum); passive ones are fixed (teeth, hard palate).
- 44 phonemes: 24 consonants, 20 vowels (12 pure vowels + 8 diphthongs).
- Consonants: place (9), manner (stops, fricatives, affricates, nasals, approximants/glides, lateral, liquids), voicing (9 voiceless, 15 voiced).
- Bilabial /p b m w/; labio-dental /f v/; dental /θ ð/; alveolar /t d s z n l/; glottal /h/; lateral /l/.
- Vowels: no contact, all voiced; described by tongue height, tongue horizontality, lip rounding, length (tense/lax). High = close, low = open.
- Diphthongs glide: 5 closing (eɪ aɪ ɔɪ əʊ aʊ), 3 centring (ɪə ɛə ʊə).
- Symbols come from the IPA; ':' marks length; spelling is not a guide to sound (source = 3 sounds, 6 letters).

### Theory practice

**Question:** What is the basic difference between consonant and vowel articulation, and how is each type of sound described?

*Answer outline:* Consonants: at least two articulators make contact and obstruct the airflow; described by place of articulation, manner of articulation and state of the glottis (voicing), e.g. /p/ voiceless bilabial stop. Vowels: no obstruction, no contact, all voiced; described by tongue height, tongue horizontality, lip rounding and duration (tense/lax), e.g. /i:/ high front tense. Mention the counts: 24 consonants, 20 vowels.
