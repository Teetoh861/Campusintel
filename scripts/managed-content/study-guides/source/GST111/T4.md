## Topic 4: Reading Skills I: Strategies for Effective Reading

**What you must know:** what reading is and the 4 misconceptions; the 3 types of reading (skimming, scanning, study reading) and LEARNER; the 8 bad reading habits; the 3 strategies for efficient reading (preview, read with intent, review) and the steps under each.

### Lesson 4.1: What reading is; reading efficiency and misconceptions

**Must know**

- **Reading:** the process of translating written or printed symbols and letters into words and sentences which convey meaning to the individual. The main purpose of reading is to **comprehend** a written material.
- Reading involves **both the eyes and the brain**. The **eyes** do the physical task of linking the letters and symbols to know what the author **says**; the **brain** interprets what the author **means**. Essential to reading: written or printed signs, the eyes, the brain. **Not** essential: the articulatory (vocal) system.
- Reading is a **developmental process** which can never be completely mastered. Many students get no instruction in reading and so develop unhealthy habits.
- **Reading efficiency:** the acquisition of proper reading skills which facilitate the efficient physical and mental processing of written materials; the relationship between the physical aspects of reading and reading speed, which influences comprehension.
- Target speed: about **600 words per minute** by the end of your programme. Efficiency improves only through **constant reading practice**.

**4 misconceptions about reading** (and the truth):

1. **You need to read everything.** No: read selectively, depending on your purpose, the nature of the material and your familiarity with it.
2. **Everything on a page is equally important.** No: each text mixes important and not-so-important information; sort and evaluate what you need.
3. **Everything can be read the same way.** No: purpose determines what you read, how fast or carefully, what you attend to and what you skip. Vary your technique to suit purpose and material.
4. **Everything we read is "true".** No: read with a critical, questioning eye and some scepticism. Consider the **authority of the author** and the **author's purpose**; distinguish **facts** from **opinions, value judgements and generalisations**.

**Explain more**

- **What determines how (and how fast) you read:** the **nature** of the material, your **purpose**, and your **familiarity** with the topic. A newspaper is read once; a study text is studied repeatedly. The **author** and the **publisher** do not prescribe how you read. Trap: "the author's prescription" is the odd one out among factors deciding reading speed (the reader, who varies speed to suit the material, does count as a factor). Trap: asked what determines **how a material is read**, choose **its nature** (newspaper once, study text repeatedly), not the reader, author or publisher.
- A passage in **technical or difficult language** is best read at a **slow pace**.
- **Benefits of reading:** it enlarges your vocabulary and improves your listening, speaking and writing skills. It does **not** diminish your speech ability.
- Critical reading (misconception 4) also helps in examinations that ask you to **apply, connect and evaluate** ideas.

### Lesson 4.2: Types of reading: skimming, scanning, study reading

**Must know**

- **Stages of reading development:** early primary school stresses word recognition and meaning: **"learning to read"**. From mid-primary and junior secondary school the emphasis shifts to silent reading for comprehension: **"reading to learn"**. In secondary school and higher institutions reading is used to learn facts and concepts and acquire new vocabulary and information.
- Different **purposes** give different types of reading. Three types:

1. **Skimming:** looking at the content quickly to get the **overall (general) idea**. You look at the title and author, the blurb at the back (or the abstract of an article), the table of contents, preface and foreword, then the subtitles, tables, charts and the topic sentences of each paragraph, without bothering with details. You are **not** looking for specific information.
2. **Scanning:** running the eyes quickly through the material in search of a **particular piece of specific information**, or to see whether the material is relevant to your purpose. You ignore irrelevant details. You locate the section in the **table of contents or index**, then read the indicated pages. In scanning, the **index** can be very important.
3. **Study reading:** an **intensive** kind of reading: a thorough examination of the material and a critical analysis of the issues raised; reading to gather information that will translate to **knowledge**. Guided by the acronym **LEARNER**.

**LEARNER** (for productive study reading):

1. **L**ay out the purpose for reading.
2. **E**xamine all parts of the material.
3. **A**sk questions about each subheading during the study.
4. **R**ead all materials on the topic to find answers to the set-out questions.
5. **N**ote names, places, dates, events, directions and labels while reading.
6. **E**ase away from studying one subject before starting another.
7. **R**eview notes and materials before a test or a discussion.

**Explain more**

| | Skimming | Scanning |
|---|---|---|
| Goal | General idea of the whole | One specific piece of information |
| Tool | Title, blurb, contents, headings, topic sentences | Index or contents, then the pages |

- Trap: "have a general idea of the topic" = skimming; "get specific information" = scanning. "Looking for specific details" is the one thing skimming does **not** involve.
- Trap: "spinning" and "exploring" are **not** types of reading.
- LEARNER is a **mnemonic**; REARER, REALER and NEARER are distractors.

### Lesson 4.3: Reading deficiencies (bad reading habits)

**Must know**

- Reading **speed** is directly related to **comprehension**. Bad habits lead to slow reading with poor comprehension. Reading should involve no other muscles except those of the eyes.

**8 bad reading habits:**

1. **Word-by-word reading:** emphasises word recognition and sounding out every word; slow and laborious.
2. **Vocalisation:** moving the lips or any other vocal apparatus (tongue, throat, vocal cords) while reading; whispering to yourself, with or without audible sound. Comes from an emphasis on word recognition; reduces rate and comprehension.
3. **Sub-vocalisation:** pronouncing the words in your mind with **no part of the vocal apparatus moving**. Hinders comprehension. Written words represent both a sound system and an idea system; you need not go through the sounds to reach the ideas.
4. **Moving the head from side to side:** stops the eyes focusing fully; divides attention; unnecessary and wasteful.
5. **Finger-pointing:** pointing with a finger or any object to the words. The eyes naturally take in whole phrases and sentences; pointing forces them word by word.
6. **Regression:** glancing **back** to re-read words, phrases and sentences. Usually caused by **lack of concentration**; corrected by concentrating. Some regression is sometimes necessary, but **excessive** regression hinders comprehension.
7. **Short span of recognition:** **span of recognition** = the number of words you can recognise while your eyes are fixed at one point. A poor reader recognises one or two words per glance; a good reader has a **wide** span and takes in several words.
8. **Erratic eye movement:** eyes moving across the line haphazardly instead of rhythmically. Good eyes move from the **middle of one phrase to the middle of the next**, in tune with the syntactic arrangement of the line.

**Explain more**

- Habits 7 and 8 (narrow span and erratic movement) need **continual practice over time** to correct.
- **Eye movement terms:** the eyes do not glide along a line; they move in little jumps and stops. The forward jumps are **saccadic** movements; the backward glances are **regressive** movements. Both are **natural** reading movements; regression is a deficiency only when excessive. Trap: velaric, glottalic and pulmonic are airstream mechanisms in speech, not eye movements.
- Trap: **a wide span of recognition** marks a **good** reader; it is not a bad habit.
- Trap: the backward movement of the eyes is **regression**, not "backwarding".

### Lesson 4.4: Strategy 1: Preview before reading

**Must know**

The strategies for efficient reading fall into **3 major parts**:

1. **Preview before reading:** connect to your experience; decide what you need to know.
2. **Read with intent:** search for key elements; highlight key points; annotate your impressions.
3. **Review after reading:** review and evaluate what has been read.

- **Previewing is the first step** in reading a passage. Never start reading without first **skimming**; skimming belongs to the **preview** stage. Previewing familiarises you with the content and organisation, connects the material to your experience, helps you decide what you need to know, builds interest and concentration, and gives a mental outline before you read.

**7 steps in previewing:**

1. Check the **title, subtitles and author**.
2. Read the **introduction and the first paragraph** (where the author gives the general idea).
3. Read **each heading and the first sentence** after it (headings together form a mini-outline).
4. Read the **first sentence in a few paragraphs** on each page (when there are no headings).
5. Examine all **photographs, charts, tables and diagrams**.
6. Read the **conclusion or summary** (a conclusion signals the end; a summary condenses the whole).
7. Read any **end-of-assignment questions** (they show what is important and what you will be expected to know).

**Connect the material to your own experience (3 suggestions):**

1. Think of **familiar situations** similar to the one in the material; note them in a journal or jotter.
2. **Ask questions and answer them**; this recalls similar events that illuminate the content.
3. **Allow your mind to jump** from one idea to another; write in your jotter or work with a friend.

**Decide what you need to know and remember:** reading only because "I've been told to read it" leaves little behind, just as you cannot describe a twenty-naira note despite handling many. Decide first what you need to know, then turn the preview (title, headings, first paragraph, first sentences, subtitles) into **questions** you answer as you read. This strengthens comprehension and recall.

**Explain more**

- Trap: the first step in reading a passage is to **preview** it, not to summarise, edit or appreciate it. Summarising comes after reading.
- Trap: skimming is classified under **preview before reading**, not under reading with intent or review after reading.

### Lesson 4.5: Strategies 2 and 3: Read with intent; review after reading

**Must know**

**Read with intent.** Comprehension is usually gradual; active reading is actively searching for ideas and separating important from unimportant ones. You may need to read a material more than once. Three activities:

**(a) Search for 5 key elements:**

1. The meaning of the **title and subtitles** (the title announces the topic and often the author's point of view; sometimes its meaning is clear only after reading everything).
2. The **introduction**: the opening paragraph; gives background, announces the focus, captures attention.
3. The **author's main point**: in the **thesis statement**, the one big idea the whole piece explores, usually in the **first or second paragraph**; sometimes elsewhere or merely implied.
4. The **support and explanation**: the body; each paragraph has a **topic sentence** that explains an aspect of the thesis.
5. The **conclusion**: closes the piece; may restate the main point, offer ideas for further thought or suggest future directions.

**Spelling errors are not a key element.**

**(b) Highlight key points** (read with a highlighter or pen in hand), 4 rules:

1. Decide what kinds of information to highlight (the task after reading decides this).
2. **Read first, then highlight** (prevents too much highlighting).
3. **Be selective** (makes important points stand out).
4. Highlight key elements, words and phrases: the **thesis statement, topic sentences** and everything related to the thesis.

**(c) Annotate your impressions.** **Annotation** = jotting down personal ideas about the material; your response to the author's ideas, written in the **margin**: questions, agreement, disagreement, surprise, phrases summarising important points. 5 things to annotate:

1. Important points (e.g. the thesis statement) that prompt personal responses.
2. Sections you need further information on.
3. Sections where the author reveals his or her reasons for writing.
4. Ideas you agree with, disagree with or question.
5. Any **inconsistency** on the author's part.

**Review after reading.** Do not simply close the book. Spend a few minutes reviewing and evaluating, **immediately** after reading, to increase what you remember. The **same steps used to preview are used to review**. Goal: touch on each main point once more. Write a **summary** from the highlighted items and annotations (a start if you must later write an essay). Pay attention to **3 things**:

1. The **heading**.
2. The **highlighted or underlined items** that point to the main ideas.
3. The **annotated items** on the margin.

**References are not part of the review.**

**Explain more**

- **Why reading can be difficult:** the topic is uninteresting, you cannot "connect" with the author, or the style is confusing. You will not experience much difficulty if you **follow the appropriate steps** (preview, read with intent, review).
- **Graphic organiser:** a diagram of the structure of the main points in a material; an active way to connect and review major ideas. It must include all the key elements. Helps when a material is hard.
- With persistence and practice you gain speed, read more actively, and improve comprehension and the rate and depth of recollection. Reading is a conscious activity requiring total concentration.
- Trap: annotation is **personal response** in the margin; highlighting is **marking the text**. Both feed the review summary.

### One-minute recap

- Reading = translating written/printed symbols into meaning; eyes (what the author says) + brain (what the author means); purpose: comprehension.
- Target about 600 words per minute; efficiency grows only through constant practice.
- 4 misconceptions: read everything; everything equally important; everything read the same way; everything in print is true.
- How you read depends on nature of material, purpose and familiarity, never the author or publisher.
- Skimming = general idea; scanning = specific information (index); study reading = intensive, guided by LEARNER.
- 8 bad habits: word-by-word, vocalisation, sub-vocalisation, head movement, finger-pointing, regression, short span of recognition, erratic eye movement. Saccadic and regressive movements are natural.
- 3 strategies: preview (first step; 7 steps; skimming belongs here), read with intent (5 key elements, 4 highlighting rules, 5 things to annotate), review (heading, highlights, annotations).
- Difficulty: uninteresting topic, no connection with the author, confusing style; a graphic organiser helps.
