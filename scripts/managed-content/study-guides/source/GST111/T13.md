## Topic 13: Information Retrieval Tools

**What you must know:** the tools that locate information (library catalogue and OPAC, indexes, abstracts, bibliographies); how to search the OPAC by title, author, subject and keyword; the Library of Congress classes A to Z and the NLM scheme; the internet and its domain types; library databases; the five criteria for evaluating an information resource.

### Lesson 13.1: The library catalogue

**Must know**

- **Information retrieval tools:** the tools or aids used to access information. They act as a guide to library resources. The three main ones are the **library catalogue**, **indexes and abstracts**, and the **bibliography**.
- **Library catalogue:** the **index** and essential tool for retrieving library resources. It gives access to **all** items the library holds, physical and digital: books, e-books, audiovisual materials, reference materials, reserve materials and databases. A library's entire holding is recorded in it.
- **2 formats of the catalogue:**

1. **Manual catalogue** (catalogue cards): arranged by **author, title and subject**. Entries are filed in **alphabetical** order.
2. **Online Public Access Catalogue (OPAC):** the electronic catalogue on the library website. Searched by **title, author, series and keywords**.

- **UNILAG's two public catalogue cabinets:** the **Author/Title** cabinet and the **Subject** cabinet.
- **Catalogues for official use only:** the **Shelf-list** and the **Authority** catalogue.
- **Call number** (call mark): the number generated when a material is catalogued. It tells you where the item sits on the shelf.

**Explain more**

- The internet is not always the best or first place to look; the catalogue shows what your own library has and where it is.
- **Trap:** the manual card catalogue has author cards, title cards and subject cards. An "index card" is **not** a type of catalogue card.
- **Trap:** you can search the catalogue by author, title, subject (and in the OPAC, series and keyword). **Language** is not a search option.
- **Trap:** the catalogue is a **locating tool**; it does not arrange books on the shelves. Shelf arrangement is the job of the **classification scheme** (Lesson 13.3).
- OPAC means Online **Public** Access Catalogue, not "primary" or "private". It is open to all library users.

### Lesson 13.2: Searching the OPAC

**Must know**

- The OPAC has a **Basic Search** and an **Advanced Search** platform.
- **Search by title:**

1. In the drop-down menu, select **"Title Keyword"**.
2. Type the title of the book in the next box.
3. A results page lists the items matching your terms; select your desired title.
4. Click the title to see its **full record** (bibliographic details), which also shows where the book is.
5. Check the **call number** to know the location.
6. Go to the shelf to retrieve the material.

- **Search by author:**

1. Select **"Author Keyword"** or **"Author (last name first)"**.
2. Type the name as **LAST NAME, FIRST NAME** if you chose "Author (last name first)". If you do not know the first name, leave it out or type the first initial.
3. Choose your author from the list of authors displayed.

- **Search by subject:** type the subject area in the space provided; a list of subjects is displayed. Subject searching lets you **browse for items by subject**.

**Explain more**

- Choosing the access point: if you do not know the author, use the **title keyword** when you know the title; if you know neither, use the **subject keyword**. Repeat the process with any other search term you have.
- **Trap:** "Clause heading" and "phrase heading" are not search types. The real access points are title, author, subject, series and keyword.

- **Questions to ask while searching the catalogue:** how many titles does the library have on your topic? What do the titles, dates and authors tell you? Are there subheadings? - **Interlibrary loan:** students have no reciprocal borrowing privileges at all Nigerian universities, but UNILAG library can borrow materials from any university library in Nigeria for you. It can take a week or longer, so start early.

### Lesson 13.4: Indexes, abstracts and bibliographies

**Must know**

- **Indexes and abstracts:** bibliographic information sources that help users find information quickly and efficiently. They are also called **bridges to published information**.
- **Index:** an **indicator or pointer** to required information. It gives a systematic guide to the contents of a text or series of texts: names, places, titles, keywords and titles of publications. An index may be published at the **back of a book** or as a **separate publication**. It does **not** give the full text.
- **4 uses of an index:**

1. Serves as a gateway to the information material.
2. Gives the basic information.
3. Provides only the entry points to users during their search for information.
4. Can list all the sources cited within a text or article.

- **Examples of indexes:** Newspaper Index, Engineering Index, Index Medicus. **Types of index:** book index, index to collections, periodical index, newspaper index.
- **Abstract:** a **brief summary** of the contents of a book, research article, thesis, speech or conference proceedings. **3 uses:**

1. Provides a description or summary of the content of the article.
2. Serves as a real **surrogate** (stand-in) of the document.
3. Lets a user know the content of the document without reading it from beginning to end.

- **Examples of abstracts:** Chemical Abstracts, Dissertation Abstracts, Library and Information Science Abstracts, Social Science Abstracts. **3 types of abstract:** **indicative**, **informative** and **evaluative**.
- **Bibliography:** a **list of books or written sources of information on a subject**, usually consulted in preparing a piece of work and referred to in the text. Examples in the University Library: **National Bibliography of Nigeria (NBN)** and **British National Bibliography (BNB)**.

**Explain more**

- Index versus abstract: an index only **points** to where information is; an abstract **summarises** what the document says. Both are now mainly online; abstracts are usually collected, organised systematically and indexed.
- **Trap:** "An index serves as a ______ to required information": the full answer is **pointer and indicator**, not one alone.
- **Trap:** "Review abstract" is not a type of abstract. The three types are indicative, informative and evaluative.
- Bibliography definitions you may see, all correct: a list of written sources on a subject; a list of books and articles someone used when writing on a subject; a list of books, magazines and articles about a particular subject. If all three appear together, the answer is "all of the above".

### Lesson 13.5: The internet and library databases

**Must know**

- **Internet:** has a wealth of knowledge for anyone with access, but **anyone with access can place false information** on it. There are **no guidelines** on the general web for accuracy. So information from the internet **cannot** be assumed accurate; it must be evaluated.
- **4 domain types:**

1. **.com:** originally commercial, but can be used for anything as long as it is paid for.
2. **.edu:** used **only by accredited post-secondary institutions** (institutions of higher learning).
3. **.gov and .us:** owned by the United States Government.
4. **.org:** owned by an organisation, but it does not matter **what type** of organisation.

- **Library databases:** collections of e-books, journals, newspapers and magazines, available anywhere with an internet connection and a login. They make searching for **peer-reviewed** articles easy. Examples of interfaces: **EBSCO, Science Direct, LexisNexis, JSTOR**.
- **Major benefit:** they are designed by proprietary publishers or aggregators and **subscribed to by the library to support student assignments and topics**. They are more organised than Google results and easier to document and cite.

**Explain more**

- The Martin Luther King example: Google returns Wikipedia (anyone can edit it, so not reliable), the Nobel Peace Prize site (generally reliable) and www.martinlutherking.org, which is hosted by Stormfront, a white-supremacist organisation. A .org address proves nothing about reliability.
- **Access at UNILAG:** databases are reached through the library website when you are within the university **IP range**; some fee-based databases need a **password** for remote access.
- **Match subject coverage:** most disciplines have their own databases; others are **interdisciplinary**. **EBSCO Host and JSTOR** are interdisciplinary; **Westlaw, HeinOnline and LexisNexis** are for Law; **JaypeeDigital** is for Clinical and Biomedical Sciences.
- **Match time coverage:** some databases give full text for the most recent 15 to 20 years, some only the current year, some over 100 years back.
- **7 types of information in databases:** full-text periodical articles; full-text encyclopaedic or reference sources; full-text books or chapters (e-books); government or primary documents; images, audio or video clips; statistical information; citations and/or abstracts.
- **Update frequency:** newspaper databases update **daily**; an encyclopaedia database irregularly or yearly. The more current your need, the more frequent the update you want.
- **2 most frequent ways results are ranked:** **chronologically by date** (most recent first; **LIFO**, Last In First Out) and by **relevancy**. Relevancy ranking counts how often search terms appear, how close they are to each other and how prominent they are; a term in the **title** counts as more relevant than one in the body.
- Read the **abstract** before the full article; it may show the article is not needed. Databases have **PRINT, EMAIL and SAVE** buttons; still keep a back-up on a storage device.

### Lesson 13.6: Evaluating information resources

**Must know**

- Resources are evaluated to determine their **credibility and relevance** to your topic before you select them for research or an assignment.
- **5 criteria:**

1. **Currency:** publication date; how up to date the citations are; how current the information needs to be for your topic.
2. **Relevancy:** does it relate directly to your topic or answer your question? Who is the audience (scholarly or general)? Is it primary or secondary? General background or in-depth?
3. **Authority:** who created it? Qualifications of the author, publisher or organisation; the author's education and experience; look for the author's biography or publisher information.
4. **Accuracy:** is it supported by evidence? Can you verify it in another source? How extensive is the bibliography? Are there spelling, grammar or typographical errors?
5. **Purpose:** is it meant to inform, sell or persuade? Is it fair and balanced? Are there political, ideological, cultural, religious or personal biases? Is opinion or propaganda easy to recognise? Are there advertisements?

**Explain more**

- **Trap:** **Size** is not a criterion. A long book is not more credible because it is big. Nor are "intelligence" or "strategy" criteria.
- "Currency, authority, ______ and ______": the missing pair is **relevancy and accuracy**.
- Quick reliability test: is it accurate, well written and current? Does it cover the topic thoroughly, state its purpose and give supporting documentation? Apply the five criteria to print and non-print sources alike.

### One-minute recap

- Information retrieval tools: library catalogue, indexes and abstracts, bibliography.
- Catalogue formats: manual (cards; author, title, subject; alphabetical) and OPAC (title, author, series, keyword). Shelf-list and Authority catalogues are for official use.
- OPAC search: Title Keyword or Author Keyword, then full record, then call number, then shelf.
- UNILAG Main Library: Library of Congress scheme (mixed, alpha-numeric notation; A to Z; O, X, Y reserved). College of Medicine: NLM scheme.
- K Law, P Language, T Technology, Q Science, R Medicine, H Social science, HM Sociology.
- Dewey Decimal: 000 to 999; not used at UNILAG.
- Index = pointer and indicator; abstract = brief summary (indicative, informative, evaluative); bibliography = list of written sources on a subject (NBN, BNB).
- Internet: anyone can post false information; .com, .edu (accredited post-secondary only), .gov/.us, .org.
- Library databases are subscribed to by the library for student assignments; EBSCO and JSTOR are interdisciplinary.
- Evaluate with currency, relevancy, authority, accuracy, purpose. Not size.
