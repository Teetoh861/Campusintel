## Topic 14: Search Strategies

**What you must know:** what a search strategy is and its 3 basic steps; the 5 stages of an electronic search; simple, advanced and meta searches; the 6 search techniques (Boolean AND/OR/NOT, parenthesis, phrase, truncation, wildcards, field searching); keyword vs subject searching; search engines and Google Scholar; how to narrow or broaden a search, save sources and choose quotations.

### Lesson 14.1: What a search strategy is

**Must know**

- **Search strategy:** the art of planning and conducting searches for the most efficient result. Its whole essence is to conceptualise a research topic and access the right information resources needed for a project, so you can navigate your way to the end of the project.
- **Effective use of library resources requires a search strategy.** It is the process employed to achieve effective search results.
- **A research exercise must be driven by a subject matter or topic.**

**3 basic steps** in a search strategy, in order:

1. **Identification of the subject matter/topic.**
2. **General knowledge/overview of the subject matter/topic:** attempt a definition of the topic to get the right conceptualisation or scope. Use a **general encyclopedia** (Encyclopedia Britannica, Encyclopedia Americana), or sometimes a specialised reference source. Overviews give insight into the historical development or antecedents of the area.
3. **Narrower overview:** move from the general concept to a narrower scope focused on specific areas of research and variables. Resources: **abstracts, indexes, bibliographies** (pointers or bridges to specific resources through their full bibliographic details), plus the **card catalogue** or **online catalogue** for specific titles or subjects.

- **Electronic search strategy:** a structured organisation of terms used to search a database; it shows how search terms combine to retrieve the best results. **Relevant** results are on target or very close to the information sought.
- A complex search statement is refined by adding **Boolean operators, quotation marks** (exact phrases), **truncation symbols** or **field search limiters**.

**The 5 stages of effective electronic searching** (do not rush to the first search box):

1. Define your information need.
2. Decide which resource/s to use (both e-resources and search engines).
3. Find out what search options your chosen resource offers.
4. Run your search.
5. Review and refine your search.

**Explain more**

- The 3 steps apply to both print and electronic resources. The physical library can be explored with the library guide and professional guidance; electronic resources demand **extra skills**.
- Trap: the 3 steps go from **general to narrow**. Encyclopedias belong to step 2; abstracts, indexes and bibliographies to step 3.
- Trap: "effective use of library resources requires..." **search strategies**, not punctuality, smartness or maturity.
- Keep the counts apart: **3 basic steps** of a search strategy; **5 stages** of an electronic search.

### Lesson 14.2: Types of searches

**Must know**

To search is to carefully look for something; effective searching requires planning. There are **3 types of searches**:

1. **Simple search:** the user uses some **keywords** to perform a **quick information search** from a database or a search engine. It may retrieve a **huge amount of output** that takes time to sieve through. It is browsing rather than focused searching; one or two concepts may be used. Use it when you are **not sure of the type of information required** and when the topic is **not focused on any area**.
2. **Advanced search:** the use of techniques that help to **define** the information being searched. **Filtering** is done to reduce the amount of items retrieved; filtering refines the search for relevancy. The user can apply **multiple search fields** to broaden or narrow the search. Available in most search tools such as **ScienceDirect, Ebrary, EbscoHost**.
3. **Meta search:** the user uses a **variety of search tools simultaneously**, through **meta search engines** such as **Dogpile, Search.com, Metacrawler and Vivisimo**. These search many search tools (Google, Yahoo, MSN) at once.

**3 things advanced searching helps you do:** apply filtering; reduce the number of items retrieved; apply multiple search fields.

| | Simple search | Advanced search | Meta search |
|---|---|---|---|
| Advantage 1 | Quick information search from a database or search engine | Allows filtering, so more relevant materials are retrieved | Uses a variety of search tools simultaneously |
| Advantage 2 | Helpful when not sure of the type of information required | Reduces the number of items retrieved | |
| Advantage 3 | Useful when the topic is not focused on any area | Applies multiple search fields | |

**Explain more**

- Example of a simple search: **Malaria and Africa**. It can cover any period, adults or children, male or female, any part of Africa, and may even retrieve information on Africa unrelated to malaria. The results are overwhelming, so the user must apply search techniques to focus them.
- A meta search is conducted the same way as a search in a single engine; the **only difference** is that results come from several engines simultaneously.
- Trap: "retrieves a huge amount of information" belongs to **simple** search, so it is the **exception** in any list of advanced-search advantages.
- Trap: "complex search" and "quick search" are not recognised types. Multiple search fields = **advanced search**; many search tools at once = **meta search**; quick keyword search = **simple search**.
- Advanced search lets you limit by fields such as **year of publication**, language or author, not by size of book, number of pages or volume.

### Lesson 14.3: Search techniques

**Must know**

- **Search techniques:** ways of using search terms to find required information from search tools such as **OPAC (Online Public Access Catalogues), general search engines, search directories, portals and online databases (the deep web)**.
- **Choosing keywords:** identify the main concepts of your topic and build a list of keywords; this increases your search capacity (acid rain: "wet deposition", "acid deposition"). Do **not** type a complete sentence. Leave out minor words: articles (a, an, the) and prepositional or verb phrases. Stick to **nouns and noun phrases** that express the major concepts, and search for **synonyms** as alternative terms.
- To find alternative keywords use a **thesaurus**, the **Library of Congress Subject Headings**, a **reference book** or **Internet subject directories**. Write down all your search terms so you know which worked.

**The 6 search techniques:**

1. **Boolean searching**
2. **Parenthesis**
3. **Phrase searching**
4. **Truncation**
5. **Wildcards**
6. **Field searching**

- **Boolean searching:** based on the symbolic logic of **George Boole**, a 19th-century English mathematician. Keywords are combined with connecting terms called **Boolean operators**; the **3 basic operators are AND, OR, NOT**. Boolean techniques **reduce irrelevant documents**. **2 reasons for using them:** to focus a search when the topic has multiple search terms; to connect pieces of information exactly as you want them (Second creation (title) AND Wilmot and Campbell (author) AND 2000 (year)).

| Operator | Effect | Tells the database | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **AND** | **Narrows** results | **ALL** terms must be present | cloning AND humans AND ethics |
| **OR** | **Broadens** results; connects similar concepts (**synonyms**) | **ANY** term may be present | cloning OR genetics OR reproduction |
| **NOT** | **Excludes** words; narrows | Ignore concepts implied by your terms | cloning NOT sheep |

- **Parenthesis:** brackets specify the **order** of interpretation. Information inside brackets is read **first**, then information outside. (vomit OR diarrhoea) AND blood; Malaria AND (prevention OR control). Without brackets, keywords are searched left to right.
- **Phrase searching:** surround keywords in **quotation marks** so the engine finds only words that appear **side by side** in that order. It narrows results significantly. "capital gains tax", "physician assisted suicide", "human genome project".
- **Truncation:** using an **asterisk (*)** at the **root** of a word so the tool retrieves all word variations after it. Purpose: to **broaden** results. teen* retrieves teen, teens, teenager, teenagers, teenaged, teensy, teeny.
- **Wildcards:** characters that represent **one character or letter** in a word; useful when you are **not sure of the spelling** (American vs English spelling). Most common symbol: **?**. An?mia retrieves anaemia and anemia; colo?r retrieves colour and color.
- **Field searching:** searching a **particular part of a computer record** (title, author's last name, date range, language; on the web, domain name, URL or file type). Use it to narrow very specifically: year of publication, language, affiliation, review articles, age, gender or abstract only.

**Explain more**

- **Discipline matters.** Information is organised into disciplines, and your topic's discipline shapes the research: one or more organisations may disseminate its information; there may be a standard terminology and a standard index; scholarly resources may be preferred over popular, primary over secondary, current over historical; and certain investigative methods may be preferred (laboratory experiments, simulations, field work, interviews, surveys, statistical analysis, tests and measurements, original thought and analysis).
- Keyword example: for a topic on university students who are heavy drinkers of alcohol and risk-taking behaviour, choose alcohol, alcoholic beverages, drinking, risk-taking behaviour, university students.
- **AND is implied** in many databases: Google turns "college students test anxiety" into college AND students AND test AND anxiety, so the words may appear separately. Fix this with phrases: "college students" AND "test anxiety".
- **Search order:** databases usually treat **AND as the primary operator** and connect AND terms first. When mixing AND and OR, put the alternatives in brackets: (ethic* OR moral*) AND (bioengineering OR cloning).
- Truncation cautions: do not truncate a very short root (too many words, poor relevance). Some engines truncate automatically for plural, -ing or -ed endings; symbols vary; some tools do not support it.
- Trap: **\* = truncation** (any letters after a root, broadens); **? = wildcard** (one letter inside a word, spelling variants). "The asterisk sign (*) is known as..." **truncation**, not wild card.
- Trap: **filtering is not a search technique**; it is what advanced searching does.
- Trap: OR **broadens**; AND and NOT **narrow** or limit. "Develop" and "differentiate" are not Boolean functions.

### Lesson 14.4: Keyword vs subject searching, search engines and Google Scholar

**Must know**

Most databases offer two ways to search:

- **Subject searching:** browsing for items by **subject heading**; returns only results where the term appears in the **subject field**.
- **Keyword searching:** looks for documents containing **one or more words specified by the user**, anywhere in the record.

| Keyword searching | Subject searching |
|---|---|
| Natural language words describing your topic; good to start with | Pre-defined **controlled vocabulary** used to describe each item's content |
| More flexible; terms combine in many ways | Less flexible; you must know the exact controlled vocabulary term |
| Database looks for keywords **anywhere** in the record, not necessarily connected | Database looks only in the **subject heading or descriptor field**, where the most relevant words appear |
| May yield too many or too few results | If too many results, **subheadings** focus on one aspect of the broader subject |
| May yield many irrelevant results | Results usually very relevant to the topic |

- **Search engines:** huge databases of Internet files collected by a computer program called a **crawler, robot or spider**. They have **no policies for selection of content** or evaluation, so results may not be what you expect. They use web browsers (e.g. Internet Explorer) to query the databases, index files into terms such as title, author and URL, and search mostly by **keywords**. Example: **Google**.
- **3 times to use a search engine:** for general information; when a topic has many facets; for current information.
- **Google Scholar:** a useful search tool for **academic information**. It holds scholarly material: **books, patents, journal articles and conference papers**, and links to the full text of articles and to related or similar articles. A search on Malaria +Africa gave results from a book and from journal articles.
- **Ranking of results:** databases rank retrieved articles by **date** (most recent first) and **relevance** (how often and how prominently the search terms appear in the record).

**Explain more**

- Trap: the tool for academic information is **Google Scholar**, not Google (whole web, no scholarly selection), not a web crawler (a program that collects pages) and not Opera Mini (a browser).
- Keyword searching is where you **start**; subject searching gives **precision** once you know the controlled term.
- Ranking is never by "number of times the search was conducted" or "number of records".

### Lesson 14.5: Assessing results, saving sources and quoting

**Must know**

- Research is seldom neat. A first search may give **too much**, **too little**, or **unhelpful** information; you may need to redesign your topic question. The most common problem is results that are **too broad or unrelated**; the way out is to **rethink (change) your search strategy**, not your topic, author or location.

**6 ways to narrow a search (too many results):**

1. Use **AND** to add relevant terms.
2. Use **NOT** to eliminate terms.
3. Search for words in the **title field** instead of the full text.
4. Use **phrase searching**.
5. Use **more specific vocabulary**.
6. Choose a **narrower category** of the major topic.

**5 ways to broaden a search (too few results):**

1. Combine **synonymous terms with OR**.
2. Think of the topic in **broader terms** and use more general vocabulary.
3. Use the **appropriate vocabulary for the discipline**.
4. **Eliminate the least important concept.**
5. Use the **"related documents"** option offered by some engines and databases.

**Saving sources:** save as much information about each source as possible, so you can find it again and build citations. Catalogues and databases let you **email, print or save** the citation information and sometimes the **full text**. For a website, save the **URL** (cut and paste it into your email). **Memorising** a source is not a way of saving it.

**Identifying sources to quote:** pick ideas and statements relevant to your topic. **4 reasons to use a quotation:** to add the power of the author's words to your argument; to disagree with the author's argument; to compare and contrast specific points of view; to note important research already available on your topic.

**Explain more**

- The narrowing and broadening lists are the Boolean table in action: AND and NOT narrow; OR broadens. Too **few** sources: **OR with synonyms**, never AND or NOT.
- Example of saving: in Academic Search Complete, open the record or full text and look for the email, print or save options. Some online newspapers and magazines let you email articles to yourself.

### One-minute recap

- Search strategy = the art of planning and conducting searches for the most efficient result; effective library use requires it.
- 3 basic steps: identify the topic; general overview (encyclopedia); narrower overview (abstracts, indexes, bibliographies, catalogue).
- 5 stages of electronic search: define need; choose resources; learn search options; run; review and refine.
- 3 types: simple (quick keywords, huge output), advanced (filtering, multiple fields), meta (many search tools at once: Dogpile, Metacrawler, Search.com, Vivisimo).
- 6 techniques: Boolean, parenthesis, phrase, truncation, wildcards, field searching. Filtering is not one.
- AND narrows (all terms); OR broadens (synonyms, any term); NOT excludes.
- Brackets read first; quotation marks = phrase; \* = truncation (broadens); ? = wildcard (one letter).
- Keyword search = natural language, anywhere in the record; subject search = controlled vocabulary, subject field only.
- Google Scholar is the tool for academic information; results are ranked by date and relevance.
- Too many results: AND, NOT, title field, phrases. Too few: OR synonyms, broader terms. Save sources by email, print or save.

### Theory practice

**Question:** Explain the three Boolean operators and give two reasons for using them in an online search.

*Answer outline:* Name George Boole. AND narrows: all terms must be present (cloning AND humans AND ethics). OR broadens: connects synonyms, any term may be present (cloning OR genetics OR reproduction). NOT excludes a word (cloning NOT sheep). Reasons: to focus a search with multiple terms; to connect pieces of information exactly as wanted (title AND author AND year). Mention that AND is often implied and that brackets fix the order.
