## Topic 9: Vocabulary Development

**What you must know:** what vocabulary is and where English words came from (Anglo-Saxon, Latin, French, Greek); active versus dormant (recognition) vocabulary and how to grow both; general and specialised dictionaries, including the thesaurus; the types of meaning (denotative, connotative, contextual); the word-formation processes (coining, blending, compounding, clipping, reduplicating, acronym, affixation, neologism); morphemes, roots, prefixes and suffixes; and how to choose the right word from a commonly confused pair.

### Lesson 9.1: What vocabulary is and where English words came from

**Must know**

- **Vocabulary:** the **total stock of words available in a language**.
- **Lexicographers** (word experts) estimate English has about **one million** words, yet hardly any learner knows **10,000**. **William Shakespeare** is said to have used **21,000** words in all his works.
- **Poor vocabulary** makes a speaker substitute an **almost-right word** for the right word, or simply avoid speaking his or her mind.
- **Meaning in words is largely a matter of agreement:** a word means what speakers agree it means. That is why a word from another language can become English once English speakers adopt it (e.g. **vuvuzela**, only Zulu before the FIFA 2010 World Cup in South Africa, English afterwards).
- English vocabulary is vast because of **four main influences**:

1. **Anglo-Saxon:** the **basic form** of English and the bulk of words known as **Native English**. The Anglo-Saxons were **three Germanic tribes**: the **Angles, the Saxons and the Jutes**, who invaded Britain (Britannia) in **450 AD** and settled. The land where the Angles settled was called **Angle-land**, from which **England** comes. Later the **Norse and the Vikings** (Scandinavian tribes) also invaded, adding to the cultural mix.
2. **Latin:** came in (a) through **Christianity** (Latin was the language of European Christianity, carried by the Roman church); (b) through the **Renaissance** in England, the rebirth of learning Greek and Roman classics. The Romans had invaded Britain long before the Anglo-Saxons but did **not interfere** with the native languages, **Gaelic, Celtic and Welsh**.
3. **French:** through the **Norman Conquest of 1066 AD**. French gave words for the **kitchen, cooking, government, administration and persons**. French also entered English indirectly through Latin, because French is **80% Latin**.
4. **Greek:** through the **Renaissance** and scholarly interest in Greek arts. To this day **scientists** use Greek to name inventions and discoveries.

**Explain more**

- Example words by origin (exams ask "which word came from..."):
    - **Anglo-Saxon:** everyday words: father, mother, son, wife, king, queen, house, home, sea, sheep, cow, dog, fire, bed, the numbers; tell, bring, drink, say, do, go, come, run, buy, sell, eat; dim, wise, good, little, brave, strong, merry; in, with, by, at, for, of, on, under; often, seldom; and, or, but, nor, until, because, unless.
    - **Latin:** estate, clergy, pastor, janitor, temple, altar, choir, disciple, candle, priest, century, nun, communion, martyr, cross, rite, shrine, monk, mass, lily, robe, pear, virgin. Many are **church** words.
    - **French:** vis-à-vis, souvenir, ambience, façade, milieu, elite, fiancée, mirage, genre, cuisine, vogue, faux pas, protégé, naïve, brochure, cliché, liaison, war, poultry, foyer, jacket, envoy, entrepreneur, debut, silhouette, courtesy, laissez-faire, connoisseur, camouflage, jargon, rapport, précis, résumé, chic, repertoire.
    - **Greek:** alphabet, grammar, syllable, analysis, mystery, angel, myth, hymn, anecdote, rhetoric, logic, school, economics, apostle, anthology, chronicle, sympathy, pseudonym, epitaph, democracy, acrobat, geometry, **atmosphere**.
- Trap: the Anglo-Saxons are **three** tribes (Angles, Saxons, Jutes); the Norse and Vikings came **a few centuries later** and are not Anglo-Saxons.
- Trap: the Norman Conquest is **French** influence (1066), not Latin. The Renaissance brought both **Latin and Greek**.

### Lesson 9.2: Two kinds of vocabulary, learning strategies and the dictionary

**Must know**

- **2 kinds of vocabulary:**

1. **Active vocabulary:** the words a speaker **uses frequently** in spoken and written English.
2. **Dormant or recognition vocabulary:** words a user **recognises** when seen or heard but **cannot clearly recall the meaning** of.

- **Strategies for growing vocabulary:**

1. Turn **recognition** vocabulary into **active** vocabulary: look up in the **dictionary** words that keep coming up in your reading, then **use them always** until you master them.
2. Keep your spoken and written English **simple** even as your vocabulary grows.
3. Build recognition vocabulary by **drawing new words** from the books you read and speeches you hear. **The wider your reading, the larger your recognition vocabulary.**

- **A good dictionary is the basic tool** every student needs for effective study. It aids vocabulary development by giving **meanings**, **pronunciation**, **spelling** and **context of usage** (all of these).
- A dictionary tells you a word's **definition, spelling, usage, capitalisation, synonyms and antonyms, syllabication, word class and pronunciation**; unabridged ones add **etymology** (origin). It does **not** record the **year a word became obsolete** (at most it labels a word archaic).
- **2 types of dictionary:**

1. **General (non-specialised):** wider coverage and usage. Two kinds: **unabridged** (contain most words of English with full details, some with etymology; the best source, so get one: e.g. Oxford Advanced Learner's Dictionary, Longman Dictionary of Contemporary English, BBC English Dictionary, Cambridge Dictionary of English) and **abridged** (shorter versions; guide spelling, capitalisation, punctuation and vocabulary; easy to carry: e.g. The Oxford Pocket Dictionary, Roget Pocket Dictionary, American Heritage Dictionary of the English Language, The Short Oxford English Dictionary). Always buy the **newest edition**.
2. **Specialised (technical):** compiled for a **special purpose**; more thorough coverage of one subject. Three kinds:
    - **Dictionary of synonyms:** lists of words of **similar meaning**. The **thesaurus** is designed for this. Examples: **Webster's Collegiate Thesaurus, Roget International Thesaurus, Modern Guide to Synonyms and Related Words**.
    - **Scholarly dictionaries:** specialised in an area of learning: Oxford Dictionary of Philosophy / Psychology / Sociology, A Dictionary of Medical Terms, Standard Dictionary of Folklore, Mythology and Legend, Modern Science Dictionary, Medical Dictionary.
    - **Usage dictionaries:** show **how words are used** in speech and writing; help with **collocation**, **synonyms** and **common errors**.

- **Collocation:** a group of words that habitually go together and behave like a **single word with a single meaning** (e.g. make a decision, earn a living).

**Explain more**

- A **thesaurus** is a **specialised dictionary** (not simplified, pictorial or phonetic). Because it is a dictionary of synonyms, it is at once a **list of words**, a set of **synonymous terms** and a **collection of selected terminology**; if an option says "all of the above", take it.
- Other specialised dictionaries you may meet in questions: a **Dictionary of American Slang**, a dictionary of a particular language (e.g. Yoruba), a dictionary of politics. **Webster's** and the **Oxford English Dictionary** are **general** dictionaries.
- Usage example: instead of the vague word **thing**, a usage dictionary suggests precise words: aspect, attribute, characteristic, detail, feature, issue, matter, point, subject, topic, trait. Say "very useful", not "a very useful thing"; "something important", not "an important thing"; "so much", not "so many things".
- Dictionary usage entries draw on classroom research and the **British National Corpus**.
- Trap: dormant vocabulary is **not** unknown vocabulary; you recognise the word, you just cannot pin down its meaning. Unknown words are a third group.

### Lesson 9.3: Types of meaning

**Must know**

- A word may have **two types of meaning**, and **context** can add a third:

1. **Denotative meaning:** the **literal** meaning; the meaning **lexicographers give a word and enter into the dictionary**; the meaning of a word **in isolation**, making **direct reference** to a thing. Dark = lightlessness, no visibility; night = the hours outside daytime.
2. **Connotative meaning:** meaning **associated with a word based on feelings, emotions and experiences**; an **underlying** meaning beyond the literal; it is **figurative**, and it depends on the word's **environment and usage**. "He's dark" = evil or malicious; "in my dark moments" = difficult, harsh, sorrowful times.
3. **Contextual meaning (verbal context):** the meaning you work out for an unfamiliar word from the **other words in the sentence**.

- Useful clues to contextual meaning come from **paired words** or **parallelism**. Paired words are either **complementary (parallel)**, so the words are **similar** in meaning, or **contrasting**, so they are **opposite** in meaning.
- **Poetic meaning** is usually **figurative** (connotative), not denotative.

**Explain more**

- Connotations may be **positive or negative**. In the pairs persistent/stubborn, notorious/famous, secret/covert, smart/intelligent, light/fair, mountainous/huge, brilliant/clever, the word with the kinder feeling (persistent, famous, intelligent, fair, brilliant) has the positive connotation. **Persistent/stubborn** is the pair where one side (**stubborn**) is clearly negative; light/fair, brilliant/clever and smart/intelligent are approving on both sides.
- Worked examples of contextual clues:
    - "Since he isn't **wasteful**, he is **frugal**": the negation of wasteful tells you frugal = careful with money (contrast).
    - "He was **unscrupulous** and **dishonest**": paired by "and", so similar (complementary).
    - "Her **pulchritude** and **beauty** charmed the audience": pulchritude is a synonym of beauty.
    - "He's slow because he's a **tyro**" = beginner. "**Vitriolic** remarks" = angry. "The counsel **prayed** the judge" = pleaded. "A **sneaking** suspicion" = niggling.
- Trap: options exams swap for "denotative": "words used scientifically", "words which refer to the past", "listing in alphabetical order". None is right; denotative words **make direct reference**. "Expressive" and "discursive" are not types of meaning; the pair is **denotative/connotative**.

### Lesson 9.4: Word-formation strategies

**Must know**

Words have entered English through various formation processes. The **processes of inventing words** are **coining, blending, compounding, clipping and reduplicating**; words are also formed by **affixation, neologism and acronyms** (8 processes in all).

1. **Coining:** creating a word or phrase for a **specific use**, knowing it does not exist in the lexicon; the situation gives it meaning. Example: **"wardrobe malfunction"** (Janet Jackson). Others: skyjacker, skydiver, credibility gap, Clintonomics, normalcy, Oliver Twist (a person never contented), Casanova (a flirtatious man).
2. **Blending:** combining **part of one word with part of another**; the blend **shares the meaning of both**. sit-com (situation + comedy), smog (smoke + fog), chillax (chill + relax), netiquette (internet + etiquette), camcorder (camera + recorder), chortle (chuckle + snort), telex (teleprinter + exchange), mimsy (miserable + flimsy). Also brunch, malware, webinar.
3. **Compounding:** the **easiest** method; combining **two or more existing (independent) words** to form a new word. Written as **one word** (boardroom, litterbug, sweatshop, linesman), **two words** (floppy disk, chat show, rummage sale, surrogate mother) or **hyphenated** (fail-safe, on-screen, one-upmanship, link-up).
4. **Clipping:** **shortening long words that are awkward in everyday speech**; part is clipped off, leaving the frequently used part. gov (governor), phone (telephone), bike (bicycle), exam (examination), lit (literature), civvy (civilian), lunch (luncheon); also ad, zoo, prep, gents, fax (facsimile).
5. **Reduplicating:** **doubling all or part of a word** with a single vowel or consonant change. hanky-panky, humdrum, boogie-woogie, helter-skelter, walkie-talkie, yum-yum, topsy-turvy, nitty-gritty, dilly-dally, titbit.
6. **Acronym:** an abbreviation formed from the **initials** (or first two letters) of each word of a term; it becomes a word when added to the lexicon. Mostly from **names of organisations, inventions and discoveries**. WAN (wide area network), VAT (value added tax), NASA, FIFA, NATO, OPEC, POTUS (President of the United States), scuba (self-contained underwater breathing apparatus), WYSIWYG (what you see is what you get).
7. **Affixation:** using **prefixes and suffixes** to form words (Lesson 9.5).
8. **Neologism:** the process in which **old words are given new meanings**. Many dated words have taken on new meanings; some keep the old meaning as well.

**Explain more**

- Neologism examples (old meaning → new meaning): **nice** (pretending to be good → beautiful, kind, smart); **lady** (wife of a noble → spinster, single mother); **dull** (slow to learn → not flashy, boring); **first lady** (queen → wife of a president or governor); **promised land** (Canaan → a place of happiness and safety); **Waterloo** (a place name → a defeat); **bad** (unjust, wicked → dangerous, amazing). T. S. Eliot, in *Murder in the Cathedral*, used **malevolence** so that his audience read it as "violence by men".
- Trap: in its widest sense a **neologism** is any **newly coined word or new meaning**. So in a question like "all but one are neologisms: atmosphere, brunch, malware, webinar", the odd one out is **atmosphere**, an old Greek-derived word; brunch, malware and webinar are recent blends. But when asked to **name the process in which old words get new meanings**, the answer is **neologism**, not coining (which makes a brand-new word) or blending.
- **Forms a word can take:** **simple** (one unit: table), **compound** (two or more independent words: suitcase, blackboard) and **complex** (a base with affixes: unreliable). **Compound-complex** is a type of **sentence**, never a word form.
- Trap: a blend joins **parts** of two words (smog); a compound joins **whole** words (boardroom); an acronym uses **initials** (NATO); clipping **cuts** one word (exam). "Chopped", "chipped" and "crimped" are not word-formation terms; the term is **clipped**.
- **Conversion** (changing a word's class without changing its form, e.g. "to google") is another process you may meet in options; it is not clipping.

### Lesson 9.5: Affixation and morphemes

**Must know**

- **Morpheme:** the smallest meaningful unit of a word.
- **Free morpheme:** can stand alone as a word (happy, boy, the). Free morphemes are sub-categorised into **lexical** (content words: nouns, verbs, adjectives, adverbs, e.g. boy, run, happy) and **functional** (grammar words: articles, prepositions, conjunctions, pronouns, e.g. the, of, and, she).
- **Bound morpheme:** cannot stand alone; must attach to a free morpheme or root (un-, -ness, -ed). **Affixes are bound morphemes.**
- **Root (stem, base):** the part of a word **to which other elements can be added**. The base of **educational** is **educate** (educate + -ion → education, + -al → educational).
- **Affixes:** the general name for **prefixes and suffixes**. **Affixation** is the **process** of adding them. **Every complex word contains an affix.**
- **Prefix:** a bound morpheme added at the **beginning** of a root (un- in unfaithful, unreliable). **Suffix:** a bound morpheme added at the **end** (-ly in happily).
- **Unhappily = prefix (un-) + root (happy) + suffix (-ly).**
- Affixes are **derivational** when they make a new word, changing the meaning and/or the word class (un- in unreliable; -ness in sadness), and **inflectional** when they only change tense or number without making a new word (-ed, -s).
- **Suffix types:**

1. **Noun-forming, added to verbs:** -al, -ance, -ion, -ment, -ure (arrival, severance, governance, domination, development, failure).
2. **Noun-forming, added to adjectives:** -dom, -ism, -cy, -ity, -ty, -ness, -th (freedom, socialism, normalcy, normality, loyalty, goodness, warmth).
3. **Noun-forming, added to nouns:** -age, -er, -hood, -ful, -dom, -ry, -ule (harbourage, worker, boyhood, handful, dukedom, forestry, globule).
4. **Adjective-forming, added to nouns:** -ful, -less, -ish (faithful, faithless, girlish).
5. **Verb-forming, added to adjectives:** -ize (socialize, idolize).
6. **Tense-forming, added to regular verbs:** -ed, -d (wanted, changed).
7. **Plural-forming:** -s, -es, -i, -ies, -ia, -ae (boys, classes, foci, criteria, personae).

- **Prefix types:** a **negative prefix** yields the **opposite** meaning of the root (a-, de-, dis-, contra-, mis-, im-, in-, retro-, un-). A **positive prefix** indicates **intensity, degree, emphasis or position** and does **not** form an antonym (extra-, post-, re-, sub-, semi-, co-, ultra-, hyper-, trans-, pro-, pre-, inter-, ante-, mega-, self-, circum-, bi-, mono-, pseudo- and others).

**Explain more**

- Affixation may **change meaning and word class** (govern → government), **change class but keep meaning** (arrive → arrival), or keep both while changing tense or number (wanted; boys).
- A **verb-forming prefix** can turn a noun into a verb: disgrace, imperil. A negative prefix on a negative word repositions it as positive: fetter → unfetter.
- **-ness** attaches to **adjectives** to make nouns: apt → aptness, just → justness, sad → sadness. It does not attach to an adverb (sadly) or to a word that is already a noun (comfort). If asked which of apt, sadly, comfort, just takes -ness "by way of spelling", the keyed answer is **apt** (aptness).
- **anti-** means **against** (anti-social, antibiotic); do not confuse it with **ante-**, which means **before** (antenatal). **-un** in unreliable is a **prefix**, a **bound** morpheme and, more precisely, a **derivational** one, because it creates a new word with the opposite meaning.
- Trap: a **phrase** is a group of words, not a word part. Prefix and suffix are the things **added**; the root is what they are added **to**.
- Trap: "grammatical" is just another name for functional; the standard sub-categories of free morphemes are **lexical and functional**.

### Lesson 9.6: Choosing the right word: commonly confused pairs

**Must know**

Good vocabulary means picking the **right** word, not the almost-right one. Learn these tested pairs:

1. **Discover / invent:** you **discover** what already exists (a planet, a mineral); you **invent** what did not exist until someone made it. *Who **invented** the telephone?*
2. **Persuaded / convinced:** **convinced** = made to **believe** a fact is true; **persuaded** = talked into **doing** something. *The judge was **convinced** that the man was guilty.*
3. **Took / received:** a prize is **given**, so the winner **receives** it; **took** suggests seizing. *He **received** a prize for his diligence.*
4. **Interfere in / interfere with:** you **interfere in** affairs that are not your business; something **interferes with** another thing by disturbing or blocking it (noise interferes with reading). *It is not wise to **interfere in** family quarrels.*
5. **Accept / agree:** **agree to** do something (say yes to a request); **accept** takes a noun object (accept an offer), never "to go". *She did not **agree** to go.*
6. **Deals with / deals in:** **deal in** = buy and sell as a trade; **deal with** = handle or manage a problem or person. *A butcher **deals in** meat.*
7. **Earn / win:** you **earn** a living (by work); you **win** prizes, games, competitions. *How does that man **earn** his living?*
8. **Remember / remind:** to **remind** someone is to make them remember; you **remember** for yourself. *Please **remind** me to give the money back.*
9. **Avenged / revenged:** to **avenge** a wrong is to punish the wrongdoer **on behalf of a victim**, in the name of justice; **revenge** is personal retaliation (revenge yourself on an enemy). *The judge **avenged** the wrong done to the child.*
10. **Love / like:** **love** is deep and intense; **like** is weak (find pleasant). *Some people **love** freedom so much that they are ready to die for it.*

**Explain more**

- Test each pair by the **object** it takes: invent a device, discover a fact; convinced **that**, persuaded **to**; agree **to go**, accept **an invitation**; deal **in** goods, deal **with** people or problems; earn **money**, win **a match**.
- Check the **strength** of the word: the seriousness of "ready to die for it" tells you **love**, not like.
- These are the "almost-right word" errors a weak vocabulary produces; a usage dictionary lists them under common errors.

### One-minute recap

- Vocabulary = the total stock of words in a language; English has about one million words, Shakespeare used 21,000, most learners know under 10,000.
- Sources of English words: Anglo-Saxon (Angles, Saxons, Jutes, 450 AD; Native English), Latin (Christianity, Renaissance), French (Norman Conquest 1066), Greek (Renaissance, science).
- Active vocabulary = words you use; dormant/recognition = words you recognise but cannot define. Grow both by reading widely and using the dictionary.
- Dictionaries: general (unabridged, abridged) and specialised (synonyms/thesaurus, scholarly, usage). A thesaurus is a specialised dictionary of synonyms.
- Denotative = literal, dictionary, in isolation; connotative = feelings, figurative, context; contextual meaning comes from paired words (complementary = similar, contrasting = opposite).
- Word formation: coining, blending, compounding, clipping, reduplicating, acronym, affixation, neologism (old words, new meanings).
- Word forms: simple, compound, complex; compound-complex is a sentence type.
- Morphemes: free (lexical, functional) and bound (affixes). Prefix + root + suffix (un-happi-ly). Every complex word has an affix.
- anti- = against; ante- = before. -ness makes nouns from adjectives.
- Right word: invent/discover, convinced/persuaded, received, interfere in, agree to, deal in, earn a living, remind, avenge, love.
