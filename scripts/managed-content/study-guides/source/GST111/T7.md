## Topic 7: Writing Skills I: The Paragraph

**What you must know:** what a paragraph is; the 8 types and their helper words; the topic sentence and the 5 qualities of a good one; the 3-stage structure; the 5-step process; the 3 troubleshooting problems; the 4 elements; when to start a new paragraph; transitions and signposts.

### Lesson 7.1: What a paragraph is

**Must know**

- **Paragraph:** a unit of any composition comprising a collection of related sentences dealing with a single topic. Simply: a sentence or group of sentences that support one main idea.
- It usually contains a **main idea** and may contain a **sub-idea**.
- **Essay:** a combination of ideas from one paragraph to another that creates a united whole; all paragraphs support each other, leading from the first idea to the final resolution.
- **Length is not a determinant** in defining a paragraph. Single-sentence paragraphs are rare, so write a **minimum of three sentences**.
- A good paragraph shows **unity and coherence**, just as a good composition shows them among its paragraphs. These are the features shared by paragraphs and essays.

**Explain more**

- The unit described is the paragraph: not the sentence (too small), the essay (made of paragraphs) or "text" (too general).
- Trap: the shared pair is **unity and coherence**, not "unity and self-sufficiency", "cohesion and transparency" or "transition and cohesion". Transition is only a device for achieving coherence.
- True of paragraphs: they vary in length, each addresses only an aspect of the topic, and a paragraph is hardly ever longer than the entire essay. **Not** true: every paragraph begins with a topic sentence (Lesson 7.3).

### Lesson 7.2: The 8 types of paragraph

**Must know**

Learn each type by what it does, then its **helper words**.

1. **Definition:** explains the key words or main topic of a composition. Helpers: "is defined as", "a kind of". *Suicide is defined as the act of taking one's own life.*
2. **Classification:** groups things or ideas into specific categories. Helpers: "is a kind of", "can be divided into", "is a type of", "falls under", "belongs to", "is a part of", "fits into", "is grouped with", "is related to", "is associated with". *Coal is a kind of non-renewable resource.*
3. **Descriptive:** describes a person, place or thing. Helpers: "measurement", "location", "size", "length", "is like", "colour", "width", "resembles", "above", "shape", "mass/weight", "below", "purpose", "speed", "beside", "near", "north/east/west". *Ogbomosho is west of Ibadan.*
4. **Comparison and contrast:** similarities and differences between two or more people, places, things or ideas. Helpers: "is similar to", "on the other hand", "both", "however", "also", "but", "too", "in contrast to", "differs from", "while". *Both Jos and Lagos have rain in August. However, rain in Lagos is heavier.*
5. **Sequence:** describes a series of events or a process in some sort of order, usually based on **time**. Helpers of order: "first", "second", "third", "in the beginning"; of time: "recently", "previously". *First, join a political party. Second, win your party's nomination. Third, win the election.*
6. **Choice:** you choose which object, idea or action you prefer and give your opinion. Helpers of point of view: "in my opinion", "belief", "feel"; of personal opinion: "like/dislike", "hope", "idea". *In my opinion, football is more fun than lawn tennis.*
7. **Explanation:** explains **how or why** something happens; explores **causes and effects**. Helpers: "because", "therefore", "thus", "since". *Living conditions were terrible. Thus, many people moved to Europe.*
8. **Evaluation:** makes **judgements** about people, ideas and possible actions, based on **criteria** you develop; you state your evaluation or recommendation and support it. Helpers of criteria: "good/bad", "correct/incorrect", "moral/immoral"; of recommendation: "suggest", "recommend", "advise". *I recommend that pesticides should not be used because they are harmful.*

**Explain more**

- Trap: "a series of events or a process in some sort of order" = **sequence**, not descriptive (a person, place or thing) and not explanation.
- Trap: "explains how or why something happens" = **explanation**, not definition (key words) or sequence (time order).
- Choice vs evaluation: choice is what *I prefer*; evaluation is a judgement by criteria (good/bad, moral/immoral) plus a recommendation.

### Lesson 7.3: The topic sentence

**Must know**

- **Topic sentence:** the sentence that **states the main idea** of the paragraph and **works as a summary** of it.
- It is **the most important sentence** (and the most important part) of a paragraph.
- It is **often but not always** the first sentence: usually first or second, occasionally last, sometimes in the middle.
- A thesis statement is to an **essay** what a topic sentence is to **the paragraph**: it is the **thesis of the paragraph**.
- Read in sequence, an essay's topic sentences give a **sketch of the argument**.

**5 qualities of a good topic sentence:**

1. **Concise and emphatic:** no longer than the idea requires; stresses the important word. *The Nigerian banking system has collapsed*: brief, clear, strong, with the key word "collapsed" stressed at the end.
2. Lets readers **see your point immediately**; especially useful in an application or an argumentative essay.
3. Placed **at the end** of the paragraph when specific details **lead up to a generalisation**.
4. Occasionally **avoided**, when the main idea is so obvious it need not be stated.
5. **Carefully worded and restricted** (focused), which helps you generate and control your information.

**Explain more**

- Trap: "the most important sentence in a paragraph" is the **topic** sentence, not "first", "last" or "concluding". First and last are positions; the concluding sentence is optional.
- Trap: "states the main idea and works as its summary" is the topic sentence, not the conclusion (which only closes the paragraph).
- Since it can be first, middle, last or omitted, "every paragraph begins with a topic sentence" is **false**.

### Lesson 7.4: Structure of a paragraph and the 5-step process

**Must know**

**3 stages of a typical academic paragraph:**

| Stage | Name | Status |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Topic sentence or main idea | Essential |
| 2 | Body: support or detail sentences | Essential |
| 3 | Conclusion or concluding sentence | Optional |

- A paragraph is **complete** when it has **a topic sentence and supporting details**.
- Paragraph structure **parallels essay structure** in order and content: a coherent argument, supporting evidence/analysis, and a conclusion.

**3 contents of a paragraph:**

1. **Topic sentence:** **two purposes**: (1) the **thesis of your paragraph**; (2) pushes the essay's thesis forward and presents an arguable point. Usually first or second sentence; at the end occasionally, but not as a habit.
2. **Support or detail sentences:** the **evidence/analysis** that makes your claim digestible. Balance **evidence** (facts, quotations, summary of events) with **analysis** (interpretation). Evidence-heavy = no argument; analysis-heavy = claim not supported.
3. **Concluding observation:** closes with an observation that is **more than a summary**; a final idea that leads to the next step. Usually the last or second-to-last sentence.

**Length:** paragraphs are hardly ever longer than one page, double spaced. **Lengthy** paragraphs signal a **lack of structure** (split them); **short** paragraphs signal a **lack of substance** (develop or merge).

**The 5-Step Process to Paragraph Development** (model paragraph: African oral narratives, proved by **illustration**):

1. **Decide on a controlling idea and create a topic sentence.** The controlling idea directs the paragraph's development and often appears as the topic sentence. *African oral narratives often had hidden double meanings.*
2. **Explain the controlling idea:** tell the reader how to interpret the topic sentence. *On one level the narratives entertained; on another they spoke truths and imparted moral virtues.*
3. **Give an example (or multiple examples):** support or evidence for the idea. The story "The tortoise goes to a feast in the sky": tortoise, disguised as a bird, attended a feast meant for birds; his greed made the birds take back their feathers.
4. **Explain the example(s):** show each example's relevance to the topic sentence, usually in a separate sentence. Abandoned in the sky, tortoise sent a message home; the birds delivered the wrong message, his wife brought out furniture instead of mattresses, and he broke his back.
5. **Complete the paragraph's idea or transition into the next paragraph:** tie up loose ends and recall the main idea, or move the reader on. *The moral is that greed could lead a person to becoming pretentious and would ultimately lead to his destruction.*

**Explain more**

- Steps 3 and 4 **can be repeated** until the main idea is fully developed.
- Trap: the process has **5** steps, not 1, 3 or 7. "Choosing a controlling idea", "giving examples" and "explaining the controlling idea" are all real steps; asked which is not a step, pick "none of the above".
- Trap: "complete" does not mean a capital letter and a full stop, and "an argument" on its own is not what completes a paragraph; the answer is a topic sentence and supporting details.

### Lesson 7.5: Troubleshooting and the 4 elements

**Must know**

**Troubleshoot a paragraph in 3 cases:**

1. **Topic sentence problem.** The topic sentence may be at the beginning, middle or end; it must be **in there somewhere**. A paragraph may open with a **transition sentence** (*Like sharks, snakes and scorpions, dogs are widely feared*); the topic sentence should follow (*Although most people consider dogs quite dangerous, they are, for the most part, entirely harmless*). If it never comes, you have a problem.
2. **Multiple controlling ideas.** **Eliminate** sentences on the second idea, or **split** into two or more paragraphs with one idea each. In the dogs paragraph, the sentences about groups that fry and eat dogs branch off; the revision removes them and ends by returning to the main idea.
3. **Transitions within the paragraph** (intra-paragraph linkages): single words or short phrases that relate ideas and create logical progression, especially across multiple examples (*however*, *The first is...*, *The second is...*).

Length (too long or too short) is **not** one of the three cases.

**4 elements of an effective paragraph** (they overlap):

1. **Unity:** a **single focus** throughout; it must not begin with one point and end with another, or wander. Unity = **singularity of focus**.
2. **Coherence:** the trait that makes the paragraph **easily understandable to a reader**, created by **logical bridges** and **verbal bridges**.
    - **Logical bridges (2):** the same idea carried from sentence to sentence; successive sentences in **parallel form**.
    - **Verbal bridges (4):** repeated **key words**; repeated **synonyms**; **pronouns** referring to nouns in earlier sentences; **transition words** linking sentences.
3. **A topic sentence:** indicates in a general way the idea or thesis of the paragraph. Not every paragraph has a clear-cut one, and it can be anywhere, but the easy way is **near the beginning**. Either way you should be able to summarise the paragraph.
4. **Adequate development:** the topic is discussed **fully and adequately**. A paragraph of only two or three sentences is probably not fully developed.

**11 methods of adequate development:** (1) use examples and illustrations; (2) cite data (facts, statistics, evidence, details); (3) examine testimony (quotes and paraphrases); (4) use an anecdote or story; (5) define terms; (6) compare and contrast; (7) evaluate causes and reasons; (8) examine effects and consequences; (9) analyse the topic; (10) describe the topic; (11) offer a chronology of an event (time segments).

**Explain more**

- Trap: "easily understandable to the reader" = **coherence**, not unity or development. "Completeness" is not an element.
- Trap: unity is not "connection with other paragraphs", brevity or elocution.
- Trap: ways of enhancing coherence are transition words, synonyms, repeated key words **and pronouns**. "Avoiding pronouns" is **not** one.

### Lesson 7.6: New paragraphs, transitions and signposts

**Must know**

**Start a new paragraph in 4 cases:**

1. **When you begin a new idea or point.** Each new point within an extended idea gets its own paragraph.
2. **To contrast information or ideas:** sides in a debate, points in an argument, any other difference.
3. **When your readers need a pause:** a short break for readability, when the paragraph grows too long or the material is complex.
4. **When you are ending your introduction or starting your conclusion.** These always sit in new paragraphs; many introductions and conclusions have several.

It is **not** ideal to start a new paragraph **when you have just introduced the topic**; stay and support it.

**Transitions and signposts** are two very important elements of paragraphing:

- **Signposts:** internal aids to readers; usually several sentences or a paragraph outlining **what the article has covered and where it is going**.
- **Transitions:** usually one or several sentences that move from one idea to the next, used at the **end of most paragraphs** so they flow into the next. Transitions are also needed within paragraphs (Lesson 7.5).

**Explain more**

- **Linking devices** (transition words) show how an idea follows from the last: *furthermore* (adding), *however* (contrasting), *finally* (ordering), *therefore*, *thus*, *on the other hand*.
- Trap: **because** is **not** a linking device in this sense; it is a subordinating conjunction joining a dependent clause to a main clause inside one sentence. Among *furthermore*, *however*, *finally* and *because*, the odd one out is *because*.
- Trap: a new paragraph is **not** ideal "when you have introduced the topic"; concluding, contrasting and introducing a new idea are all proper moments.

### One-minute recap

- Paragraph = unit of composition: related sentences on a single topic; at least 3 sentences; length does not define it.
- Paragraphs and essays share **unity and coherence**.
- 8 types: definition, classification, descriptive, comparison and contrast, sequence (time order), choice, explanation (how or why), evaluation (criteria plus recommendation).
- Topic sentence = states the main idea and works as its summary; the most important sentence; the thesis of the paragraph; often but not always first.
- Good topic sentence: concise and emphatic; shows the point at once; at the end when details build to a generalisation; sometimes avoided; carefully worded and restricted.
- Structure: topic sentence (essential), support sentences (essential), concluding sentence (optional).
- 5 steps: controlling idea and topic sentence; explain it; example(s); explain example(s); complete or transition.
- Troubleshoot 3 problems: topic sentence, multiple controlling ideas, transitions within the paragraph. Not length.
- 4 elements: unity, coherence (logical and verbal bridges), topic sentence, adequate development (11 methods).
- New paragraph: new idea, contrast, pause, ending introduction or starting conclusion. Signposts say where the piece has been and is going; transitions carry one idea into the next.
