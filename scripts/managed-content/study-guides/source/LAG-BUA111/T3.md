## Topic 3: Marketing Concepts and Market Orientation

**What you must know:** what a marketing concept (philosophy) is; the five competing concepts in order (production, product, selling, marketing, societal) plus holistic/relationship marketing; each concept's core assumption, focus, strengths and dangers; the selling versus marketing contrast; and market orientation with its three behavioural components and two decision criteria.

### Lesson 3.1: Meaning of a marketing concept and the order of evolution

**Must know**

- **Marketing concept (broad sense) / marketing management philosophy:** the guiding idea about what should drive a firm's marketing activities, and how much weight to give the interests of the **organisation**, **customers** and **society**. It is a business philosophy and an attitude of mind that the whole organisation must adopt, not just the marketing department.
- **5 major competing concepts** of marketing activities in organisations:

1. Production concept
2. Product concept
3. Selling (sales) concept
4. Marketing concept
5. Societal marketing concept

- Newer thinking adds the **holistic marketing concept** (with relationship marketing inside it).
- **Order of development:** production → selling → marketing → societal marketing. (The product concept is usually left out of this four-step sequence.)
- **Direction of the shift:** from product-centric efficiency towards **customer-centric value creation**, with an increasing emphasis on customer **and** societal needs.

| Concept | Consumers will favour... | So the firm should... |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Production | products widely available and low in price | improve production and distribution efficiency |
| Product | products of the highest quality, performance, features | keep improving the product |
| Selling | not buy enough unless persuaded | sell and promote aggressively |
| Marketing | products that meet their needs better than rivals' | find needs, then satisfy them |
| Societal marketing | the above, done in a way that protects long-run welfare | balance profit, wants and society's interests |

- **Inside-out:** starts from the factory and the firm's existing products (production, product, selling).
- **Outside-in:** starts from the market and customer needs, then coordinates the firm to satisfy them (marketing, societal).

**Explain more**

- You will also meet the older split between the **traditional concept** of marketing (narrow, product oriented: the physical transfer of goods for money) and the **modern concept** (broad, customer oriented: earning profit through customer satisfaction). The traditional view lines up with the production, product and selling concepts; the modern view with the marketing and societal concepts.
- Never put the marketing concept before the selling concept: firms turned to customer needs only after aggressive selling stopped working. Later concepts did not wipe out earlier ones; many Nigerian firms still think in production or selling terms today.

### Lesson 3.2: The production and product concepts

**Must know**

- **Production concept:** consumers will buy (favour) products that are **widely available** and **low in price**. Management should concentrate on **high production efficiency** and **widespread distribution**.
  - The **earliest** philosophy; it describes business in the **late 1800s** and early industrial era.
  - Emerged in response to **industrial advancements enabling mass production**, when **demand for goods was greater than supply**.
  - Primary objective: **maximising output and distribution efficiency**; firms succeeded through **economies of scale and broad distribution**.
  - Still useful when (1) **demand exceeds supply**, or (2) the product's **cost is too high** and must be brought down.
  - Its assumption is challenged when consumers **prioritise quality and differentiation over price**.
  - **Main danger:** the firm becomes so focused on its own operations and efficiency that it **loses sight of satisfying customers' needs**.
- **Product concept:** consumers will buy (favour) products of the **highest quality**, most **performance** and most **innovative features**. Management should make continuous product improvements.
  - Based on the **"technology-push"** model of innovation: products designed by engineers and scientists for consumers to use.
  - Trap: "build a better mousetrap and customers will beat a path to your door", while ignoring what customers actually want.
  - Leads to **marketing myopia**.
- **Marketing myopia** (Theodore Levitt, 1960): an undue concentration on the **product** rather than on the **customer need or benefit** it provides.

**Explain more**

- **Production example:** a Kano noodle maker facing demand far above supply adds production lines, cuts unit costs and spreads distribution so more homes can afford a ₦250 pack. That is the production concept. Dangote Cement pushing scale and low cost per bag uses the same logic.
- Hyundai (cars) and Goldstar, Sharp and Sanyo (electronics) succeeded with low-cost production abroad. So the production concept "has a place" today, but focusing on production while ignoring customers is as faulty as focusing on customers while ignoring production efficiency.
- **Product example:** a Lagos electronics firm keeps adding ports, a torch and a radio to its power bank because managers believe buyers always choose the most advanced model, but never asks what buyers need. That is the product concept. The slogan "good thinking, good product" in a car advertisement in Nigeria reflected the same product orientation.
- **Myopia examples:** railway managers thought users wanted **trains** rather than **transportation** and missed the threat from airlines, buses, trucks and cars. Slide-rule makers thought engineers wanted slide rules rather than **calculating capacity** and lost to pocket calculators. Such firms are "looking into a mirror when they should be looking out of the window".
- **Trap:** "widely available and low in price" = production; "highest quality" = product. Options often swap these two.

### Lesson 3.3: The selling concept and the marketing concept

**Must know**

- **Selling (sales) concept:** consumers will not buy enough of the firm's products unless it undertakes a **large-scale selling and promotion effort**. Put another way: the firm **produces what it wants** and then makes great efforts to **convince consumers** to buy.
  - Became dominant mainly because of **overproduction** and the need to **clear inventory**; it thrives where consumers need convincing because of **surplus goods** (supply exceeds demand).
  - Gained traction when firms needed to **overcome consumer resistance through persuasion**.
  - Goes hand in hand with **high-pressure selling**; the **"sales-push"** model, pushing unwanted products through the channel.
  - Typically practised with **unsought goods** (goods buyers do not normally think of buying, such as insurance or burial plots).
  - **Risky assumption:** customers coaxed into buying will like the product, or if not, will forget their disappointment and buy again.
  - **Major limitation:** focuses on **short-term sales** over long-term customer loyalty; aims at making the sale, not building relationships.
- **Marketing concept:** achieving organisational goals depends on **knowing the needs and wants of target markets** and **delivering the desired satisfactions better than competitors**.
  - Basic form: the identification and satisfaction of customers' needs and wants. **Broadened form:** doing so **more effectively and efficiently than competitors**.
  - Origin: Drucker (1954), McKitterick (1958) and Levitt (1960); all agree the purpose of business is to **create a satisfied customer**.
  - Emerged as a response to **heightened competition and evolving consumer expectations**.
  - Hinges on **aligning offerings with customer needs and desires**; success = **delivering what customers value, profitably**; customer satisfaction drives profitability.
  - Slogans: **"sense and respond"**, not "make and sell"; find the **right products for your customers**, not the right customers for your products.
  - **4 pillars:** (1) target market, (2) customer needs, (3) integrated marketing, (4) profitability.
  - **Integrated marketing:** all the firm's departments and marketing activities work together to serve the customer's interests.

| | Starting point | Focus | Means | Ends |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Selling concept | Factory | Existing products | Selling and promoting | Profits through sales volume |
| Marketing concept | Market | Customer needs | Integrated marketing | Profits through customer satisfaction |

- **Levitt's contrast:** "Selling focuses on the needs of the **seller**; marketing on the needs of the **buyer**." Selling is preoccupied with converting the product into cash; marketing with satisfying the customer through the product and everything associated with creating, delivering and consuming it.
- **Bell and Emory's 3 elements of the marketing concept:** (1) **customer orientation**, (2) **integrated effort**, (3) **profit direction** (profit, not sales volume).
- **Barksdale and Darden's 2 notions:** (1) the consumer is the focal point of all business activity; (2) **profit, rather than sales volume**, is the criterion for evaluating marketing activities.

**Explain more**

- **Selling example:** an Abuja insurer recruits a big team of agents to pitch people in markets and offices because few people seek out life insurance. Selling concept. As a long-term strategy it is weak: it may help meet short-term sales targets or survive hard times, but it fails against rivals who serve customers with products they really want.
- **Marketing example:** a restaurant near the University of Ibadan surveys students, learns they want affordable, healthy meals delivered fast, and designs its menu, prices and delivery around that, aiming to profit from repeat customers. Marketing concept.
- **Customer-driven vs customer-driving marketing:** customer-driven marketing finds out what customers want now and serves it. **Customer-driving marketing** studies customers so closely that it anticipates **latent needs** they have not yet expressed and creates products before they ask (think of how mobile money apps such as OPay and Moniepoint met needs many traders had never voiced).
- Customer orientation does not rule out stimulating needs or using aggressive selling for goods created for customers.
- **Criticisms of the marketing concept:** it shifted attention from long-term product development and quality to short-term advertising, selling and promotion; implementing it has caused organisational stress, excessive costs and high product-failure rates; and relations between business, the public and government deteriorated (**consumerism**). Most failures are blamed on **implementation**, not on the concept itself. Today it is often called **customer-centricity**.
- **Trap:** the selling concept starts at the **factory**, not the market; the marketing concept's means is **integrated marketing**, not selling and promoting. "Mass production" is never one of the four pillars.

### Lesson 3.4: The societal and holistic marketing concepts

**Must know**

- **Societal marketing concept:** the organisation's task is to determine the needs, wants and interests of customers and to satisfy them **more effectively and efficiently than competitors**, in a way that **preserves or enhances the welfare of the consumer and society**.
  - Evolved from **criticisms of the marketing concept**, chiefly that it ignores **long-run societal welfare** and overlooks conflicts between consumers' **short-run wants** and **long-run welfare**.
  - Gained prominence through growing concern over **sustainability and ethics**.
  - It **integrates ethical considerations and societal welfare** into the marketing concept.
  - **3 considerations to balance:** (1) **company profit**, (2) **consumer satisfaction (wants)**, (3) **interests of society (the public)**. In the four-way version: consumers' wants, the company's requirements, consumers' long-run interests, society's long-run interests.
  - It does **not** ask the firm to give up profit.
- **Holistic marketing concept:** "everything matters" in marketing, so a broad, integrated perspective is needed in developing, designing and carrying out marketing programmes. **4 components:**

1. **Relationship marketing:** building mutually satisfying long-term relationships with customers, suppliers, distributors and other partners to earn and keep their business.
2. **Integrated marketing:** all marketing activities and departments combined to create, communicate and deliver value.
3. **Internal marketing:** hiring, training and motivating able employees who want to serve customers well.
4. **Performance marketing** (earlier called **socially responsible marketing**): understanding the financial and non-financial returns to the business and society, including ethical, legal and environmental effects.

**Explain more**

- **Societal example:** a Nigerian soft-drink maker cuts sugar, moves to recyclable bottles and funds clearing of drains blocked by plastic, while still aiming to satisfy customers and earn a profit. Societal marketing concept.
- **Short-run wants vs long-run welfare:** a Port Harcourt fast-food chain sells cheap, tasty fried meals customers love; a critic says it satisfies short-run wants but ignores long-run health (obesity). That critic reasons from the **societal** concept. The pure marketing concept would see no problem, because customers are satisfied.
- **Trap:** "It requires the firm to give up profit for social welfare" is **false**; societal marketing balances profit with welfare.
- "Production marketing" and "mass marketing" are **not** components of holistic marketing.
- Internal marketing: a GTBank or MTN service promise means nothing unless staff are trained and motivated to keep it.

### Lesson 3.5: Market orientation and its components

**Must know**

- **Market orientation:** the organisation-wide putting into practice of the marketing concept. The firm **continuously gathers information about customers and competitors** and **coordinates all departments** to create **superior customer value**.
- **Marketing concept vs market orientation:** the marketing concept is the **philosophy**; market orientation is the **set of behaviours that puts it into practice**.
- **3 behavioural components** (Narver and Slater):

1. **Customer orientation:** understanding target buyers well enough to create superior value for them continuously.
2. **Competitor orientation:** understanding the short-term strengths and weaknesses and long-term capabilities and strategies of **current and potential** competitors.
3. **Inter-functional coordination:** the coordinated use of the company's resources **across all departments** to create superior value for target customers.

- **2 decision criteria:** (1) **long-term focus**, (2) **profitability**.
- **Information-processing view** (Kohli and Jaworski): market orientation is the organisation-wide (1) **generation** of market intelligence, (2) **dissemination** of that intelligence across departments, and (3) organisation-wide **responsiveness** to it.
- **Market-oriented definition of the business:** define the business by the customer need it serves, not by the product it makes.

**Explain more**

- **Customer orientation:** a Lagos fintech holds monthly focus groups with Balogun Market traders, tracks every complaint and redesigns its app around what users need. Other signs: measuring satisfaction regularly, researching target customers' needs, following up after sales.
- **Competitor orientation:** a Nigerian paint firm's managers meet weekly to review rivals' price changes and launches, and to assess a foreign firm planning to enter. Studying a rival's **cost structure** is competitor orientation, so it is the activity **least** associated with customer orientation.
- **Inter-functional coordination:** at an Aba shoe company, production, finance, R&D and sales staff share customer feedback and jointly plan a new school-shoe range.
- "Production orientation" and "selling orientation" are **not** components of market orientation.
- **Business definitions:** a bank that says it is in the business of "helping Nigerians move and keep money safely and cheaply" (not "operating bank branches") has a **market-oriented** definition. "We run bank branches" or "we make trains" is **product-oriented** and invites myopia.
- Market orientation does **not** replace customers with competitors as the main focus; it watches both.

### One-minute recap

- A marketing concept is the guiding philosophy that weighs the interests of the firm, customers and society.
- Five concepts: production, product, selling, marketing, societal; order of evolution production → selling → marketing → societal.
- Production = available and cheap; demand > supply; efficiency and distribution; danger is forgetting customers.
- Product = highest quality and features; technology-push; leads to marketing myopia (product, not need).
- Selling = consumers must be persuaded; overproduction, unsought goods; factory, existing products, selling and promoting, profit through volume.
- Marketing = market, customer needs, integrated marketing, profit through satisfaction; "sense and respond"; four pillars.
- Societal = marketing concept plus consumer and society welfare; balance profit, wants and public interest; profit is not given up.
- Holistic = relationship, integrated, internal and performance (socially responsible) marketing.
- Market orientation puts the marketing concept into practice: customer orientation, competitor orientation, inter-functional coordination; long-term focus and profitability.

### Theory practice

**Question:** Distinguish the selling concept from the marketing concept, and state the three behavioural components of market orientation.

*Answer outline:* The selling concept holds that consumers will not buy enough unless the firm sells and promotes aggressively: it starts at the factory, focuses on existing products, uses selling and promoting, and seeks profit through sales volume (seller's needs, inside-out). The marketing concept holds that goals are reached by knowing target customers' needs and satisfying them better than competitors: it starts with the market, focuses on customer needs, uses integrated marketing, and seeks profit through customer satisfaction (buyer's needs, outside-in). Market orientation puts the marketing concept into practice through (1) customer orientation, (2) competitor orientation and (3) inter-functional coordination, guided by long-term focus and profitability.
