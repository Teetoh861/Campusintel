## Topic 6: Marketing Mix and Product Life Cycle

**What you must know:** the meaning and importance of the marketing mix; the 4Ps (McCarthy, 1960s), the 7Ps of services and the 4Cs (1990s); product levels, classes, branding and packaging; pricing objectives, methods and strategies (skimming, penetration); distribution strategies and channels; the promotion tools; people, process, physical evidence and the features of services; the product life cycle stages, their features and strategies; market share.

### Lesson 6.1: Meaning and importance of the marketing mix

**Must know**

- **Marketing mix (simple meaning):** putting the **right product**, or a combination of products, in the **right place**, at the **right time** and at the **right price**. The difficult part is doing it well: you must know every aspect of your business plan.
- **Marketing mix (formal):** the set of **controllable marketing tools** a firm **blends** to produce the response it wants in its **target market**. It is the set of actions a firm uses to build and market its offering.
- It is a programme of marketing elements designed for a specific product in response to the marketing forces (consumer behaviour, competitors, government) that affect it. The mix is **controllable**; the environment is **uncontrollable**.
- **Three frameworks:**

| Framework | Elements | Origin |
|---|---|---|
| **4Ps** (marketing mix) | Product, Price, Place, Promotion | **E. Jerome McCarthy**, **1960s** |
| **7Ps** (services marketing mix) | 4Ps + **People, Process, Physical evidence** | Extension of the 4Ps, used mostly for services |
| **4Cs** (customer's view) | Customer solution (wants and needs), Cost, **Convenience**, Communication | Developed in the **1990s** |

- **4Ps to 4Cs:** product → customer solution; price → customer cost; **place → convenience**; promotion → communication.

**8 points of importance of the marketing mix.** It:

1. Clarifies what the offering gives customers.
2. Helps plan a successful product offering.
3. Helps plan, develop and execute effective strategies.
4. Helps leverage strengths and avoid unnecessary costs.
5. Helps the firm be proactive about risks.
6. Shows whether the offering suits customers.
7. Identifies customer requirements.
8. Shows when and how to promote.

- **Purpose:** to combine the controllable tools to respond to the target market, **balancing customer value with organisational goals**.

**Explain more**

- **Using the mix:** all 7Ps **work together**, and they also affect **segmentation, targeting and positioning** decisions. Never plan one element alone, and the mix is not fixed: it changes over the product's life.
- Traps: "4 Cs model", "4 Es" and "4 Ms" are not the name of the marketing mix; the mix is the **4 Ps framework**. People is **not** one of McCarthy's original 4Ps, and **positioning** is **not** one of the 7Ps. The 4Ps were created by McCarthy, not Kotler, Porter or Drucker.
- Example: Indomie gets the mix right with a product children like (product), a low pack price (price), presence in every kiosk (place) and catchy TV and school campaigns (promotion).

### Lesson 6.2: Product: levels, classes, branding and packaging

**Must know**

- **Product:** anything that can be offered to a market for **attention, acquisition, use or consumption** that might satisfy a want or need. It is a **bundle of tangible and intangible benefits**: a good (shoes) or a service (hotel, airline) that solves a customer need. A mix **always begins with a product** to sell.
- **Product decisions:** designing offerings that meet customer expectations: **features, quality, design, branding, packaging, labelling, support services** and planning across the product's **life cycle**.
- **Key questions:** What does the client want from the product? How, where and why does the client use it? Is it better than competitors'?

**3 product levels:**

1. **Core customer value (core benefit):** what the buyer is **really** buying, the problem solved.
2. **Actual product:** **brand name, features, design, quality level and packaging**.
3. **Augmented product:** extra services and benefits: **delivery, installation, warranty, after-sales service, credit**.

**4 classes of consumer products:**

| Class | How it is bought | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Convenience** | Frequently, immediately, little comparison or effort | Bread, sachet water, toothpaste |
| **Shopping** | Less often; buyer **compares** quality, price, style | Furniture, clothes, phones |
| **Specialty** | Strong brand preference; buyer makes a **special effort**, will not accept substitutes | A particular luxury watch or car |
| **Unsought** | Buyer does not know of it or does not normally think of buying it until a need arises | Life insurance, funeral services, blood donation |

- **Industrial (business) products:** materials and parts, capital items, supplies and services.
- **Brand:** a name, term, sign, symbol or design (or a combination) that identifies one seller's product and differentiates it from competitors. A **trademark** is a legally protected brand. **Sub-branding** creates a separate but related brand for a new segment without diluting the parent brand.
- **Packaging:** designing and producing the container or wrapper. Functions: **contain and protect, inform (label), promote, give convenience**.

**Explain more**

- Worked example of levels: a trader pays ₦850,000 for a generator in Ibadan. Keeping his shop running during power cuts is the **core benefit**; the brand, the kVA rating, design and the box are the **actual product**; free delivery, installation and a two-year warranty are the **augmented product**.
- Convenience products include **staples** (bread), **impulse** goods (sweets at the till) and **emergency** goods (umbrellas in a downpour).
- A collector in Abuja who travels to Lagos for the only authorised dealer of one watch brand treats it as a **specialty** product. The same watch could be a shopping product for someone comparing several brands: classification depends on **how the buyer buys**.
- **Product mix:** all the product lines a firm sells (Dangote sells cement, sugar and salt lines). Do not confuse it with the marketing mix.
- Branding and packaging also count as **physical evidence** (Lesson 6.5). In Nigeria, food and drug labels carry a **NAFDAC** registration number. Tamper-proof packaging was one firm response to consumer fears and legal pressure.

### Lesson 6.3: Price: objectives, methods and strategies

**Must know**

- **Price:** the amount customers pay for a product. **Price is the only element that produces revenue**; the others mainly represent costs. It determines **profit**, and changing it affects the whole strategy, sales and demand.
- **3 things price is set against:** **production cost**, **competitors' price ranges**, **customers' perceived value**. Pricing must balance **customer perceived value and competitive positioning**.
- **Price signals quality:** buyers often judge a higher-priced product as better quality.

**Pricing objectives:** survival; current profit maximisation; market-share leadership; product-quality leadership; target return on investment; meeting or matching competition.

**Pricing methods:**

1. **Cost-based (cost-plus / mark-up):** add a standard mark-up to unit cost.
   - Unit cost = variable cost + (fixed cost ÷ unit sales)
   - Mark-up on cost: price = unit cost × (1 + mark-up)
   - Mark-up on selling price: price = unit cost ÷ (1 − desired return on sales)
   - Break-even volume = fixed cost ÷ (price − variable cost)
2. **Value-based:** price set on buyers' perceived value.
3. **Competition-based (going-rate / competitive parity):** price in line with competitors' prices.

**Pricing strategies:**

| Strategy | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Price skimming** | High price at launch to earn **high margins on innovative products** in the early period, then lower step by step |
| **Penetration pricing** | **Low entry price** to win a **large market share rapidly** |
| **Competitive pricing** | Prices **in line with competitors** |
| **Psychological pricing** | ₦999 instead of ₦1,000 |
| **Prestige (premium) pricing** | Deliberately high price to signal status |

**Explain more**

- **Worked cost-plus example.** Variable cost ₦400 per unit, fixed cost ₦2,000,000, expected sales 50,000 units.
  - Unit cost = 400 + (2,000,000 ÷ 50,000) = 400 + 40 = **₦440**.
  - 25% mark-up on cost: 440 × 1.25 = **₦550**.
  - 20% return on selling price: 440 ÷ (1 − 0.20) = 440 ÷ 0.80 = **₦550**.
  - Break-even at ₦550: 2,000,000 ÷ (550 − 400) = 13,333.3, so **13,334 units** (rounded up to whole units).
- **Skimming:** a phone launched in Lagos at ₦1,200,000 for buyers who want the newest model, then cut step by step. **Penetration:** a new mobile network entering with call rates far below established networks, to win many subscribers quickly.
- **Why a newcomer cannot charge very high prices:** the brand is only starting out, so customers do not yet **recognise or trust** it; trust comes with time. Skimming suits a genuinely innovative product with buyers willing to pay, not an unknown brand of an ordinary product.
- **Perceived quality:** a shopper who assumes a ₦28,000 perfume is better than a ₦3,500 one without trying either shows that price **shapes perceived quality**. Inexperienced buyers lean on price most.
- Traps: penetration is a **pricing** strategy, not a distribution strategy. Skimming does **not** aim at mass-market penetration, and cost-plus ignores demand and competition.

### Lesson 6.4: Place and promotion

**Must know**

- **Place:** the **position and distribution** of the product so it is **accessible to the target audience**: the right location at the right time (a shop, an online store, mail order, an easy-to-navigate website). It covers **supply-chain logistics and channel management**.
- **Distribution:** moving products from the point of production to the point of consumption. **Intermediaries** (wholesalers, retailers, agents) create **place, time and possession utility**.
- **Place questions:** Where do clients look for my product? Where do clients usually shop? Should I sell online?

**4 distribution strategies:**

| Strategy | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Intensive** | As many outlets as possible | Soft drinks in every supermarket, kiosk, filling station and roadside stall |
| **Selective** | A few carefully chosen outlets per area | A home-appliance maker using chosen retailers in each city |
| **Exclusive** | One (or very few) dealers per area | A luxury car maker with one dealer in Lagos State |
| **Franchising** | Independent owners use the brand's name, recipes and systems for fees | Fast-food outlets in Port Harcourt and Enugu |

**Channel levels:** zero-level (direct: producer → consumer); one-level (producer → retailer → consumer); two-level (producer → wholesaler → retailer → consumer); three-level (adds an agent or jobber).

- **Promotion:** methods to win attention, **build awareness and persuade** the target audience, driving **brand visibility, purchase intent, sales and brand recognition**.
- **Promotion tools (promotion mix):**
  1. **Advertising:** paid, non-personal presentation through media.
  2. **Sales promotion:** short-term incentives (buy two, get one free; coupons; samples).
  3. **Personal selling** (sales organisation): face-to-face selling that **builds direct customer relationships**.
  4. **Public relations:** building goodwill and a positive image (sponsorships, press releases).
  5. **Direct and digital marketing**, and **customer service**.
- **Promotion questions:** When is the best time to promote? What strategy are competitors using? Should I use social media? How can I send messages to my audience? Which channel is best for my audience?
- The promotion choice depends on **budget, communication goals and target market**.

**Explain more**

- MTN recharge cards and Coca-Cola use **intensive** distribution; luxury car brands use **exclusive** dealers; Jumia is an online channel (place), not a promotion tool.
- A Surulere supermarket's "buy two tins of milk, get one free" for two weeks = **sales promotion**. A beverage firm sponsoring a free health walk in Ibadan and sending a press release = **public relations**.
- Traps: **franchising** is a distribution strategy, not a promotion tool; **penetration** is pricing, not distribution. "Place" in the mix is not the customer's home region and not the shop's physical surroundings (that is physical evidence).
- Services usually use a **direct channel** (bank, hotel, doctor) and widen reach through **franchising**.

### Lesson 6.5: People, process, physical evidence and services

**Must know**

- **People:** everyone tied to the offering: **customers** (is the target audience and demand large enough?) and **employees** who deliver it. Staff must be **fully trained** (customer service assistants, copywriters, designers, sales representatives).
- **Process:** the systems and steps for delivering the service. Be **easy to do business with: efficient, helpful and timely**. A good process **saves time and money** and keeps the **standard of service consistent**, building reputation and loyalty. Remove bottlenecks; use process maps.
- **Physical evidence:** **everything a customer sees or hears** when interacting with the business: **branding, packaging, physical space (shop) and even the way staff and sales representatives act and dress**. It must reflect brand values and can raise or lower sales.

**4 characteristics of services:**

1. **Intangibility:** cannot be seen or touched in advance.
2. **Inseparability:** produced and consumed at the same time; cannot be separated from the provider.
3. **Heterogeneity (variability):** quality differs from one provider or occasion to another.
4. **Perishability:** cannot be stored; unused capacity is lost forever.

- Other features: **fluctuating (seasonal) demand**; prices set by **demand and competition**; a **direct channel**, widened by franchising.

**Explain more**

- A hotel in Victoria Island training front-desk staff for two weeks strengthens **people**. A bank branch in Ikeja cutting account opening from two days to 30 minutes improves **process**. A Lekki restaurant repainting, relighting, reprinting menus and replacing its signboard changes **physical evidence**.
- Trap: training staff is **people**; how staff look and dress as the customer sees them is part of **physical evidence**. If asked what physical evidence includes among branding, packaging, shop space and staff dress, choose **all of them**.
- **Why physical evidence matters:** services are **intangible**, so customers look for **visible cues** to judge quality (a clean banking hall, smart uniforms).
- Empty seats on a half-full Lagos-Abuja flight cannot be sold tomorrow: **perishability**. Customers waiting for one particular barber in Yaba because haircuts differ: **variability**. Variability is reduced by training and standards but never removed.

### Lesson 6.6: The product life cycle and market share

**Must know**

- **Product life cycle (PLC):** the course of a product's **sales and profits over its lifetime**. Before launch there is **product development** (no sales, rising costs). After launch: **Introduction → Growth → Maturity → Decline**.

| Feature | Introduction | Growth | Maturity | Decline |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Sales | Low | Rising rapidly | **Peak** | Declining |
| Cost per customer | **Highest** | Average | Low | Low |
| Profits | Low or negative | Rising | High | Declining |
| Customers | Innovators | Early adopters | Middle majority | **Laggards** |
| Competitors | Few | Growing number | Stable, beginning to decline | Declining |
| Objective | Awareness and trial | Maximise market share | Maximise profit, defend share | Cut spending, milk the brand |
| Price | Cost-plus (or skim or penetrate) | Price to penetrate; lower prices | Match or beat competitors | Cut price |
| Distribution | Selective | Intensive | More intensive | Selective; close unprofitable outlets |
| Advertising | Awareness among **early adopters and dealers** | Awareness and interest in mass market | **Stress brand differences and benefits** | Reduce to level that keeps **hard-core loyals** |
| Sales promotion | **Heavy, to entice trial** | Reduce | **Increase to encourage brand switching** | Reduce to minimum |
| Product | Basic product | Extensions, service, warranty | Diversify brands and models | **Phase out weak items** |

- **Maturity** usually **lasts longest**. Its strategies: **modify the market** (new users, new uses, new segments), **modify the product** (quality, features, style, variants), **modify the marketing mix**.
- **Decline** is triggered by **new technology or shifts in consumer preferences** (and competition). Options: **maintain** (reposition or revitalise, find new uses or markets), **harvest** (cut R&D and advertising, milk short-run cash), **drop** (discontinue or sell). Marketers must **find new ways to increase sales again** or reinvent the product.
- **Style:** a basic, distinctive mode of expression, lasting generations. **Fashion:** a currently accepted, popular style. **Fad:** a short period of unusually high sales driven by consumer enthusiasm and immediate popularity, then a quick fade.
- **Market share** = (firm's sales ÷ total industry sales of that product type) × 100.

**Explain more**

- **Value of the PLC:** a dynamic tool to tailor strategy to each stage and to help forecast performance. It is not a fixed roadmap.
- **Spotting the stage:** a plantain-chip brand six months old, slow sales, few aware, almost no rivals, heavy awareness spending = **introduction**. A bottled-water brand going ₦10m → ₦25m → ₦60m (up 150% then 140%) while rivals enter = **growth**. A detergent ad stressing brand differences plus promotions to make rivals' customers switch = **maturity**.
- A mature tea brand pushing iced tea to young non-drinkers = **modifying the market**. A declining noodles brand cutting research and advertising to earn short-run cash = **harvesting**.
- Introduction: high costs, low sales, low or negative profits, overcoming **consumer unfamiliarity**. Growth: rising acceptance, competitors enter, so **differentiate**. Maturity: slowing growth, intense competition, sustained promotion, new variants or segments.
- Traps: relaunching an unchanged product does not return it to introduction; raising prices for exclusivity and aggressive expansion are not decline strategies.
- **Market share example:** industry sales of bottled water in a state are ₦800 million; one brand sells ₦120 million. Share = (120 ÷ 800) × 100 = **15%**. Not 85% (the rest of the market) and not 6.67% (800 ÷ 120, upside down).

### One-minute recap

- Marketing mix = controllable tools blended for the target market: right product, place, time, price.
- 4Ps by McCarthy (1960s); 7Ps add people, process, physical evidence; 4Cs (1990s): place = convenience.
- Product levels: core benefit, actual, augmented. Consumer products: convenience, shopping, specialty, unsought.
- Price is the only revenue element and signals quality. Skimming = high launch price; penetration = low price for quick share.
- Distribution: intensive, selective, exclusive, franchising. Promotion: advertising, sales promotion, personal selling, PR.
- Services: intangible, inseparable, variable, perishable; physical evidence gives visible cues.
- PLC: introduction, growth, maturity (peak, longest), decline (laggards; maintain, harvest, drop).
- Market share = firm's sales ÷ industry sales × 100.

### Theory practice

**Question:** Industry sales of packaged juice in Lagos were ₦2,500 million last year and ₦2,600 million this year. Brand A sold ₦400 million last year and ₦390 million this year. Find Brand A's market share each year, the industry growth rate, and the likely PLC stage of the category. Suggest two strategies.

*Solution:*
- Last year share = (400 ÷ 2,500) × 100 = **16%**. This year share = (390 ÷ 2,600) × 100 = **15%**.
- Industry growth = (2,600 − 2,500) ÷ 2,500 × 100 = **4%**. Brand A's own sales fell by (390 − 400) ÷ 400 × 100 = **−2.5%**.
- Sales are near their peak with only slow growth and competitors taking share, so the category is in **maturity**.
- Strategies: **modify the market** (new users or uses, e.g. juice for school lunches) and **modify the product or mix** (new flavours, smaller packs, promotions that encourage brand switching while stressing brand differences).
