## Topic 7: Market Segmentation

**What you must know:** the meaning and origin of segmentation; the nature of a good segment and preference patterns; the bases (geographic, demographic, psychographic, behavioural, plus business markets) and every variable under each; the five requirements for effective segmentation; benefits and disadvantages; targeting strategies; positioning; simple segment-size and market-share sums.

### Lesson 7.1: Meaning, origin and the nature of a segment

**Must know**

- **Market segmentation:** the process of dividing a broad market into smaller groups of customers who share meaningful characteristics, such as demographics, geography, psychographics or behaviour. Put another way, it is dividing the market of potential customers into smaller, more defined segments by shared characteristics such as demographics, interests, needs or location.
- **Market segment:** one of those groups. Its members have one or more things in common, so the marketer can craft one message for the whole group.
- **Major reason for segmenting:** to create a **custom marketing mix for each segment** and cater to it accordingly.
- **Strategic goal:** to meet diverse customer needs more precisely, so the firm can customise offerings for distinct groups and focus effort on solutions tailored to each group.
- **Origin:** the concept was introduced by **Wendell R. Smith** in his 1956 article "Product Differentiation and Market Segmentation as Alternative Marketing Strategies". Marketers have practised it since the late 1900s.
- **Opposite of segmentation:** **mass marketing** (one product, one programme, for everyone).
- **Segmentation vs product differentiation:** segmentation focuses on the **market** (the buyers); product differentiation focuses on **product differences**.

**Nature of a good segment**

1. **Internally homogeneous:** members share something the marketer can use.
2. **Externally heterogeneous:** each segment differs clearly from other segments.
3. Members **respond in a similar way** to the marketing activities aimed at them (similar features alone are not enough).

Memorise the rule: **a good segment is internally homogeneous and externally heterogeneous.**

**Preference patterns** (how buyers' preferences spread out)

| Pattern | What it looks like | What it means |
|---|---|---|
| Homogeneous | All buyers want roughly the same thing | Treat the whole market as one segment (e.g. table salt) |
| Diffused | Preferences scattered everywhere | In the extreme, each consumer is a separate segment |
| Clustered | Groups of buyers with similar preferences | Natural segments form (clusters) |

**Customer-driven marketing strategy (STP), in order:**

1. **Segmentation:** divide the market.
2. **Targeting:** choose which segment(s) to serve.
3. **Positioning:** create a clear, distinctive place in target customers' minds (then differentiate the offer to deliver it).

**Explain more**

- Segmentation makes it easier to personalise campaigns, focus on what is necessary and group similar consumers so they can be reached cost-effectively. Today its main purpose is to get more **conversions** (sales) through personalised campaigns.
- Wrong options in exams: "treating all customers the same", "ignoring consumer diversity", "standardising marketing efforts", "reducing product diversity", "escalating costs". These describe mass marketing or nonsense, never segmentation.
- "Concentrated preference pattern" is **not** one of the three patterns; it is a distractor borrowed from "concentrated marketing". Products where tastes hardly vary (salt, sugar, petrol) show homogeneous preferences; fashion clothing, perfume and cars show scattered or clustered preferences.
- Order trap: segmentation always comes first, positioning last. "Targeting, segmentation, positioning" is wrong.
- **Example:** Indomie does not sell one pack to everyone. It offers small single packs, bigger "hungry man" sizes and multipacks for families. Each pack serves a group with similar needs (internally alike) that differs from the others (externally different).

### Lesson 7.2: Bases of segmentation, demographic and geographic

**Must know**

- **Basis (base) of segmentation:** the characteristic used to split the market. The four major consumer bases: **geographic, demographic, psychographic, behavioural**.
- **Two ways to group bases:**
  - **Descriptive bases:** dimensions that simply **describe** consumers in a segment, e.g. age, income, location.
  - **Causal bases:** dimensions that explain **why** customers respond to marketing mixes, e.g. benefits sought.
- **Segmentation by consumer characteristics** uses who the consumer is: geographic, demographic and psychographic variables (age, sex, income). **Segmentation by consumer responses** uses what consumers do or want: benefits, usage, occasions, loyalty (behavioural).
- Simple bases listed one by one: **gender, age group, income, place, occupation, usage, lifestyle**.

**Demographic segmentation:** groups people by population characteristics. Variables: **age, gender (sex), marital status, family size, income, religion, race, occupation, nationality** (also education). It is the **most common** type, the one most people think of when they hear "market segmentation". Its key strength: it uses **measurable, easily available data**. It assumes buying behaviour is strongly influenced by demographics.

- **Gender:** simple but important; male and female needs differ (cosmetics, clothing, jewellery).
- **Age group:** most products do not suit all ages; age targeting improves strategy and conversion.
- **Income:** decides **purchasing power** and whether to market the product as a **need, a want or a luxury**. Three groups: **high income, mid income, low income** (the split varies with product, use and area).
- **Occupation:** like income, it shapes purchases; a need for an entrepreneur may be a luxury for a civil servant.
- **Generational segmentation:** grouping by period of birth (Generation Z, millennials, Generation X, baby boomers).
- **Life stage segmentation:** grouping by where people are in life now: finished school or not, earning or not, married or not, children (and their ages), home owner or not.
- Generational and life stage segmentation are a **subset of demographic** segmentation.

**Geographic segmentation:** groups people by the region where they live or work (country, state, city, neighbourhood). It is the base most sensitive to **regional preferences**: use it when needs, tastes or climates differ by region. Its **6 variables:**

1. **Location:** solely where customers live or work, ignoring language or culture.
2. **Time zone.**
3. **Climate and season.**
4. **Cultural preferences.**
5. **Language** (including slang and colloquialisms).
6. **Urbanicity:** how urban an area is (urban, suburban, rural).

**Business (industrial) markets** are segmented by variables such as **industry type, company size and location**, plus operating variables (technology, user status, customer capabilities), purchasing approaches, situational factors (urgency, order size) and personal characteristics of buyers.

**Explain more**

- **Millennials with disposable income** = demographic (age generation plus income). **Coastal residents** = geographic.
- **Income example:** a car maker treats a Toyota Corolla as a need for mid-income buyers and a Lexus as a luxury for high-income buyers.
- **Occupation example:** a heavy-duty phone power bank may be a necessity for a self-employed trader in Onitsha who is out all day, but a luxury for a salaried civil servant who sits near a socket.
- **Generational example:** a social media app such as Snapchat markets itself to Generation Z rather than Generation X.
- **Life stage example:** two 30-year-old men grew up in the same Ibadan neighbourhood. One is married with a child; the other is single and lives with his parents. Same generation, different needs: a marketer who separates them is using life stage segmentation. Combining generation and life stage gives deeper insight.
- **Climate and season:** a clothing retailer pushes raincoats and umbrellas in Port Harcourt in the rainy season and warm wear in Jos during harmattan. Bottled water sells strongly where water is scarce and weakly where it is plentiful.
- **Cultural preferences:** drinking beer in the open is almost normal in Lagos but may be frowned upon in parts of Northern Nigeria, so a beer brand markets differently in each. Nigeria's Yoruba, Hausa and Igbo sub-cultures can be treated as distinct segments.
- **Urbanicity:** a dealer promotes small saloon cars in cities and pick-up trucks that handle rough roads and haul farm inputs in rural areas.
- **Place (geographic):** a snack brand sells a big ₦4,500 party pack in cities where teenagers buy snacks for parties and small ₦200 packs in rural areas where people spend little on snacks.
- **Trap:** "spending habits" and "customer journey stage" are behavioural, not geographic. "Personality" is psychographic, not demographic.

### Lesson 7.3: Psychographic and behavioural segmentation

**Must know**

**Psychographic segmentation:** groups people by psychological traits: **personality, lifestyle and attitude** (also values, interests, opinions). It uncovers **intangible** factors and explains consumer motivations, so it is very useful for understanding **why** consumers choose certain brands. Its **6 variables:**

1. **Inner beliefs.**
2. **Likes and dislikes.**
3. **Lifestyle.**
4. **Attitudes and values.**
5. **Priorities and motivations.**
6. **Personality.**

- **Personality:** the combination of characteristics forming a person's distinctive character (habits, traits, attitude, temperament): introverted, extroverted, sociable, optimistic, creative, emotional.
- **Lifestyle:** how a person lives; measured by **activities, interests and opinions (AIO)**. Lifestyle also covers marital status, hobbies, religion and values (but asked on their own, religion and marital status are demographic variables).
- **Social status:** how highly society views a person; people buy to enhance it.
- **Attitudes:** people's perceptions of the world, usually shaped by upbringing and cultural and religious background.

**Behavioural segmentation:** groups people by how they behave towards the product or business: **usage, loyalty, purchase occasions**, preference, choices, decision making, product knowledge. It is reported as the **most effective** method: **91%** of marketers said so (2019 Marketing Week survey). Its **6 variables:**

1. **Spending habits.**
2. **Purchasing habits.**
3. **Brand interactions.**
4. **Customer journey stage.**
5. **User status.**
6. **Benefits sought.**

- **Purchasing habits, 4 groups:**
  1. **Complex buyers:** do not yet know which product or brand to buy; still researching.
  2. **Variety-seeking:** happy with their current brand but open to trying other brands and varieties.
  3. **Dissonance-reducing:** happy with their current brand but think other brands could offer better.
  4. **Habitual:** loyal to their brand and unlikely to switch.
- **User status:** those who know or do not know the product, **ex-users, potential users, current users, first-time users**.
- **Usage rate:** **heavy, medium, light** users.
- **Loyalty status:** **brand loyal, brand-neutral, competitor loyal**.
- **Benefit segmentation:** segments formed by **what consumers want from the product** (the benefit sought).
- Other behavioural variables: occasions (when they buy or use), buyer readiness, attitude towards the product.

**Explain more**

- **Personality:** an events company tells introverts the concert lets them choose quiet activities, and tells extroverts it is full of socialising. Same event, two personality segments.
- **Lifestyle:** an online sneaker store in Lagos runs one campaign for joggers and gym users and another for buyers who want sneakers as a fashion statement.
- **Social status:** a five-star hotel in Abuja sells luxury and quality service; a budget hotel in the same city sells affordability.
- **Attitudes:** some gym users train to avoid health problems; others train for a sense of accomplishment.
- **Lifestyle example:** a person with a lavish lifestyle sees an air conditioner in every room as a need; a conservative person in the same city sees it as a luxury.
- **Purchasing habits:** Ada is buying her first laptop, does not know which brand, and is reading reviews and asking friends: a **complex buyer**. "Impulse buyers" is **not** one of the four groups.
- **User status:** MTN or Airtel can send ex-users a "come back" bonus, first-time users a welcome bundle and current users a loyalty reward.
- **Usage rate:** an athlete is a heavy user of an energy drink; a non-sporty person who buys it for the taste is a light or medium user.
- **Behavioural targets:** frequent flyers (usage), brand-loyal customers for retention campaigns (loyalty). Benefit segmentation for toothpaste: one group wants white teeth, another wants cavity protection, another wants sensitivity relief.
- **Trap:** "values and lifestyles" or "attitudes, interests and opinions" means psychographic. "How heavily they use the product", "purchase frequency" and "loyalty" mean behavioural. Behavioural looks at what people **do**; psychographic looks at who they **are inside**.

### Lesson 7.4: Requirements, benefits and disadvantages, and segment sums

**Must know**

**5 requirements for effective segmentation** (segments must be):

1. **Measurable:** the size, purchasing power and profiles of the segment can be measured.
2. **Accessible:** the segment can be effectively reached and served.
3. **Substantial:** the segment is large and/or profitable enough to serve.
4. **Differentiable:** segments are conceptually distinguishable and respond differently to different marketing mix elements and programmes.
5. **Actionable:** effective programmes can be formulated for attracting and serving the segment.

"Ability" and "inexpensive" are **not** requirements.

**Benefits of market segmentation:**

1. **Better matching of customer needs.**
2. **Identification of gaps in the market.**
3. **Increased ROI** (less wasted spending, more repeat sales, referrals and word of mouth).
4. **Customer retention.**
5. **Increased market share.**

Also: better allocation of resources (directing them to high-potential segments), greater customer satisfaction, better spotting of opportunities, lower costs, higher conversions, more specific planning, and reaching niche users through smaller segments.

**Disadvantages (limitations):**

1. **Extensive research and development** is needed, which some businesses cannot afford.
2. **Expensive process** in both time and money.

**Formulas**

- Segment size = Total market (buyers or units) x Segment percentage
- Market share (%) = Firm's sales / Total market sales x 100

**Explain more**

- **Failing requirements:** left-handed golfers in Owerri, too few to be profitable, fail **substantial**. A large, profitable group that no media or channel the firm uses can reach fails **accessible**. A small airline with seven attractive segments but too few staff and too little money to run seven programmes fails **actionable**. Two "segments" that react the same way to every offer fail **differentiable**.
- **Gap example:** after segmenting the Lagos food market, a start-up finds nobody serves healthy ready-to-eat lunches for night-shift workers and launches for them: identification of gaps in the market.
- **Not benefits:** "using one identical marketing mix for everyone" and "removing the need for market research". Segmentation needs **more** research, not less.

**Worked example 1: segment size**
A study finds 200,000 potential buyers of a new study app in a city; 35% are aged 16 to 24.
- Segment size = 200,000 x 35% = 200,000 x 0.35 = **70,000 buyers**.
- Trap answers: 7,000 (decimal slip), 130,000 (the other 65%).

**Worked example 2: market share**
A town buys 300,000 sachet-water units in a month; one brand sells 45,000.
- Market share = 45,000 / 300,000 x 100 = **15%**. Competitors together hold 255,000 units (85%).

**Worked example 3: is the segment substantial?**
Of those 70,000 young buyers, 20% are expected to pay ₦2,000 a year.
- Buyers = 70,000 x 0.20 = 14,000. Revenue = 14,000 x ₦2,000 = **₦28,000,000** a year: substantial.
- The golf segment: 120 buyers x ₦50,000 = **₦6,000,000**, against ₦15,000,000 needed to serve them: not substantial.

### Lesson 7.5: Targeting strategies

**Must know**

- **Target market:** a set of buyers sharing common needs or characteristics that a company decides to serve.
- **Market targeting:** evaluating each segment's attractiveness and selecting one or more to enter. Evaluate segments on (1) **size and growth**, (2) **structural attractiveness** (competitors, substitutes, buyer and supplier power), (3) **company objectives and resources**.

| Strategy | What the firm does | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Undifferentiated (mass) | Ignores segment differences; one offer, one programme for the whole market | A salt producer selling one product nationwide |
| Differentiated (segmented) | Targets several segments with a separate offer and programme for each | A drinks firm with a low-sugar drink, an energy drink and a malt drink, each with its own ads |
| Concentrated (niche) | Goes after a large share of one (or a few) small segments; suits limited resources | A small Lagos firm making only handmade shoes for very large feet |
| Micromarketing | Tailors products and programmes to specific individuals and local customer groups | See below |

**Micromarketing, 2 forms:**

1. **Local marketing:** tailoring to cities, neighbourhoods or specific stores.
2. **Individual (one-to-one, customised) marketing:** tailoring to one customer's needs and preferences.

**Factors in choosing a strategy:** company resources, product variability, product life-cycle stage, market variability, competitors' strategies.

**Explain more**

- The strategies run from broad to narrow: undifferentiated, then differentiated, then concentrated, then micromarketing.
- **Undifferentiated** keeps costs low but struggles where tastes differ. It suits homogeneous products such as salt or sugar.
- **Differentiated** usually raises total sales but also costs (separate products, research, advertising). Nestlé Nigeria (Milo, Golden Morn, Maggi) and Dangote's different pack sizes of sugar and salt serve different segments.
- **Concentrated** gives deep knowledge of the niche and a strong position, but it is risky if that one segment shrinks or a big rival enters.
- **Local marketing:** a supermarket chain stocking different goods in its Lekki and Ikorodu branches. **Individual marketing:** a tailor in Abeokuta who takes each customer's measurements and sews to that one order.

### Lesson 7.6: Positioning

**Must know**

- **Positioning:** arranging for a product to occupy a **clear, distinctive and desirable place** relative to competing products in the minds of target consumers.
- **Product position:** the way consumers define the product on important attributes, compared with competitors.
- **Differentiation:** actually making the offer different (product, services, channels, people, image) so the firm can deliver the position it claims.
- **Value proposition:** the full mix of benefits on which a brand is positioned.
- **Perceptual (positioning) map:** a chart of how consumers see competing brands on key buying attributes such as price and quality.
- **Positioning statement:** To (target segment and need), our (brand) is (concept) that (point of difference).

**Explain more**

- Positioning is the **last** step of STP; the marketing mix must then deliver it (product, price, place and promotion all support one position).
- **Examples:** GTBank positions on modern, convenient banking; Dangote Cement on strength and reliability for builders; Jumia on wide choice and home delivery. Each place is fixed relative to rivals, in buyers' minds.
- **Trap:** positioning is about the **mind** of the target customer. Choosing which group to serve is targeting; splitting the market is segmentation; the 4Ps are the marketing mix.
- **Industry examples of STP:** a beauty brand ran an "Age Defying" range for mature adults and a "Clearly Clean" range for teens and young adults (age). Fast-food chains give toys with kids' meals but promise fast service and free Wi-Fi to working adults. Sports brands such as Nike and Adidas segment by the sport played.

### One-minute recap

- Segmentation divides a broad market into groups with shared characteristics, so each gets its own marketing mix; opposite = mass marketing; Wendell Smith, 1956.
- Good segment: internally homogeneous, externally heterogeneous; members respond alike. Preference patterns: homogeneous, diffused, clustered.
- STP order: segmentation, targeting, positioning.
- Demographic (most common; age, sex, income, occupation, religion; generational and life stage are subsets). Geographic: location, time zone, climate and season, cultural preferences, language, urbanicity.
- Psychographic: personality, lifestyle (AIO), attitudes, values, social status. Behavioural (91% say most effective): spending and purchasing habits, brand interactions, journey stage, user status, benefits sought, usage rate, loyalty.
- Descriptive bases describe; causal bases explain why. Business markets: industry type, company size, location.
- Requirements: measurable, accessible, substantial, differentiable, actionable.
- Benefits: needs matched, gaps found, ROI, retention, market share. Disadvantages: extensive research, expensive.
- Targeting: undifferentiated, differentiated, concentrated, micromarketing (local, individual). Positioning = a clear, distinctive, desirable place in consumers' minds.
- Segment size = total x %; market share = firm's sales / market sales x 100.

### Theory practice

**Question:** A noodle maker finds 480,000 noodle buyers in Ibadan, of whom 25% are students. It sells to 18,000 students. (a) How large is the student segment? (b) What share of that segment does the firm hold? (c) Which segmentation base is "students" and which targeting strategy would the firm use if it served only this group?

*Solution:*
- (a) Segment size = 480,000 x 0.25 = **120,000 students**.
- (b) Share of segment = 18,000 / 120,000 x 100 = **15%**.
- (c) Students is a **demographic** base (occupation and age or life stage). Serving only this one segment is **concentrated (niche) marketing**.
