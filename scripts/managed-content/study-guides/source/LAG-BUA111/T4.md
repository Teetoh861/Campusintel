## Topic 4: Marketing Environment

**What you must know:** the meaning of the marketing environment and its five features; internal versus external, micro versus macro; every micro force (company, suppliers, intermediaries, customers, competitors, publics) with its sub-types; the six macro forces and their factors; PESTLE, SWOT and environmental scanning; why studying the environment matters; strategic windows and megamarketing.

### Lesson 4.1: Meaning, types and features of the marketing environment

**Must know**

- **Marketing environment:** all the internal and external factors that directly or indirectly influence an organisation's decisions on its marketing activities. Put more simply: the factors and forces that influence the marketing function of an organisation. It is the mix of forces that affect a firm's ability to build relationships with and serve its customers.
- **Internal environment:** the internal forces and factors that influence the marketing function. Internal factors are **within the organisation's control** (e.g. its own strengths, weaknesses and competencies).
- **External environment:** the external forces and factors that influence the marketing function. External factors are **not within its control** (e.g. government, technology, economy, society, competitors).
- The environment is usually divided into **2 main types:** the **micro environment** and the **macro environment**.

| | Micro environment | Macro environment |
|---|---|---|
| Other names | Task environment; immediate environment | Broad environment |
| Meaning | Forces closely linked to the firm that **directly** affect its activities | Larger societal forces, **beyond the firm's control**, that affect it (and all actors in its immediate environment) to a significant extent and are subject to constant change |
| Members | Company, suppliers, marketing intermediaries, customers, competitors, publics | Demographic, economic, natural, technological, political-legal, socio-cultural |

**5 features of the marketing environment:**

1. **Specific and general forces:** specific forces **directly** affect the organisation (e.g. customers, investors); general forces affect it **indirectly** (e.g. social, political, legal, technological factors).
2. **Complexity:** the environment contains many factors, conditions and influences, and their **interaction** makes it complex.
3. **Vibrancy:** the environment is **dynamic**; it does not stay stable but **changes over time**. Marketers can control some forces but never all.
4. **Uncertainty:** market forces are **unpredictable**; some changes are hard to predict (e.g. tastes in clothes).
5. **Relativity:** explains why **demand differs** from country to country, region to region or culture to culture.

**Explain more**

- "Stability" is **not** a feature; the environment is the opposite of stable. That is exactly why it demands **constant monitoring**: so the firm can adapt to unpredictable shifts in external and internal conditions, rather than keep static strategies.
- **Uncertainty** example: a Lagos fashion house sells out an ankara style one month and cannot move it the next. A fashion may last a few days or a few years.
- **Relativity** example: aso-oke caps are always in demand in south-west Nigeria because they are traditional dress, yet demand abroad may be almost zero.
- **Vibrancy** sounds like "lively"; in the exam it means **dynamic, always changing**. Do not confuse it with complexity (many interacting factors).
- A complete environmental analysis must cover **both micro** (e.g. suppliers) **and macro** (e.g. economic trends) forces, not customers or competitors alone.
- Marketers monitor the environment to predict changes, because changes create **threats and opportunities**, and plans are modified to match.

### Lesson 4.2: The internal environment and the supply side of the micro environment

**Must know**

- **Internal environment (the company):** the firm's own departments (top management, finance, research and development (R&D), purchasing, operations/production, personnel/HR, accounting) whose relationships affect marketing decisions. Marketing plans need **top management approval** before they are implemented.
- **4 organic business functions:** marketing, personnel, production, finance.
- **Five Ms** of the internal environment: **Men, Money, Machinery, Materials, Markets.** Internal forces are **controllable** and can be adjusted to external change.
- The micro environment has a **supply side** (suppliers, marketing intermediaries, competitors) and a **demand side** (customers).
- **Suppliers:** other firms and individuals that provide the resources (raw materials and other inputs) a company needs to produce its goods and services.
- **Make-or-buy decision:** the purchasing department's decision on which resources to make itself and which to buy from outside. **3 options:** (i) make everything and assemble or process it; (ii) buy everything and confine the firm to assembly; (iii) make the complicated parts and buy the less complicated ones.
- **Marketing intermediaries:** independent businesses that perform the activities needed to move products from manufacturers to ultimate consumers; they link the firm with customers by **promoting, selling and distributing** its products. They include wholesalers, retailers, agents and distributors.

**4 types of marketing intermediaries:**

1. **Resellers:** buy from the firm and sell to customers (wholesalers, retailers).
2. **Distribution centres:** store goods (e.g. a warehouse).
3. **Marketing agencies:** promote the products by making customers aware of their benefits (e.g. an advertising agency).
4. **Financial intermediaries:** finance or insure business transactions (banks, credit organisations, insurance companies).

**5 utilities created by intermediaries (middlemen):**

| Utility | How it is created |
|---|---|
| Place | **Stocking** products where customers are located |
| Time | Opening **long hours** so customers buy at their convenience |
| Quantity | Making products available in **quantities customers can afford** |
| Assortment | **Gathering different products at one point** so customers buy them on one shopping trip |
| Possession | **Transferring** products to customers through successful transactions |

**Explain more**

- Suppliers affect **profit**, because the price of raw materials determines the final price. Firms monitor suppliers regularly for **supply shortages** and **input price changes**, and check reliability, delivery, stock availability, credit facilities and servicing.
- **Worked example (supplier force):** a Lagos bakery pays ₦60,000 for a bag of flour. Its miller raises the price by 30%. Increase = 30% × ₦60,000 = ₦18,000. New price = ₦60,000 + ₦18,000 = ₦78,000 a bag. The bakery raises the price of its bread. The force at work is the **supplier**.
- Car assemblers buy windscreens, tyres, headlamps and steel sheet from many suppliers; those suppliers in turn have their own suppliers. Firms also buy specialist marketing services (advertising, consulting, market research) from outside.
- Intermediary examples: a Kano warehouse company storing a beverage maker's cartons = **distribution centre**; an Ikeja agency making radio jingles and billboards = **marketing agency**; a bank financing a distributor's stock = **financial intermediary**. A raw-material supplier is **not** an intermediary.
- Trap: in the supply chain the firm collaborates most closely with **suppliers and intermediaries**.
- Some lists call agencies, research firms, banks, insurers, transport companies and brokers the firm's **partners**: separate entities doing business with it.
- Channels change slowly but constantly, like the hour hand of a watch; the firm must manage and satisfy its intermediaries.

### Lesson 4.3: Customers, competitors and publics (the rest of the micro environment)

**Must know**

- **Customers:** those who buy the organisation's products for final consumption; their **satisfaction is the organisation's main goal**. They form the **demand side** of the micro environment. Firms do R&D to analyse customer needs and make products to match.

**5 types of customer markets:**

1. **Consumer markets:** individuals and households buying for personal consumption.
2. **Industrial (business) markets:** organisations buying goods and services to produce other products and services, for profit or other objectives.
3. **Reseller markets:** organisations buying goods and services to resell at a profit.
4. **Government and nonprofit markets:** agencies buying to produce public services or to transfer goods to those who need them.
5. **International markets:** buyers abroad (foreign consumers, producers, resellers, governments).

**4 types of organisational buyers:**

1. **Industrial buyers:** procure products or services for use in manufacturing other products or for operating their own businesses.
2. **Resellers:** intermediaries that buy products in order to resell them.
3. **Government agencies:** Federal, State and local government units that procure products and services to perform their social functions.
4. **Institutional buyers:** buy for organisations that render services, such as museums, hospitals, schools and churches.

- **Business-to-business (B2B) marketers:** marketers who sell to business organisations rather than to individual consumers (B2C).
- **Competition:** a situation in which various organisations offer similar products and try to gain market share by adopting different marketing strategies. Competitors push a firm to **differentiate** its product to maintain its position in the market.
- **Intratype competition:** between organisations that engage in the **same basic activity**.
- **Intertype competition:** between **different types** of organisations.
- **Public:** any group that has an actual or potential interest in, or impact on, an organisation's ability to achieve its objectives (serve its customers).

**7 publics:** financial (banks, investors), media (newspapers, radio, TV, blogs), government, citizen-action (consumer and environmental groups), local (community), general public, internal (employees, managers, board).

**Explain more**

- Trap: "market segments" is **not** a type of customer market; "individual buyers" are **not** organisational buyers (they are consumers).
- Intratype examples: Coca-Cola's bottler against Seven-Up Bottling Company for soft-drink buyers; First Bank, UBA, Zenith and GTBank against each other for savings and loans. Intertype examples: finance houses or fintech apps against banks for savings customers.
- **Competitors** are the micro force that most directly challenges a firm's strategy (rivals' pricing and innovation tactics), and **competitor pricing and positioning** is the external factor that most directly affects a firm's own pricing.
- Media public example: newspapers, radio stations and online blogs writing about a Lagos bank. Financial public: the bank's shareholders and lenders.

### Lesson 4.4: The macro environment

**Must know**

**6 macro forces:** demographic, economic, natural (physical), technological, political-legal, socio-cultural. Suppliers, intermediaries, customers, competitors and the company are **micro**, never macro. Some lists add the **international situation**.

1. **Demographic:** the scientific study of human population by age, gender, education, occupation, income, location, size, density, race, marriage and death rates (the **demographic variables**). Also covers the rising role of women. It explains variation in tastes and buying patterns. Prices are **not** demographic.
2. **Economic:** affects the firm's **cost structure** and customers' **purchasing power**. Purchasing power depends on **current income, prices of products, savings and availability of credit**. Factors:
    - **Inflation:** affects demand (higher petrol prices lead to a fall in demand for cars).
    - **Interest rates:** determine borrowing; higher rates may make firms cut important activities.
    - **Unemployment:** a no-income state that reduces purchasing power.
    - **Customer income:** changes spending patterns (e.g. on food and clothing).
    - **Monetary policy:** stabilises the economy by controlling **interest rates and money supply**. **Fiscal policy:** government **spending**, financed by revenue from **taxes**.
    - Others: economic growth (GDP, GNP), exchange rates, employment level, raw-material scarcity, energy costs, import duties, balance of trade, income distribution, savings, subsidies. Ethnic structure is **not** economic.
3. **Natural (physical):** natural resources used as raw materials, plus natural conditions. Factors: **natural resources** (depletion leads some firms to **de-marketing**), **weather**, **pollution** (air, water, noise). Also climate, floods, extreme heat, natural disasters, access to water.
4. **Technological:** a rapidly changing force creating new opportunities and threats. **3 trends:** **pace of technological change** (fast change makes products obsolete quickly, so firms modify products often); **research and development** (raises growth opportunities); **increased regulation** (government guidelines that ban unsafe products, which marketers must know and obey).
5. **Political and legal:** legal bodies and government agencies that influence and limit organisations and individuals, plus laws, policies and pressure groups. **2 kinds of law:** **anti-pollution laws** (affect production) and **customer (consumer) legislation** (protects customers' interests).
6. **Socio-cultural:** society's basic **values, attitudes, perceptions and behaviour**, lifestyles, beliefs and culture. It shapes what people buy, which brand, and when.

**Explain more**

- **Demographic:** a telecoms firm sees that a very large share of Nigerians are under 30 and launches cheap student data bundles. Nigeria's large population makes it one of the biggest single markets in Africa.
- **Economic:** during a recession, watch economic conditions affecting purchasing power. Nigeria shows developing-economy traits: low incomes, low literacy and technology know-how, high unemployment, inadequate roads, power and telecoms. A weak naira raises the cost of imported raw materials, causing **low capacity utilisation**. Poor infrastructure is a threat (traffic, higher costs) but also an opportunity for firms supplying it.
- **Worked example (interest rates):** a Nnewi auto-parts maker owes ₦50,000,000. At 20% a year, interest = 0.20 × ₦50,000,000 = ₦10,000,000. At 28%, interest = 0.28 × ₦50,000,000 = ₦14,000,000. Extra yearly cost = ₦4,000,000, so it postpones expansion. In Nigeria, monetary policy is set by the **Central Bank of Nigeria** (e.g. its policy rate); raising VAT to spend more on roads is **fiscal** policy.
- **Natural:** harmattan raises sales of body lotion, lip balm and petroleum jelly; the rainy season raises umbrella sales; hot months raise fans and cold drinks. Plastic waste blocking Lagos drains pushes supermarkets towards paper bags. **De-marketing:** a petroleum marketer urging the public to use less of its own fuel.
- **Technology:** smartphones and instant transfers let a Yaba vendor sell on Instagram and take transfers instead of cash. Technology can be a constraint when equipment is lacking; firms that ignore it struggle to survive. In Nigeria, drugs and foods must be registered with **NAFDAC**, and product standards are set by the **Standards Organisation of Nigeria (SON)**.
- **Political-legal (Nigeria):** the **Federal Competition and Consumer Protection Act 2018** (enforced by the **FCCPC**) protects consumers; **ARCON** (formerly APCON) regulates advertising; **NESREA** enforces environmental standards; tobacco packs must carry a health warning. Lagos State banned styrofoam food packs, forcing eateries to switch packaging. Government also **facilitates**: tax incentives for growth industries, development-bank loans, research institutes such as FIIRO. Political stability attracts business; instability hurts the economy.
- **Socio-cultural:** with husbands and wives both working full-time, families have less time to shop, so supermarkets and malls with everything under one roof grow. Nigerian values include respect for age and extended-family responsibility; Yoruba, Hausa and Igbo **sub-cultures** can be treated as segments. Values also cover attitudes to health and nutrition, materialism, self-expression, product safety and ecology.
- **International situation:** easier shipping and communication widen markets, but trade barriers (quotas, duties) and different economies, cultures and politics add risk.

### Lesson 4.5: PESTLE, SWOT, environmental scanning and why the environment matters

**Must know**

- **PESTLE:** a checklist for the macro environment: **P**olitical, **E**conomic, **S**ocial, **T**echnological, **L**egal, **E**nvironmental (natural). PEST is the shorter version.
- **SWOT:** **S**trengths and **W**eaknesses (internal, controllable); **O**pportunities and **T**hreats (external).
- **Environmental scanning:** the systematic process of gathering and monitoring information about a company's external events and relationships, to detect trends, opportunities and threats early enough to respond and to help top management decide its future course.
- **3 phases of scanning:** data gathering; environmental interpretation (analysis); strategy formulation.
- **Sources:** company reports, industry reports, magazines, newspapers, government reports, marketing-intelligence reports, personal networking (e.g. trade fairs).
- **Controllable variables:** the internal factors and the marketing mix (the firm's own **product, price, place, promotion**). **Uncontrollable:** macro forces such as inflation, population growth and competitors' decisions.
- **Strategic window:** a short period in which a company must adjust its strategic marketing approach to new market realities, or quit the market if it fails to adjust. **4 changes create it:** new primary demand (growth in the overall market); new technology; market redefinition through competitive activity; changes in distribution channels.
- **Megamarketing:** the use of **power and public relations** (also lobbying and lawsuits) to penetrate **blocked or protected markets**.

**3 importances of studying the marketing environment:**

1. **Identification of opportunities** (e.g. exploiting customer preference with discounts).
2. **Identification of threats** (warning signals before it is too late).
3. **Managing changes** (adapting to survive in the long run).

**Environmental analysis also helps the marketer to:** know the changes; gain quality information for strategy; understand market needs and modify products; handle government-legal-regulatory policies; allocate resources and diversify; identify threats (new competitors, price wars, rival products); identify opportunities (new markets, mergers, joint ventures, alliances, a vacuum left by an exiting competitor); identify weaknesses (low-quality goods, lack of marketing expertise, no unique products) and turn them into strengths; identify and exploit strengths. It also aids planning, understanding customers and competitors, and **first-mover advantage**.

**Explain more**

- Threat: a juice maker hears a foreign multinational is entering and prepares price cuts and heavy promotion. Opportunity: a rival paint brand exits and a local maker wins its retailers. Weakness: an Ibadan cosmetics firm finds poor packaging and no marketing expertise.
- "Eliminating all competition" is **never** a benefit of environmental study.
- Environmental analysis comes first in the situation analysis, so that **positioning** (target-market choice) and the **marketing mix** can be adjusted. Example: more working mothers make day-care a more attractive segment.
- Megamarketing is not guaranteed: it is costly, decided by top management and uncertain. Do not confuse it with **demarketing** (reducing demand) or **synchromarketing** (smoothing irregular demand).

### One-minute recap

- Marketing environment = all internal and external factors that directly or indirectly influence marketing decisions; internal is controllable, external is not.
- 5 features: specific and general forces, complexity, vibrancy (dynamic), uncertainty (unpredictable), relativity (demand differs by place).
- Micro (immediate, direct): company, suppliers, intermediaries, customers, competitors, publics; supply side vs demand side (customers).
- 4 intermediaries: resellers, distribution centres, marketing agencies, financial intermediaries; 5 utilities: place, time, quantity, assortment, possession.
- 5 customer markets; 4 organisational buyers (industrial, resellers, government, institutional); intratype vs intertype competition; make-or-buy.
- Macro (broad, uncontrollable): demographic, economic, natural, technological, political-legal, socio-cultural.
- Purchasing power = income, prices, savings, credit; monetary = interest rates and money supply; fiscal = spending and taxes.
- PESTLE, SWOT, scanning (3 phases); importance: opportunities, threats, managing change.
- Strategic window = short time to adjust or quit; megamarketing = power and PR to enter blocked markets.

### Theory practice

**Question:** Distinguish the micro environment from the macro environment and give three forces in each.

*Answer outline:* The micro (task) environment is closely linked to the firm and directly affects its activities: suppliers (provide inputs), marketing intermediaries (promote, sell, distribute), customers (buy for final consumption) and also competitors and publics. The macro (broad) environment is the larger set of societal forces beyond the firm's control that change constantly and bring opportunities and threats: demographic (population variables), economic (cost structure and purchasing power), technological (pace of change, R&D, regulation), plus natural, political-legal and socio-cultural.
