## Topic 5: Consumer and Organisational Buying Behaviour

**What you must know:** the meaning of buying behaviour; the six stages of the consumer buying decision process and cognitive dissonance; involvement, perceived risk and the types of buying behaviour; the three categories of factors (personal, psychological, social) and everything inside each; family buying roles and reference groups; organisational markets, the buying centre, the three buy classes and the buy phases.

### Lesson 5.1: Meaning of buying behaviour and the buying decision process

**Must know**

- **Buying behaviour:** the decisions and actions of people involved in buying and using products.
- **Consumer buying behaviour:** the buying behaviour of the **ultimate (final) consumer**, who buys for personal or household use.
- **Consumer behaviour as a field** is multidisciplinary: it covers the **psychological, social and cultural influences** on purchase decisions.
- **Why study it:** studying what, where, when and how people buy lets marketers **predict how buyers are likely to respond to marketing stimuli** and build a marketing mix that gives customers satisfaction (utility).

**The 6 stages of the consumer buying decision process (complex decision):**

1. **Problem recognition** (need awareness): the buyer becomes aware of a **gap between a desired state and an actual state**. Always the **first** step.
2. **Information search:** **internal** (the buyer's own memory of past purchases) and **external** (word of mouth from friends and relatives, marketer-controlled sources such as adverts and salespeople, comparison shopping, public sources such as consumer reports). The result is a short list of alternatives called the **evoked set** (consideration set).
3. **Evaluation of alternatives:** the buyer sets **criteria** (wanted and unwanted features), then **ranks or weights** the options, or goes back to search.
4. **Purchase decision:** choosing the alternative: product, package, store, method of payment.
5. **Purchase:** the actual buying. It **can differ** from the decision (time gap, out of stock).
6. **Post-purchase evaluation:** outcome is **satisfaction or dissatisfaction**. Always the **last** step.

- **Cognitive dissonance:** doubt felt by a buyer, **after** a purchase, about whether the right choice was made. Reduced by **warranties, after-sales communication**, and channels for customers to contact the firm.
- A shorter **four-step** version of the complex process: (1) problem recognition, (2) information search and brand evaluation, (3) product choice and purchase, (4) post-purchase evaluation (satisfaction or dissatisfaction). First and last steps are the same in both versions.

**Explain more**

- Memory hook: **Problem, Search, Evaluate, Decide, Buy, Review**. Traps swap "purchase decision" and "purchase", or put evaluation before search.
- **Marketers can trigger problem recognition.** Tunde had not thought about sneakers until a billboard for a new design made him notice his old pair was worn out.
- **Internal versus external search:** Amaka recalling how last year's hair dryer performed is **internal** search. Asking friends, reading Jumia reviews or touring Computer Village is **external**; memory is **never** external.
- **Evoked set:** a student who ends up seriously considering only Tecno, Infinix and Samsung has those three as the evoked set. Marketers influence evaluation by "framing" alternatives (stressing the criteria their brand wins on).
- **Decision versus purchase:** Funmi chose one phone brand, found it out of stock, and bought another.
- **Not every process ends in a purchase**, and only complex (extensive) decisions pass through all six stages.
- **Dissonance:** a new-car buyer who fears the rival model was better is reassured by the dealer's warranty, thank-you call and helpline. Removing contact channels makes dissonance **worse**.

### Lesson 5.2: Involvement, perceived risk and types of buying behaviour

**Must know**

- **Level of involvement:** the **importance and intensity of interest** in a product in a particular situation. It **determines the type of buying behaviour**.
- **High-involvement purchases:** purchases that are **important** to the consumer: high-priced, risky or publicly visible goods (a Honda motorbike, a car, a house).
- **Low-involvement purchases:** made when consumers do **not** consider the product important or risky enough to give it much thought and deliberation.
- As perceived risk rises, involvement **increases**.
- **Types of perceived risk:** **personal**, **social** (fear of what others will think), **economic** (losing money). Extensive (expensive, unfamiliar) purchases also carry high **performance** and **psychological** risk.

**4 types of consumer buying behaviour:**

| Type | Involvement | Features | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. **Routine response** (programmed) behaviour | Low | Frequent, low-cost, little search or effort, almost automatic | Soft drinks, snacks, milk, Indomie |
| 2. **Limited decision making** | Moderate | Occasional purchase; familiar product category but **unfamiliar brand**; moderate search time | A new brand of jeans |
| 3. **Extensive decision making** (complex, high involvement) | High | Unfamiliar, expensive, bought rarely, high perceived risk, a lot of search; passes through all 6 stages | Cars, homes, computers, education |
| 4. **Impulse buying** | Low | **No conscious planning**; a sudden urge to buy | Chocolate at the fuel-station counter |

- **Complex decision making** (= extensive) is a high-involvement purchase that requires a great deal of thought.
- **Inertia:** repeatedly buying the same brand mainly because it **saves effort**, not because of strong commitment. **Brand loyalty** is repeat buying **with** strong commitment.

**Kotler's 4 types (involvement plus differences between brands):**

| | High involvement | Low involvement |
|---|---|---|
| **Significant differences between brands** | Complex buying behaviour | Variety-seeking buying behaviour |
| **Few differences between brands** | Dissonance-reducing buying behaviour | Habitual buying behaviour |

**Explain more**

- **Limited:** Bola buys clothes often but asks friends before trying a jeans brand she has never used. **Extensive:** a couple buying their first house consult friends, relatives and estate agents for weeks. **Impulse:** Ngozi stops for fuel and buys an unplanned chocolate bar. "Frequent purchase of low-cost items" is **routine response**, never extensive.
- **Risk examples:** Chioma avoids a cheap-looking dress because classmates may laugh at her: **social risk**. Emeka hesitates over a ₦2,500,000 used car because he could lose a lot of money if it is faulty: **economic risk**.
- **The same product can trigger different behaviour.** Dinner out is extensive for someone who rarely eats out and limited for a regular; an anniversary dinner gets more thought than a casual one.
- **Kotler's grid:** complex = a laptop; dissonance-reducing = floor tiles (costly, brands look alike, so the buyer later seeks reassurance); habitual = salt (often inertia); variety-seeking = biscuits (switching for change, not dissatisfaction).
- **Impulse buying** is driven mainly by **motivation tied to immediate gratification**, a psychological factor. About 60% of purchases are unplanned, so sweets sit at the checkout.

### Lesson 5.3: Factors influencing consumer behaviour and psychological factors

**Must know**

- **3 categories of factors** affecting the consumer buying decision process: **personal, psychological and social**. Kotler splits **cultural** out of social, giving **4**: cultural, social, personal, psychological.

| Category | What it covers |
|---|---|
| **Personal** influences | The **personal characteristics** of the consumer: age and life-cycle stage, occupation, economic situation, demographics (sex, race), lifestyle, who in the family decides |
| **Psychological** influences | Motives (motivation), **perception, learning (ability and knowledge), attitudes**, personality and self-concept |
| **Social** influences | Roles and family, **reference groups**, social class, **culture and subculture** |

**Psychological factors:**

- **Motive:** an **internal energising force** that directs a person's activities towards satisfying a need or achieving a goal. Behaviour is usually driven by several motives at once.
- **Maslow's hierarchy of needs (5 levels, lowest to highest):**
  1. **Physiological** (food, water, shelter)
  2. **Safety** (security, protection)
  3. **Love and belonging** (social acceptance, affection)
  4. **Esteem** (status, recognition, respect)
  5. **Self-actualisation** (reaching one's full potential)
- **Perception:** the process of **selecting, organising and interpreting** information inputs received through the senses (sight, taste, hearing, smell, touch) to produce meaning.
- **3 selective processes** of perception:
  1. **Selective exposure:** choosing which inputs reach awareness (we notice what meets current needs, or a sharp change such as a big price cut).
  2. **Selective distortion:** changing or twisting information that conflicts with what we already believe.
  3. **Selective retention:** remembering information that supports our beliefs and forgetting what does not.
- **Learning:** a **relatively permanent change in behaviour** that results from the consequences of past behaviour and experience.
- **Knowledge:** familiarity with or expertise in a product. Buyers with little knowledge or experience tend to **use price as an indicator of quality**.
- **Attitude:** a person's **knowledge and positive or negative feelings** about an object or activity (tangible or intangible, living or not). Attitudes are **learned** through experience and interaction, and they shape perception.
- **Personality:** all the **internal traits and behaviours** that make a person unique, arising from **heredity and experience**. Traits: workaholism, compulsiveness, self-confidence, friendliness, adaptability, ambitiousness, dogmatism, authoritarianism, introversion, extroversion, aggressiveness.
- **Self-concept** is also known as **self-image**: the way a person sees himself or herself. People buy brands that match that image.

**Explain more**

- **Maslow in action:** a newly promoted bank manager buys a designer watch so colleagues admire his status: **esteem**. A nutritional drink was marketed to fitness and energy-conscious buyers (belonging or esteem level), but research showed low-income inner-city buyers used it as a **meal substitute**: **physiological**. Motives are often subconscious and hard to measure. Order trap: safety comes **before** love and belonging; esteem comes **after** it.
- **Selective exposure:** of about 1,500 adverts Kemi sees daily, she notices the one announcing a big price cut on the laptop she needs. **Selective distortion:** an MTN advert compares its data plans with a rival's, and viewers who favour the rival remember it as the rival's advert. This is the danger of comparative adverts. **Selective retention:** Seyi remembers praise for his favourite boot brand and forgets last week's bad review.
- "Selective motivation" is **not** a selective process; there are only three (exposure, distortion, retention).
- **Learning:** to change behaviour, give new product information and experience, e.g. **free samples**. **Price as quality cue:** buyers new to non-alcoholic beer picked the dearest six-pack.
- **Attitudes:** a favourable attitude does **not** always mean the person intends to buy or is able to buy. A student may love the newest iPhone yet be unable to afford it. **Social class is not a personality trait**; it is a social factor.

### Lesson 5.4: Personal and social factors

**Must know**

**Family life cycle stages** (part of the personal factor "life-cycle stage"):

1. Bachelor stage (young, single)
2. Newly married, no children
3. Full nest I (youngest child under 6)
4. Full nest II (youngest child 6 or over)
5. Full nest III (older couple, dependent children)
6. **Empty nest I:** older married, no children at home, head **still working**
7. **Empty nest II:** older married, no children at home, head **retired**
8. Solitary survivor, in the labour force
9. Solitary survivor, retired

The **modernised** family life cycle adds divorced and childless households.

**Family decision-making roles:**

| Role | What the person does |
|---|---|
| **Initiator** | Recognises the problem and **begins** the decision-making process |
| **Influencer** | Exerts **personal influence** on the decision |
| **Information gatherer** | **Seeks information** about available products and their characteristics |
| **Decision maker** (decider) | Makes the **final decision** |
| **Purchaser** (buyer) | Actually buys the product |
| **User** | Consumes or uses the product |

Kotler's general **buying roles** (5): initiator, influencer, decider, buyer, user.

**Reference group:** a group whose values, attitudes or behaviour a person adopts and uses as a guide (family, friends, associations, civic and professional bodies). It can influence positively or negatively.

- **3 types:** **membership** group (you belong to it), **aspiration** group (you want to belong to it), **disassociative** group (you do not want to belong to it).
- The **family** is the most important **primary** reference group.
- **3 kinds of influence** a reference group exerts:
  1. **Informational influence:** it is a source of **believable information** about brands and companies.
  2. **Comparative influence:** it gives members something to **compare** their beliefs, attitudes and behaviour **against**.
  3. **Normative influence:** it persuades members to **conform to its norms**.
- **Affinity marketing:** marketing aimed at members of a reference group, with the group endorsing the product to its members (e.g. a credit card endorsed by an alumni association).
- A group's influence depends on the person's **susceptibility** and **strength of involvement** with the group.

**Social class:** an **open** group of people with **similar social rank**, based on occupation, education, income, wealth, race, ethnicity and possessions. It shapes the type, quality and quantity of products bought.

**Culture:** the values, ideas and attitudes accepted by a **homogeneous** group of people and **passed down from one generation to the next**.

**Subculture:** a sub-group within a culture with its own **distinguishing modes of behaviour**, based on geographic region or human characteristics such as age and ethnic background. In Nigeria: **Yoruba, Hausa and Igbo** subcultures, among others.

**Explain more**

- Traps: "age and life-cycle stage" is **personal**; "social status", "peer influence" and "cultural heritage" are **social**. "Head still working" = **Empty nest I**; "head retired" = **Empty nest II**.
- **Family roles at home:** a child says the family needs a new television (initiator); the mother compares prices online at Slot and Jumia (information gatherer); the eldest son argues for a smart TV (influencer); the father decides (decision maker); the mother pays at the shop (purchaser); everyone watches (users). In dual-income homes children influence more purchases.
- **Reference groups:** Kunle, a 200L accounting student, copies the suits and briefcases of a professional accounting body he hopes to join: an **aspiration group**. A friend's honest phone review: informational influence. Classmates who mock anyone not wearing a certain sneaker brand: normative influence. Reference group endorsements are a **social** factor shaping brand loyalty among young adults.
- **Social class:** upper-middle-class buyers lean towards brands like Mercedes; lower-class shoppers tend to shop near home and do little information search. Stores project class images (a Lekki boutique versus a Balogun stall). "Open" means people can move between classes.
- **Culture in Nigeria:** respect for age and duty to the extended family shape buying. Culture also decides which adverts are acceptable: drinking beer in the open is normal in Lagos but may be frowned upon in parts of the North. Wise firms treat subcultures as separate market segments.
- Family, reference groups and social class are all social influences operating **inside** a wider culture. So "culture, subculture and reference groups" are correctly called social influences in the three-category scheme.

### Lesson 5.5: Organisational markets and organisational buying behaviour

**Must know**

**Kotler's 5 customer markets:** **consumer** (individuals and households buying for personal use) plus four **organisational** markets:

1. **Industrial (producer):** buy to produce other goods and services for profit or other objectives.
2. **Reseller:** buy goods and services **in order to resell them at a profit** (wholesalers, retailers).
3. **Government and non-profit:** buy to produce public services or pass goods to those who need them.
4. **International:** buyers abroad (consumers, producers, resellers, governments).

**Organisational versus consumer markets:**

1. **Fewer but larger buyers.**
2. **Geographically concentrated**; consumer markets comprise many thousands of scattered consumers.
3. **Derived demand:** comes from the demand for consumer goods.
4. Demand is fairly **inelastic** and **fluctuates** more.
5. More **formal** buying by **trained, professional** buyers, with **many people** involved.
6. **Close buyer-seller relationships**; direct buying, reciprocity, leasing.
7. Industrial products are **more technical and complex** than consumer products.

**Buying centre:** the **decision-making unit** of a buying organisation: all the individuals and groups who participate in its purchasing decision process.

| Buying centre role | What they do |
|---|---|
| **Users** | Will **use** the product or service |
| **Influencers** | Provide **information** that influences the decision (often technical staff who draw up specifications) |
| **Buyers** | Have **formal authority** to select the supplier and arrange the terms of purchase |
| **Deciders** | Make the **final decision** |
| **Approvers** | **Authorise** the actions proposed by deciders or buyers |
| **Gatekeepers** | **Control the flow of information** into the organisation (and access to members) |

Kotler adds **initiators** (those who first request the purchase). "Promoter" is **not** a buying centre role.

**Buy classes:** organisational buying situations are divided into **3 classes**, called **buy classes**:

1. **Straight rebuy:** a routine reorder; the company has **extensive experience**, the process is simple and routine, nothing changes.
2. **Modified rebuy:** a **recurring purchase that is less routine**; the buyer changes specifications, prices, terms or suppliers.
3. **New task:** buying something **for the first time**; requires the buyer to undertake a new purchasing task, with the most information search and the most people involved.

"Limited decision making" is a **consumer** behaviour type, **not** a buy class.

**Buy phases:** the stages of the organisational buying decision process (Kotler's 8):

1. **Problem recognition:** someone in the company recognises an **unmet need** that can be met by acquiring a good or service
2. General need description
3. Product specification
4. Supplier search
5. Proposal solicitation
6. Supplier selection
7. Order-routine specification
8. Performance review

**4 major influences on organisational buying:** **environmental** (economy, technology, politics, competition), **organisational** (objectives, policies, procedures, structure), **interpersonal** (the **buying centre**), **individual** (each buyer's age, education, position, personality, attitude to risk). Market segmentation is **not** one.

**Explain more**

- **Derived demand:** when more households build houses, demand for cement rises, so a cement maker's demand for **limestone** rises.
- **Buying centre in action:** at an oil-servicing firm in Port Harcourt, the manager's secretary decides which sales representatives see him and which brochures reach his desk: **gatekeeper**. At a bottling company, operators who will run a new filling machine every day: **users**. Engineers who write crane specifications but do not place the order: **influencers**. A purchasing officer with formal authority to pick the supplier and negotiate terms: **buyer**. A ₦15,000,000 generator recommended by the purchasing team cannot go ahead until the managing director signs: **approver**.
- **Buy classes in action:** a Lagos school reorders the same A4 paper from the same supplier every month with no changes: **straight rebuy**. A hotel changes the fabric specification for bed sheets and invites two new suppliers to quote alongside its current one: **modified rebuy**. A Kano textile company buys a solar power system for the first time after gathering a great deal of information: **new task**.
- **Performance review** is the **last** buy phase: the firm checks how well the chosen supplier performed.
- Do not confuse the **family roles** (initiator, influencer, information gatherer, decision maker, purchaser, user) with the **buying centre roles** (users, influencers, buyers, deciders, approvers, gatekeepers).

### One-minute recap

- 6 stages: problem recognition, information search (internal, external; evoked set), evaluation of alternatives, purchase decision, purchase, post-purchase evaluation. Cognitive dissonance = post-purchase doubt, cut by warranties and after-sales contact.
- Involvement = importance and intensity of interest; higher risk means higher involvement. Risks: personal, social, economic.
- 4 types: routine response, limited, extensive (complex), impulse. Inertia = repeat buying to save effort.
- 3 factor categories: personal (age, life-cycle stage, occupation, income), psychological (motives, perception, learning, attitudes, personality), social (family, reference groups, social class, culture, subculture). Kotler adds cultural as a fourth.
- Maslow: physiological, safety, love and belonging, esteem, self-actualisation. Perception: selective exposure, distortion, retention.
- Reference groups: membership, aspiration, disassociative; influence is informational, comparative, normative. Family is the most important primary reference group.
- Buying centre: users, influencers, buyers, deciders, approvers, gatekeepers. 3 buy classes: straight rebuy, modified rebuy, new task. Process stages = buy phases, starting with problem recognition.

### Theory practice

**Question:** A Lagos bakery that has always bought flour from one miller decides to test a new flour blend and asks two other millers to quote. Name the buy class, and explain how it differs from the other two buy classes.

*Answer outline:* This is a **modified rebuy**: a recurring purchase made less routine because the bakery is changing the specification and considering new suppliers. A **straight rebuy** would be a routine reorder of the same flour from the same miller with no changes, because the bakery has extensive experience. A **new task** would be a first-ever purchase (e.g. its first industrial oven), needing the most information search and the largest buying centre. There are three buy classes in all.
