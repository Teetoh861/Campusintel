## Topic 8: Sources of Nigerian Law

**What you must know:** what "sources of Nigerian law" means; primary vs secondary sources; received English law and the 1 January 1900 date; the test for a statute of general application and its cases; Nigerian legislation (Constitution, Ordinances, Acts, Laws, Decrees, Edicts, subsidiary legislation); customary law, Islamic law and the three validity tests with cases; judicial precedent; international law; secondary sources.

### Lesson 8.1: Meaning and classification of sources

**Must know**

- **Sources of Nigerian law:** the materials through which a legal practitioner, a court or a judge finds reliable authorities for a particular legal question. It tells you how and where to locate the law on any problem. (It is not about printers of statutes, judges' private beliefs or the 1914 amalgamation.)
- Sources are of two kinds:

| Primary sources (can bind a court) | Secondary sources (only persuasive) |
|---|---|
| 1. English law (received English law and extended English law) | Law reports |
| 2. Nigerian legislation and subsidiary enactments | Textbooks and treatises |
| 3. Nigerian case law (judicial precedent) | Legal periodicals and journals |
| 4. Customary law, including Islamic law where applicable | Law digests |
| 5. International law, once enacted into Nigerian law | Legal dictionaries, newspapers, etc. |

- **Key difference:** only **primary** sources can bind a Nigerian court; **secondary** sources merely **persuade**, and are usually relied on where no primary source is available or applicable.

**Explain more**

- A common way of counting is "six sources": received English law, Nigerian legislation, case law, customary law, delegated legislation and international law. Whatever the count, the primary/secondary split is the one examiners test.
- Trap: law reports appear on the secondary list because they are the books in which cases are found; the **decision** inside the report (judicial precedent) is the primary source.
- **Gboga problem** (a novel point, no statute or reported case, only a textbook by a leading professor): Gboga **may cite the textbook as persuasive authority**. The court is not bound by it. Its weight depends on **the standing (personality) of the author and the significance of the subject covered**. Build the answer: list primary and secondary sources; say primary sources bind; say textbooks are used where decisions are scarce or absent, if written by eminent scholars and of acceptable standard; give examples (Obilade, Kodilinye, Okonkwo, Aguda, Nwogwugwu; Sagay on international law); conclude on the facts.

### Lesson 8.2: Received English law

**Must know**

- **Received English law** = 3 components:
  1. the **common law** of England;
  2. the **doctrines of equity**;
  3. the **statutes of general application** (SOGA) that were **in force in England on 1 January 1900**.
- **Reception enactments** (local laws that brought English law in): **section 45(1) of the Interpretation Act** (now section 32(1), Interpretation Act, Cap I23 LFN 2004) and **section 14 of the Supreme Court Ordinance 1914**, among others; also the High Court Laws of the States.
- **Timing rule:** common law and equity apply **as they stand in England on the day they are applied** in Nigeria (not limited by time). Only SOGA are tied to the **1 January 1900** cut-off.
- A SOGA **remains in force in Nigeria even if later repealed in England**, until a Nigerian legislature repeals it.
- **"Statute of general application" is not defined** by the Supreme Court Ordinance, the Interpretation Act or any other reception enactment; its meaning comes from **case law**.
- **Osborne CJ's "rough but not infallible test"** (*Attorney General v John Holt & Co Ltd* (1910)): ask (1) **by what courts** is the statute applied in England? (2) **to what classes of the community** in England does it apply?
  - Applied by **all** civil and criminal courts to **all** classes on 1 January 1900: **strong likelihood** it is in force here.
  - Applied only by **certain courts** (e.g. a statute regulating procedure) or only to **certain classes** (e.g. an Act regulating a particular trade): probably **not** locally applicable.
- **Foreign (including English) decisions** do not bind Nigerian courts; Nigeria is independent and sovereign, so they are only **persuasive**.

**Explain more**

- **Cases holding statutes to be of general application:**

| Case | Statute | Point |
|---|---|---|
| *Lawal v Younan* (1961), Federal Supreme Court | Fatal Accidents Acts 1846 and 1864 | applied to all classes in England |
| *Braithwaite v Folarin* (1938), WACA | Fraudulent Conveyances Act 1571 | applies generally to ordinary affairs and dealings of men, without qualification |
| *Young v Abina* (1940), WACA | Land Transfer Act 1897 | applied generally to the estates of all persons dying in England after 1 January 1898 |
| *Labinjoh v Abake* (1924) | Infants Relief Act 1874 | held a SOGA |
| *Taylor v Taylor* (1934) | Trustee Act 1888 | held a SOGA |
| *Thomas v De Souza* (1929) | Real Property Limitation Act 1874 | held a SOGA |
| *Malomo v Olusola* (1954) | Statute of Frauds 1677 | held a SOGA |

- **\_Dede v African Association Ltd\_ (1910):** Weber J held that SOGA must mean statutes **applicable throughout the United Kingdom**, not those in force in England only. This sits uneasily with the wording of the Ordinance, which expressly says "in force in **England**".
- Worked application: an English Act of 1890 regulating procedure in certain English courts, in force on 1 January 1900, would probably **not** be a SOGA (applied only by certain courts). An Act applied by all civil courts to all classes would **likely** be one, but the test is "not infallible", so never say "certain".
- **Where SOGA do not apply:** the States carved from the old Western Region (Ogun, Ondo, Osun, Ekiti, Oyo, Edo, Delta) replaced SOGA, for matters within State competence, with their own Law of England (Application) Law 1959. They still apply in **Lagos** (the old colony of Lagos, named in the former section 45 of the Interpretation Act) and elsewhere, and everywhere on Federal matters.
- **Extended English law**: English statutes extended to Nigeria by name, and Nigerian laws that adopt the English law of a subject. It forms part of "English law" as a primary source.
- English Court of Appeal decision of 1995 cited in Abuja: **persuasive only**.
- Essay hint ("Whether an English statute is of general application is uncertain and controversial"): reception provisions; no statutory definition; *Dede*; Osborne CJ's test; other cases; conclude with the two conditions: **in force in England on 1 January 1900** and **of general application**.

### Lesson 8.3: Nigerian legislation, the Constitution and delegated legislation

**Must know**

- **Nigerian legislation** = **statutes** + **subsidiary legislation**.
- **Statutes** = laws enacted by the legislative arm. Their name depends on when, by whom and under what government they were made:

| Name | Made by |
|---|---|
| **Ordinance** | the Nigerian **central legislature before 1 October 1954** |
| **Act** | the **National Assembly** (Senate + House of Representatives), civilian rule |
| **Law** | a **State House of Assembly**, civilian rule |
| **Decree** | the **Federal Military Government**, military rule |
| **Edict** | a **Military Governor/Administrator of a State**, military rule |

- **1 October 1954** is the dividing line because **federalism** was introduced by the **Lyttelton Constitution**, which came into force that day.
- **Legislation** is the **most important source** of law in a modern nation and the **"measuring scale"** for the efficacy of other legal sources; it can alter their content.
- **Constitution of the Federal Republic of Nigeria 1999:** came into force on **29 May 1999**; the highest law, from which all other laws derive validity. **Section 1** proclaims its **supremacy** over all other laws, authorities and persons; any law inconsistent with it is **void to the extent of the inconsistency** (s 1(3)).
- **Division of legislative powers (s 4):** **Exclusive List** (Part I, Second Schedule): National Assembly only. **Concurrent List** (Part II): both National Assembly and State Houses of Assembly. **Residual matters** (on neither list): States only.
- **Subsidiary (delegated) legislation:** law made by persons or bodies other than the legislature under express powers conferred by a statute, called the **enabling statute**. Examples: **bye-laws** of local governments, **regulations** of public corporations, **statutory instruments** of ministers (also rules, orders). An Act of the National Assembly is **not** subsidiary legislation.
- **Ultra vires:** delegated legislation that exceeds the power granted is **beyond power, null and void**.
- **Laws of the Federation of Nigeria (LFN):** all federal statutes up to **31 January 1990** consolidated as **LFN 1990**; revised up to 2002 and published as **LFN 2004** in **loose-leaf** format, so future amendments can be **easily incorporated**.

**Explain more**

- **Reasons for delegated legislation:** (1) it is practically impossible for the legislature to make laws for every aspect of life; (2) unforeseeable variations in events and conduct; (3) many areas need **technical expertise** the legislature lacks; (4) the legislature sets the general framework and leaves **specialists, technocrats and those involved in specific activities** to handle particular cases (e.g. professional bodies under the Legal Practitioners Act regulating their members). Allowing administrators to **override** the enabling statute is never a reason.
- **Arguments against:** less democratic (made by unelected bodies), weaker scrutiny, risk of abuse, sheer volume makes it hard for citizens to know the law. **Controls:** the courts (ultra vires), the legislature (amending or revoking the enabling power).
- Worked example: a State Law allows a local government to fix fines of up to ₦5,000 for illegal dumping; its bye-law fixes ₦50,000. The fine exceeds the delegated power by ₦45,000 (10 times the limit), so it is **ultra vires and liable to be declared null and void**.
- Dating examples: 1950 central enactment = **Ordinance**; 1976 State Military Governor = **Edict**; 2015 Lagos State House of Assembly = **Law**. Trap: an Edict was **never** made by the Federal Military Government.
- A State Law conflicting with the Constitution is **void to the extent of the inconsistency**; recency and State autonomy do not save it.
- Legislation also **validates and modifies** other sources: the Interpretation Act receives English law; the High Court Laws validate customary law; the Criminal Code abolished trial by ordeal and witchcraft practices.
- Nigeria became independent on **1 October 1960** and a republic on **1 October 1963** (do not confuse with 1954).

### Lesson 8.4: Customary law and Islamic law

**Must know**

- **Customary law:** the customs accepted by members of a community as binding among them.
- **Classification:** (1) **ethnic (non-Moslem) customary law**: indigenous, **unwritten**, varies from one ethnic group to another; (2) **Moslem (Islamic) law**: **largely written**.
- **Sources of Islamic law (the Sharia):** 1. the **Holy Quran**; 2. the **Sunna** (practice of the Prophet); 3. **consensus** of Islamic scholars (ijma); 4. **analogical deductions** from the holy writ (qiyas). The predominant school in Nigeria is the **Maliki** school.
- **Features:** acceptance as obligatory by the community; **flexibility and adaptability**. *Lewis v Bankole* (1908), Osborne CJ: "One of the most striking features of West African native custom … is its **flexibility**; it appears to have been always subject to motives of expediency, and it shows unquestionable **adaptability to altered circumstances without entirely losing its character**."
- **3 validity tests** (prescribed by **statute**: the High Court Laws of the 36 States and the FCT, e.g. s 44 High Court Law, Oyo State 1978). A court enforces a customary rule only if it is **not**:
  1. **repugnant to natural justice, equity and good conscience**;
  2. **contrary to public policy**;
  3. **incompatible, directly or by implication, with any law for the time being in force**.
- **Inconsistency with English law is NOT a ground of invalidity.**

**Explain more**

- **Repugnancy cases:**

| Case | Holding |
|---|---|
| *Eshugbayi Eleko v Officer Administering the Government of Nigeria* [1931] AC 662 | Lord Atkin: a **barbarous** custom must be rejected as repugnant |
| *Dawodu v Danmole* (1958, Privy Council 1962) | **idi-igi** (distribution **per stirpes**, by each wife's branch of children) is **not** repugnant; Privy Council rejected Jibowu's view that it breached equality (per capita, **ori-ojori**). Natural justice in a polygamous society is not equated with that of a monogamous one |
| *Re Effiong Okon Atta* (1930) | custom letting a former slave owner administer the slave's personal estate after death: **repugnant** |
| *Guri v Hadejia Native Authority* (1959) | rule denying a person accused of highway robbery (hiraba) the right to **defend himself**: **repugnant** |

- **Public policy:** *Cole v Akinyele* (1960), Brett FJ (Federal Supreme Court): a custom that a child born out of wedlock, once paternity is accepted, becomes legitimate and shares equally with children of a marriage under the Marriage Ordinance was **invalid on grounds of public policy**.
- **Incompatibility:** if a State Law expressly prohibits what a custom permits, the custom fails the incompatibility test. Example: section 3 of the Legitimacy Act modified the Yoruba custom of acknowledgement.
- The repugnancy clause **has not received a detailed and settled explanation**; its meaning is gathered from decided cases.
- **No customary crimes:** customary criminal law has been displaced. Section 36(12) of the 1999 Constitution says no one shall be convicted of an offence unless it is **defined and its penalty prescribed in a written law** (Criminal Code, Penal Code, etc.). In *Aoko v Fagbemi* (1961) a conviction for adultery, not an offence in written law, was quashed. Customary law now operates in civil matters.
- **Afar problem** (Agbor custom: robbery suspect not heard): invalid as **repugnant**, following *Guri*. Not because it differs from English procedure. Outline: importance of custom; statutory basis; three tests; uncertainty of repugnancy; *Guri*; conclude.
- **Justice Dede problem** (striking down every custom inconsistent with English law): wrong. Judge customs only by the three statutory tests; cite *Dawodu v Danmole*.
- Diversity of ethnic customary law is a major obstacle to uniformity within each State.

### Lesson 8.5: Judicial precedent, international law and secondary sources

**Must know**

- **Judicial precedent:** the practice whereby the earlier decision of a court is followed in later similar cases; a **lower** court **must follow** the earlier decision of a **higher** court. What judges make is **case law**.
- **Stare decisis** = "let the decision stand".
- **Ratio decidendi** = the reason for the decision: the legal principle necessary for the decision, which **binds** later lower courts. **Obiter dictum** = a remark made "by the way", not necessary to the decision: **not binding**, only persuasive.
- **Advantages:** ready solutions to legal problems; uniformity, consistency and certainty; lets lawyers, litigants and the public **predict** outcomes; settled expectations are not disrupted through default, mischief or ignorance.
- **Forms of precedent:** 1. **Original**: a new rule where no similar earlier case exists (e.g. *Carlill v Carbolic Smoke Ball Co* [1893] on offer). 2. **Derivative**: extends an existing rule **by analogy** to a new situation. 3. **Declaratory**: restates an existing rule, giving it **added weight and authority**.
- Stare decisis is impracticable without (1) a **hierarchy of courts** and (2) an **efficient system of law reporting**.
- **International law** (historically **jus gentium**, the law of nations): no nation can exist in isolation. Treaties, conventions, covenants and declarations. In Nigeria a **treaty does not operate as law until the National Assembly enacts it into law** (s 12, 1999 Constitution).
- *Sani Abacha v Gani Fawehinmi* (2000): the **African Charter on Human and Peoples' Rights** (1981), incorporated by statute, is **enforceable in Nigerian courts**.

**Explain more**

- A Kaduna State High Court facing facts materially similar to a Supreme Court decision **must follow its ratio**; fairness or the report it appears in does not matter.
- Allowing a judge to depart freely from higher decisions he dislikes is **not** an advantage; it destroys certainty.
- Ratified but unenacted convention (ratified 2020, not enacted): **not enforceable** as law in Nigeria, however strong the trader's claim. Executive signature or ratification is not enough. (Jus cogens rules are an exception binding regardless of incorporation.)
- **Secondary sources:**
  - **Law reports:** essential where precedent operates, because only by reference to reported cases can courts and lawyers ascertain the law. Oldest: the **Year Books** (about 1283 to 1535). First Nigerian series: the **Nigerian Law Reports** (covering decisions from 1881), now extinct. Current: **NWLR** (since 1985), **SCNJ**, **LRCN**, **All NLR**, Federation Weekly Law Reports. Sustainability is a weakness of Nigerian law reporting.
  - **Textbooks and treatises:** English classics (Bracton, Coke, Blackstone; later Dicey, Cheshire, Hood Phillips, Wade); Nigerian (Obilade, Nwogwugwu, Okonkwo, Kodilinye, Aguda; Sagay on international law). Persuasive; weight depends on the author and the subject.
  - **Periodicals and journals:** professional, academic or both (faculty journals).
  - **Law digests** (e.g. the Digest of Supreme Court Cases): **abridgements** of cases summarising facts, issues, arguments and decisions.
  - **Dictionaries:** Jowitt's Dictionary of English Law, Stroud's Judicial Dictionary.

### One-minute recap

- Sources = materials through which a lawyer or court finds reliable authorities.
- Primary bind (English law, legislation, case law, customary/Islamic law); secondary persuade.
- Received English law = common law + equity + SOGA in force in England on 1 January 1900 (s 45/32 Interpretation Act; s 14 Supreme Court Ordinance 1914).
- SOGA undefined by statute; Osborne CJ's test asks which courts and which classes; SOGA survive English repeal.
- Ordinance (pre-1 October 1954), Act, Law, Decree, Edict; Constitution supreme (s 1), in force 29 May 1999.
- Delegated legislation beyond its enabling power is ultra vires and void.
- Custom valid unless repugnant, contrary to public policy or incompatible with written law; *Eleko*, *Dawodu*, *Atta*, *Guri*, *Cole v Akinyele*.
- Islamic law: Quran, Sunna, consensus, analogy; Maliki school.
- Stare decisis = let the decision stand; ratio binds, obiter persuades; needs hierarchy and law reporting.
- Treaties bind only once enacted by the National Assembly (*Abacha v Fawehinmi*).

### Theory practice

**Question:** "Customary law is a veritable source of Nigerian law only in so far as it passes the tests of validity." Discuss with decided cases.

*Answer outline:*
- **Introduction:** define sources of Nigerian law and customary law (customs accepted by a community as binding); classify into ethnic customary law and Islamic law; note its flexibility (*Lewis v Bankole*). Thesis: courts enforce custom only if it passes three statutory tests.
- **Statutory basis:** High Court Laws of the States and FCT (e.g. s 44 High Court Law, Oyo State 1978).
- **Repugnancy:** no settled definition; *Eshugbayi Eleko* (barbarous custom); *Re Effiong Okon Atta*; *Guri v Hadejia NA*; limits: *Dawodu v Danmole* (inconsistency with English ideas is not repugnancy).
- **Public policy:** *Cole v Akinyele*.
- **Incompatibility with written law:** e.g. Legitimacy Act; s 36(12) 1999 Constitution and *Aoko v Fagbemi* (no unwritten customary crimes).
- **Conclusion:** custom remains a primary, binding source within these limits; the tests protect justice without imposing English standards.
