## Topic 9: Legal Research and Legal Writing

**What you must know:** meaning of and reasons for legal research; the library, the librarian, classification, call numbers and the catalogue; statutes, law reports and how to cite and trace cases; periodicals, reference books and databases; types of essay and the steps in essay writing; hints on good writing; answering essay questions (IDBC) and problem questions (IRAC).

### Lesson 9.1: Meaning of legal research and why it matters

**Must know**

- **Research** (in legal method): the use of library materials to seek recorded information on a particular legal problem in order to **authoritatively determine the rights, duties and liabilities of the parties**.
- Saying attributed to King George: **lawyers do not know much more law than other people, but they know better where to find it**. It stresses legal research and effective use of the library.
- **Why research:**
  1. Law is **not static**; it is **dynamic and developing**, so the lawyer must find the **current** rules.
  2. Findings help the lawyer **persuade the court** to adopt his reasoning or interpretation.
  3. Diligent research and advocacy can persuade a court to adopt a **new position** or even **reverse its earlier decision**.
  4. Research leads to the **primary sources** of law. The library is to a lawyer what the laboratory is to a scientist.
- **Glanville Williams' warning:** the great disadvantage of confining yourself to textbooks and lecture notes is that you take **all your law at second hand**. The law is in **statutes and judicial decisions**; what the text writer thinks is not, in itself, law, and he may have misinterpreted the authorities. Going to them is going to "the fountain head".

**Explain more**

- **The law librarian** is a specialist on which sources are relevant to a subject (better still if legally trained). Once the subject area is identified, a competent librarian can **assemble the primary and secondary materials** for you. Such librarians are rare, so you must still know how to use the library yourself. The librarian does not write your opinion or decide cases.
- A library is a **storehouse of information** in many forms; law is multidisciplinary, so research is not limited to legal materials.
- Essay hint ("A good lawyer knows the law; a better lawyer knows where to find it; the best knows both"): explain research and its reasons; the librarian's role and its limits; even a lawyer who seems sure must **verify** his conclusion from source materials.
- Trap: "the law is static, so research once" is the classic wrong option.

### Lesson 9.2: Finding books: classification, call numbers and the catalogue

**Must know**

- **6 elements identifying a book:** 1. the cover; 2. the author's name; 3. the publisher; 4. the title; 5. the place of publication; 6. the date of publication. All except the cover appear on the **title page**. (The name of the owning library is **not** one.)
- Books are arranged by **subject** under a **classification system**, not by colour or size. The two commonest:

| System | Call mark begins with | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Library of Congress** (used by most Nigerian libraries) | a **letter** | K108.7 |
| **Dewey Decimal** | a **number** | 642.13 |

- **Library of Congress:** 21 letters mark broad subject classes, e.g. A General works, B Philosophy, H Social Science, J Political Science, **K Law**, Z Bibliography.
- **Call number:** identifies a book as a fingerprint identifies a person; no two books share one. In **B358.C54**: **B** = broad subject (philosophy); **358** = sub-topic; **C** = first letter of the author's last name; **54** = number further identifying the author.
- **Shelving rule:** first alphabetically by the subject letter, then numerically by the subject number, then alphabetically by the author letter, then by the author number (read as a decimal).
- **Catalogue:** lists **all** the library's books. Materials can be found by **author, title or subject** (author entry, title entry, subject entry). The **author entry is the main entry**.

**Explain more**

- **Worked example (shelving):** order B358.C54, B200.C57, K146.O2, B200.A18.
  1. Letter: the three B books come before K146.O2.
  2. Number: B200 comes before B358.
  3. Author letter within B200: A18 before C57.
  - Order: **B200.A18, B200.C57, B358.C54, K146.O2**. So B200.A18 is shelved first.
- **Why the catalogue beats browsing**, even with open stacks: a browser may miss books that are **borrowed, waiting to be reserved or on the reserve shelf**; the catalogue lists them all and saves time. In a **closed-stack** library it is indispensable.
- Forms of catalogue: printout book catalogues, microfilm or microfiche, and most commonly the **card catalogue**.
- **Author entry** shows everything on the title page (full name, full title, publisher, place and year) plus pages, series and the other entries. If you know the author but not the exact title, **check the author entry**, note the call number, go to the shelf. Literary works are often not entered by subject.

### Lesson 9.3: Statutes, law reports and citing cases

**Must know**

- Nigeria has the FCT and **36 States**; **each State has its own system of law and courts**, and the Federation and FCT have theirs (Laws of Kano State, Laws of Lagos State, Laws of the FCT, Laws of the Federation). A bail application in the Ogun State High Court is brought under **the law of Ogun State**.
- **Modern law report contains:** name of the case, date, court and judges; **headnote**; outline of the facts; names of counsel; sometimes a summary of their arguments; and **always a verbatim copy of the judgment**. It does **not** give the reporter's own verdict.
- **Citation of a reported case:** names of the parties, the year (or month), the volume, the name of the law report and the page.
- **Square brackets [ ]:** the year is **essential** to locate the case, e.g. *FBIR v Nasr* [1964] 1 All NLR 408. **Round brackets ( ):** the year is **not essential**, e.g. *Ademolekun v Council of UI* (1967) All NLR 40.
- **NWLR style:** *Marina Nominees Ltd v FBIR* [1986] 2 NWLR (Pt 20) 40: 2 = volume, **Pt 20 = part 20**, 40 = page. Part and page matter most; **a part number is never duplicated**.
- **NWLR** (from 1 October 1985) comes out **weekly** and reports **Court of Appeal and Supreme Court** cases; **SCNJ** reports **only Supreme Court** cases, usually every month or two.

**Explain more**

- **Tracing a case:**
  1. With the citation: go to the shelf for that report and trace by year, or by volume plus part (NWLR).
  2. Title known but not the citation, or citation incomplete (e.g. *Adeolu v Mabamiye* (1985) 2 NWLR p.5, part missing): check the **list of cases** in a standard textbook on the subject, or an **index**, for the full citation; otherwise search all that year's volumes. English cases: the Current Law Case Citators (from 1947) or the English Digest.
  3. All cases on a subject: use a **case index**, arranged alphabetically (locus standi under **L**). Judicially defined words ("reasonable cause", "and", "beyond reasonable doubt") are under **"Words and phrases"**. If the index is in arrears, check the list of cases in each recent issue.
- **History:** Year Books (Anglo-French, about 1283 to 1535); private reports (Dyer from 1537; Coke's "The Reports", 1572-1616; Burrow, 1756-1772); then the modern period. Nigeria: Renner's reports (Gold Coast decisions, 1874-1886, when Lagos was part of that colony); regular reporting from **1916** with the **Nigerian Law Reports (NLR)**, covering decisions from 1881 to 1955. Later: NMLR (1964), NWLR (1985), SCNJ, Federation Weekly Law Reports (2000). Most official series have died out.
- **Nigerian vs foreign reports:** NWLR, SCNJ, All NLR, LRCN are Nigerian. **WLR** (Weekly Law Reports), **AC** (Appeal Cases), **All ER** (All England Law Reports, from 1936), QB, Ch are English.
- **Statute books:** governments publish their Acts or Laws and periodically consolidate them (e.g. Laws of the Federation 1990). Check other States' laws, which may differ.

### Lesson 9.4: Periodicals, reference books and information technology

**Must know**

- **Periodicals (journals)** differ from books: (1) they appear **at intervals** (weekly, monthly, quarterly, etc.); a book treats its subject in one complete edition; (2) articles are **briefer**, so you reach the essentials faster; (3) they contain articles by **different writers**. Use them where a topic is too recent for any book.
- **2 types of journal:** **general** (many fields) and **specialised** (one field: banking, taxation, property).
- **Periodical index:** the best tool when you have no citation or do not know which articles exist; it does for articles what the **catalogue** does for books.
- **Reference books:** for brief facts (meaning of a word, date of an event, a quotation, a place) without reading cover to cover. They **do not circulate**, are used in the reference section, and carry **"R" or "REF"** above the call number.
- **2 types of reference book:** **general** (dictionaries, encyclopaedias, year books, atlases, directories) and **subject** (e.g. Black's Law Dictionary, Stroud's Judicial Dictionary, Jowitt's Dictionary of English Law, Osborn's Concise Law Dictionary, Mozley and Whiteley's Law Dictionary, Fawehinmi's Bar and Bench in Nigeria).
- **Halsbury's Laws of England:** the most comprehensive legal **encyclopaedia**, covering all areas of English law; the **take-off point** for legal research. Encyclopaedias are more current than textbooks.
- **Lexis and Westlaw:** **legal databases** making recent judicial decisions and legislation available electronically.

**Explain more**

- Nigerian journals: Nigerian Current Law Review, Nigerian Law Journal, Journal of Islamic and Comparative Law; most law faculties publish one. Foreign: Cambridge Law Journal, Harvard Law Review.
- Amaka, researching recent banking-law developments with no article or citation in mind: use the **periodical index**, not one-by-one browsing.
- Student needing only the meaning of "locus standi" and a historical date: a **reference book** (law dictionary) in the reference section.

### Lesson 9.5: Legal writing: essay types and the writing process

**Must know**

- **Essay:** a short piece of writing on a given subject; a literary composition, usually in prose, reflecting the writer's thoughts, ideas and viewpoints on an issue.
- **4 types of essay:**

| Type | Feature | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Descriptive** | reconstructs how a situation looks or feels, with the writer's feeling | a detainee describing his privations (Awolowo, *My March Through Prison*) |
| **Narrative** | events in the order they occurred | Constitutional development in Nigeria from 1914 to date |
| **Expository** | explains, clarifies, informs, using illustrations and analogies | scope of separation of powers, rule of law, natural justice |
| **Argumentative** | takes a stand, persuades, **demolishes opposing arguments** | "The Abolition of the Death Penalty: To Be or Not to Be?" |

- Types may **overlap** and be combined, but aim for a clear focus.
- **Steps in essay writing:**
  1. **Choose a topic:** do I want insight into it? will I find the materials? is the scope manageable? A good topic is **correct, complete and concise**.
  2. **Preliminary reading.**
  3. **Review (rethink) the topic:** hit it with every question; if stuck, use **what? who? where? when? how? why?**
  4. **Identify the basic question** (the most important, instructive, interesting and appropriate).
  5. **Formulate the thesis:** turn the basic question into a basic answer. A definite thesis makes a **restricted and organised assertion about the topic and clearly reveals your attitude** towards it.
  6. **Test the idea** (4 tests): **writing**, **credibility**, **friendly colleague**, **possibility**.
  7. **Prepare the outline.**
  8. **Choose materials.**
  9. **Examine sources:** organise your reading time; read **selectively**, **responsibly**, **critically**.
  10. **Write the drafts** (at least 4 recommended).

**Explain more**

- **The 4 tests:** **writing**: put the idea on paper to think, revise and rearrange; **credibility**: is it current, plausible and accurate, will it date?; **friendly colleague**: give it to someone, ideally one who has researched the topic, for the most ruthless critique; **possibility**: can you finish in the time and handle the issues? "Repugnancy" is not one of them.
- **Choosing materials:** books specifically on the topic; related materials (indexes, tables, charts, the author's references); **current** books and journals (latest editions); **classics** still relevant as guideposts, e.g. Elias on land law (some forty-one years old) or **Obilade's \_The Nigerian Legal System\_**. Better a few valuable points than a shallow bite of everything.
- **Reading critically** = asking: will it command respect when cited? are the conclusions justified by the evidence? is the reasoning faulty? has the writer covered the subject?
- **Drafts:** **first**: write the whole paper without stopping to correct: "**Don't get it right, get it written**"; **second**: add omitted words and missing details; **third**: cut the "**dead wood**" of repetition and verbosity; address content, organisation, logic, coherence; move from a **writer-oriented draft to a reader-oriented work**; then keep reviewing to a finished product.
- **Divisions:** chapters (1, 2), sections (2.1), subsections (2.5.1); letters or Roman numerals also work.

### Lesson 9.6: Good writing and answering law questions

**Must know**

- **Hints on good writing:** introduction, originality, clarity, brevity, grammatical expression, accuracy, organisation, use of paragraphs, mechanical accuracy, conclusion.
- **Effective introduction:** **identifies the subject, arouses interest, is brief**. It may be written last, once the body is organised.
- **Plagiarism:** the use of thoughts and ideas belonging to others **without acknowledgement**.
- **Brevity:** saying much in few words, never at the expense of substance.
- **3 kinds of conclusion:** **reflective** (highlights related issues and questions for future inquiry); **looking-ahead** (predicts or warns about the future); **straightforward** (states the end and makes suggestions and recommendations).
- **Socio-legal analysis:** use a **multidimensional approach**, not a strictly legal solution (e.g. cultism needs psychological, social, economic and political factors considered).
- **Essay questions: IDBC** = **Introduction, Definition, Body, Conclusion**.
- **Problem questions: IRAC (or IPAC)** = **Issue(s), Rule (or Principle), Application (or Analysis), Conclusion**.

**Explain more**

- **IDBC in practice:**
  - **Introduction:** background to the question (an apt quotation from a scholar or court helps), then a **thesis statement** saying what you will discuss. **Common mistake:** starting the body in the introduction, which loses the introduction marks.
  - **Definition:** an authoritative definition first, then explain simply.
  - **Body:** one point per paragraph, a sharp topic sentence, linking words (moreover, however), and **many cases**.
  - **Conclusion:** summarise and recommend; never leave it out.
- **IRAC in practice:** a question setting out facts and ending "**Advise him**" (e.g. an Onitsha trader whose customary claim is challenged) is a problem question: identify each **issue**, state the **rule** with authority, **apply** it to the facts, **conclude** with clear advice. Writing it as an essay or retelling the facts is wrong. **Hybrid** questions combine both methods.
- **Worked conclusion:** Bisi predicts cybercrime in Nigeria in ten years and warns of what could happen: **looking-ahead**.
- Accuracy: avoid exaggeration, bias and guesswork. Mechanical accuracy: tenses, capitals, spelling.

### One-minute recap

- Research = using library materials to determine authoritatively parties' rights, duties and liabilities.
- Law is dynamic; textbooks give law "at second hand".
- Library of Congress (letter: K108.7) vs Dewey (number: 642.13); shelve by letter, number, author letter, author number.
- Catalogue by author, title, subject; author entry is the main entry.
- Citation: parties, year, volume, report, page; [ ] year essential, ( ) not; NWLR parts never repeat.
- NWLR = Court of Appeal + Supreme Court weekly; SCNJ = Supreme Court only.
- Periodical index for articles; reference books marked R/REF; Halsbury's the take-off point.
- Essays: descriptive, narrative, expository, argumentative; topic correct, complete, concise.
- Four tests: writing, credibility, friendly colleague, possibility; first draft "don't get it right, get it written".
- IDBC for essays, IRAC for problems.

### Theory practice

**Question:** Daphine, a final-year student, asks how to prepare and write her long essay. Advise her on the methods and approaches involved in essay writing.

*Answer outline:*
- **Introduction:** define an essay; note the four types; thesis: good writing follows settled steps.
- **Pre-writing:** choose a manageable topic with available materials (correct, complete, concise); preliminary reading; rethink with what, who, where, when, how, why; identify the basic question; formulate a restricted, organised thesis.
- **Test the idea:** writing, credibility, friendly colleague, possibility.
- **Outline and materials:** topic-specific, related, current and classic works (e.g. Obilade); read selectively, responsibly, critically.
- **Writing:** at least four drafts (get it written; add detail; cut dead wood; review); chapters, sections, subsections.
- **General hints:** brief engaging introduction, originality (no plagiarism), clarity, brevity, accuracy, organisation, paragraphs, mechanical accuracy.
- **Conclusion:** choose reflective, looking-ahead or straightforward; following these steps produces an essay that impresses.
