## Topic 6: Legal Reasoning

**What you must know:** the meaning of legal reasoning and features of legal language; principles, rules and standards; rhetoric; deductive, inductive and analogical logic and the limits of syllogism; practical reasoning and how judges justify decisions; judicial precedent (stare decisis, ratio decidendi, obiter dicta, per incuriam, distinguishing, overruling, the hierarchy of courts); how to approach problem questions.

### Lesson 6.1: Legal reasoning and the language of the law

**Must know**

- **Reasoning:** thinking persuasively, in a coordinated, orderly, sensible and logical manner. **Legal:** connected with law.
- **Legal reasoning:** a **systematic, logical, coordinated, convincing or persuasive thinking, argument or presentation of points or issues relating to law.**
- **Legal reasoning in the judicial process:** the process of careful thinking by a judicial officer in the course of resolving legal issues presented by parties before his court.
- **Features of legal language:**
  1. **Law is usually expressed in general terms**, because it must apply to many people in varied circumstances. Example: **s.316 Criminal Code** (murder) names no weapon, so it covers shooting, stabbing, poisoning or strangling.
  2. **Law can be expressed in highly specific language** when no doubt is intended. Example: **s.6(5) 1999 Constitution** lists the superior courts of record one by one.
  3. **Use of abstract concepts** ("contract", "company", "rule of law", "legal personality", "ownership", "possession", "fee simple"). Main advantage: **economy of words** (no need to list every detail).
  4. **Common words with uncommon meanings** ("matter" for case; "it is submitted that").
  5. **Latin and French phrases** (*consensus ad idem*, *ultra vires*, *audi alteram partem*, *nemo dat quod non habet*).
  6. **Archaic English** ("hereinbefore", "aforementioned", "hereinafter").
  7. **Argot or jargon** for insiders: "my brief has not been perfected" = I have not been paid my fee; "I am speaking from the bar" = I speak honestly as an officer of the court.
  8. **Resonant formal expressions:** "the truth, the whole truth and nothing but the truth".

**Explain more**

- **Case law is generalised too.** *Donoghue v Stevenson* (1932) concerned a snail in ginger beer, but its principle is stated at a **higher level of generality**: a manufacturer of consumer goods owes a duty of care to the final consumer. Stating it only as snails in ginger beer would **hinder the growth of case law**; yet it is limited (consumable goods, proven health harm) so it is not too fluid.
- The rule of law as a concept saves words: it carries (a) supremacy of the law, (b) equality before the law, (c) fundamental human rights.

### Lesson 6.2: Principles, rules and standards

**Must know**

- **Principle of law:** an **established legal truth or proposition so clear that it cannot be contradicted except by a clearer proposition.** It is a timeless guide for making, executing and interpreting law, and a yardstick for judging legislation, decisions and arguments as valid or not, **notwithstanding any good result** they produce.
- Examples in constitutional law: **rule of law, separation of powers, supremacy of the Constitution**.
- **An argument contrary to an established principle immediately becomes suspect and open to legal attack.**
- **Principles furnish the basis for rules; rules are specific applications of principles.**
- **Natural justice** (a principle) gives **2 rules**:
  1. ***Audi alteram partem***: hear the other side; a party must not be condemned unheard.
  2. ***Nemo judex in causa sua***: no one should be a judge in his own cause.
- **Standard:** a general measure of conduct applied **according to the circumstances of each case**, leaving discretion to the judge. Examples: **reasonable care**, the **reasonable man** in negligence, due care, good faith.
- **Rule:** a fixed, detailed precept giving the same answer for the same facts.

| | Principle | Rule | Standard |
|---|---|---|---|
| Nature | Broad legal truth | Specific application | Flexible measure of conduct |
| Example | Natural justice | 30 km/h school-zone limit | "Drive with reasonable care" |
| Discretion | Guides reasoning | Little | Much |

**Explain more**

- **Roscoe Pound** observed that in negligence the law must leave a wide margin of discretion, as in the standard of the reasonable man, and that attempts to replace it with hard and fast rules prove futile; such general standards are applied liberally according to the circumstances of each case.
- A magistrate who hears a ₦1,200,000 claim against a company he largely owns breaches ***nemo judex in causa sua***. Dismissing a worker without hearing him breaches ***audi alteram partem***.
- Lord Hewart CJ in *R v Sussex Justices, ex parte McCarthy* (1924): justice "should not only be done, but should manifestly and undoubtedly be seen to be done."

### Lesson 6.3: Rhetoric and logic

**Must know**

- **Rhetoric:** the art of seeking to convince or persuade another, in writing or speech, to accept one's position or view. **Plato:** "the art of winning men's minds with words."
- **Aristotle** distinguished **forensic** from **deliberative** rhetoric and thought deliberative the nobler. Broadly, **lawyers use forensic rhetoric** (persuading the court for a client); **judges use deliberative rhetoric** (a rational decision justifiable for the parties and society).
- **The principal rhetorical device in law is the appeal to authority:** citing judicial and statutory authorities (primary sources). Writers' opinions and foreign decisions (secondary or foreign sources) are only **persuasive**.
- Rhetoric is an **acquired skill**, not a gift: "There is nothing like a born lawyer." Lawyers are made by diligence, patience and application of legal techniques.
- **Value of logic:** it helps the lawyer **reason clearly, express himself precisely, put his thoughts firmly, and detect bad arguments**.
- **Syllogism (deductive logic):** a deductive argument from a **major premise** and a **minor premise** to a **conclusion** that necessarily follows. Deduction runs from the **general to the particular**.
- **Inductive logic:** from **particular cases to a general principle** (from the minor to the major premise).
- **Analogical reasoning:** comparing the issue with **closely similar earlier decisions**; a **loose, not strictly logical** form of reasoning.
- ***Non sequitur***: the fallacy where the conclusion does not follow from the premises.

**Syllogism layout (statute = major premise):**

| Part | Example |
|---|---|
| Major premise (the law) | Any trader who sells in Oyingbo market without a permit shall pay a fine of ₦20,000. |
| Minor premise (the facts) | Mama Bisi sold in Oyingbo market without a permit. |
| Conclusion | Mama Bisi shall pay a fine of ₦20,000. |

**3 limitations of syllogism:**
1. Logic is concerned with **validity, not truth**. Validity = the conclusion follows from the premises; truth = the facts actually exist.
2. **A wrong premise accepted as true leads inevitably to a wrong conclusion**, though the form is valid.
3. **A statement may be logical in one context and illogical in another:** "things equal to the same thing are equal to one another" works for 2 + 2 = 4 = 3 + 1, but "a man is a human being; a woman is a human being; therefore a man is a woman" is absurd.

**Explain more**

- **Lawyer advising on a statute uses deduction:** the section is the major premise, the client's facts the minor, the advice the conclusion. Classic illustration under the old CAMA 1990: "any person under 18 cannot join in forming a company; Seyi is under 18; so Seyi cannot join". Here "Seyi is under 18" is the **minor premise**.
- **Truth versus validity in court:** a witness may be **logical yet lying**; counsel must expose it, and a judge who misses the distinction gives undeserved weight to the evidence.
- Counsel who relies on a ₦50,000 penalty section that had been **repealed** has a valid form but a **false major premise**, so a wrong conclusion.
- **Why induction for case law:** one decision may not yield a clear major premise, so the lawyer examines several cases, shows they share features with his client's case, and argues they establish a general rule (e.g. three earlier negligence cases where defendants paid damages). Then he reasons deductively from that rule.
- **Lord Diplock in *Home Office v Dorset Yacht Co* (1970)** on whether a duty of care exists: **first stage analytical and inductive** (identify the characteristics A, B, C, D common to past cases where a duty was found); **second stage deductive and analytical** (convert that into "wherever A, B, C, D exist a duty arises" and test the present facts). The analyst must already know what he is looking for: the analogical element.

### Lesson 6.4: Practical reasoning and justification

**Must know**

- **Practical reasoning** differs from formal logic: (1) it is **not tied to deduction or induction**; (2) it makes **frequent use of analogy**; (3) it **weighs various considerations** before a justifiable conclusion; (4) the link between premise and conclusion need not be formally valid; (5) whether the conclusion is good or bad is judged by **society's prevailing values**, a verdict that may be reversed with time.
- **Oliver Wendell Holmes:** "The life of the law has not been logic but experience." Meaning: logic is **only part** of legal reasoning; rules are **modified or discarded** as experience and values change ("an endless succession of reason").
- **Factors judges use to justify decisions, apart from appeal to authority (4):**
  1. **Coherence** with existing principles and authorities (the more inconsistent, the less likely).
  2. Whether the consequences are acceptable in terms of **justice**.
  3. **Common sense** and the supposed view of a **reasonable man**.
  4. **Public policy** (the larger public good).
- A judge's **personal likes and dislikes** are **not** a proper justification.
- Because these factors are weighed, outcomes can be hard to predict: litigation is sometimes called **"a gamble"** (a party may lose at the High Court and Court of Appeal yet win at the Supreme Court).
- **Rhetoric and logic are the technical components of adjudication**, but courts now depart from them where justice demands. *Chinwendu v Mbamali* (1980), per Eso JSC: "Care should be taken by the court always not to sacrifice justice on the altar of technicalities."

**Explain more**

- Judges weigh the evidence, counsel's rhetoric and logic, and fit with standards and principles, then give a **reasoned judgment**. Logic is "only part of the story".
- An appellant seeking to overturn a ₦3,000,000 judgment only because counsel misnumbered paragraphs, after full argument on the merits, should fail: the court **decides on the merits**.
- **General Showboy problem** (death penalty for coup plotting; one earlier pardon; international calls for mercy): a judge may justify a lighter sentence by **justice** (death forecloses review), **public policy** (ill will abroad), **common sense and the reasonable man**, and the earlier pardon.

### Lesson 6.5: Judicial precedent

**Must know**

- **Stare decisis (judicial precedent):** a decision of a judge on a **question of law** binds **the judge himself and judges of lower courts** to decide the same question the same way.
- **Formula:** if the facts are A, B, C, D and the judge treats A, B, C as material, discards D and decides Y, then any later case whose material facts are A, B, C must also be decided Y (an extra immaterial fact E changes nothing).
- **Farrar:** English case law is the product of **practical reasoning** emerging from decision making, combining **reasoning by analogy** with **reasoning by rules**.
- **Binding precedent:** from a court of higher rank. **Persuasive precedent:** from a court of **coordinate jurisdiction** (one State High Court for another) or a **foreign court** (English, Ghanaian); learned writers likewise only persuade.
- **Ratio decidendi:** "the reason for the decision"; the legal rule on which the decision rests, or the principle without which the court would not have reached its decision. Only the ratio binds.
- **Goodhart's 5 rules:** the principle of a case (1) is not found in the reasons given; (2) is not found in the rule of law stated; (3) is not necessarily found from all the ascertainable facts and the decision; (4) **is found from the facts treated by the judge as material and his decision on them**; (5) requires knowing what facts were held **immaterial**.
- **Julius Stone** disagreed: finding the ratio is basically **choosing an appropriate level of generality**; a case contains many potential rationes (e.g. *Donoghue v Stevenson* read at levels such as agent of harm, vehicle of harm, plaintiff's injury).
- **Obiter dictum:** a remark made by the judge **"by the way"**, not necessary for the decision; **not binding**. Its weight depends on **the level of the court and the learning of the judge**. A Supreme Court statement made after inviting counsel to address it on the law is a superior species of obiter, likely to be followed.
- **Per incuriam:** literally "through carelessness"; a decision given **in ignorance of a relevant statute or binding precedent**. A court need not follow **its own** per incuriam decision, but **a lower court must still follow a higher court's decision even if per incuriam**.
- **Distinguishing:** pointing out an **essential (mostly factual) difference** between the present case and the earlier one, so the earlier case is not followed **but remains good law**. Differs from **overruling** or refusing to follow, which only a court not bound by the earlier decision may do.
- **Glanville Williams:** **non-restrictive** distinguishing keeps the earlier ratio intact but finds the facts materially different; **restrictive** distinguishing cuts down the earlier ratio by treating extra facts as material.
- **Do judges make law?** Settled: **yes, though retrospectively**, where no statute or precedent clearly applies (Cross). Mellish LJ: the whole of equity and nine-tenths of the common law were made by judges.

**5 factors affecting the weight of a precedent:** (1) **age** (older precedents are harder to disturb); (2) **status and composition** of the court; (3) **adequacy of the law report**; (4) **history** (later approval, whether fully argued, unjust or absurd consequences, changed social conditions); (5) **unanimity** versus dissent. Length of the judgment is not a factor.

**Hierarchy and precedent:**

| Court | Position |
|---|---|
| Supreme Court | Binds all courts to which the doctrine applies. Respects its own decisions but **may overrule one reached on a wrong principle** (*Bronik Motors v Wema Bank*, 1983; *Abdulkarim v Incar*, 1992) |
| Court of Appeal | Bound by its own decisions except (*Young v Bristol Aeroplane Co*, 1944): (1) it chooses between two conflicting decisions of its own; (2) it must refuse one that cannot stand with a higher court's decision; (3) it need not follow one given per incuriam. In criminal matters, not strictly bound but reluctant to depart |
| Federal High Court (formerly Federal Revenue Court) | Binds Magistrate and District Courts; bound by Court of Appeal and Supreme Court |
| State High Courts | Coordinate: one High Court's decision only **persuades** another (*Barclays Bank v Hassan*, 1961) |

**Explain more**

- **Superior courts of record, s.6(5)(a) to (i) 1999 Constitution (made the only superior courts of record by s.6(3)):** Supreme Court, Court of Appeal, Federal High Court, High Court of a State, Sharia Court of Appeal of a State, Customary Court of Appeal of a State (with the FCT equivalents and the National Industrial Court). Magistrate, Customary, Area and District Courts are inferior courts set up by laws authorised under s.6(5)(j) and (k).
- **Ratio in appellate courts (Obilade):** if the majority judgments rest on consistent but different principles, all those principles form the ratio; if on the same principle, that principle is the ratio; a proposition rejected by the majority is not the ratio. Where the court is **equally divided**, the appeal is dismissed: ***semper praesumitur pro negante*** ("the presumption is always in favour of the negative").
- A Chief Magistrate who thinks a binding High Court decision was per incuriam must **follow it**, though she may express doubt as obiter (*Board of Customs and Excise v Bolarinwa*). Per incuriam also covers overlooked binding precedent (*Nicholas v Penny*, 1950).
- "Had the goods been perishable I would have applied a different rule", said in a cement case, is **obiter**.
- A State High Court decision on a **state** matter does not bind magistrates in other states; on a **federal** matter it binds magistrates within and outside the state.
- **Overruling** sets aside an earlier **rule** in a later case; **reversing** sets aside the decision in the **same case** on appeal.
- **Problem pattern:** an Osun State High Court judge who rejects a Supreme Court decision as wrong and treats a contrary House of Lords decision as binding **errs twice**: the Supreme Court binds him; English decisions are at most persuasive in Nigeria.

### Lesson 6.6: Approach to problems; reasoning in legislation

**Must know**

- **Problem questions: IRAC.** **I**ssue, **R**ule, **A**pplication, **C**onclusion (also IPAC, with Principle for Rule). Identify each issue, state the governing law with authorities, apply it to the facts, conclude and advise.
- **Essay questions: IDBC.** **I**ntroduction, **D**efinition, **B**ody, **C**onclusion. Give an authoritative definition; one point per paragraph with cases; always conclude.
- **Reasons for new legislation (5):** (1) dynamic nature of society; (2) **change in political and economic ideologies** of government (e.g. a military-to-civilian transition: the Petroleum Trust Fund was abolished); (3) **global shifts in value systems** (women's and children's rights, environment); (4) **scientific and technological breakthroughs** (computers, the internet); (5) **arrest of anti-social behaviour** (the **Advance Fee Fraud** law against "419"; failed banks laws; NDLEA).
- **Ambiguity:** a provision bears **two or more meanings**, each deducible from the text (lexical, syntactic, contextual). **Vagueness:** the meaning is **cloudy, indeterminate and confusing**; it is **worse** than ambiguity because context can hardly cure it.

**Explain more**

- "Mr Ade sued Mr Ola in the Ibadan High Court citing only a House of Lords decision. Advise Mr Ola." Use **IRAC**: issue (is an English decision binding?), rule (precedent binds only within a hierarchy; foreign decisions persuade), application, conclusion (persuasive only).

### One-minute recap

- Legal reasoning: systematic, logical, persuasive thinking on law; language usually general (s.316 Criminal Code), sometimes specific (s.6(5) Constitution); concepts save words.
- Principles ground rules (natural justice: audi alteram partem, nemo judex in causa sua); standards (reasonable man) flex with each case.
- Rhetoric: Plato's definition; lawyers forensic, judges deliberative; principal device is appeal to authority.
- Deduction: general to particular (syllogism); induction: particular to general (case law); analogy is loose.
- Syllogism limits: validity not truth; false premise, false conclusion; context.
- Practical reasoning; Holmes's "experience"; four justifying factors; *Chinwendu v Mbamali*.
- Precedent: ratio (Goodhart, Stone), obiter, per incuriam, distinguishing; *Young v Bristol Aeroplane* exceptions. Problems: IRAC; essays: IDBC.
