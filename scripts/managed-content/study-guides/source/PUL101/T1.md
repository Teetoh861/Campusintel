## Topic 1: Law in Social Context

**What you must know:** what legal method is; the meaning, definitions and features of law; every school of thought (positivist, pure theory, natural law, historical, sociological, utilitarian, functional, realist, Marxist), its jurists, its criticisms and its relevance today; the evolution and functions of law; the four theories on the functions of law; law and morality.

### Lesson 1.1: Meaning, definitions and features of law

**Must know**

- **Legal method:** an attempt to explain or analyse the technique of "thinking like a lawyer": studying the use and construction of legal rules to see how law is organised to achieve its objectives.
- **General sense of "law":** law of a club, law of God, law of demand and supply. **Technical sense:** the sense used in legal studies.
- **Working (institutional) definition:** "a rule or body of rules made by institutions, bodies and persons vested with the power to make such rules which are binding and enforced among the members of a given state or society."

| Jurist | Definition |
|---|---|
| John Austin | "A command set by a superior being to inferior beings and enforced by sanctions" |
| H.L.A. Hart | The union of primary rules of obligation and secondary rules of recognition |
| Cicero | The highest reason, rooted in nature, which commands what must be done and forbids the opposite |
| Thomas Aquinas | "An ordinance of reason for the common good, made by him who has care of the community" |
| Aristotle | Reason without desire (free from passion) |
| Blackstone | "A rule of action dictated by some superior being"; municipal law is a rule of civil conduct prescribed by the supreme power in a state, commanding what is right and prohibiting what is wrong |
| Salmond | The body of principles recognised and applied by the state in the administration of justice ("the rules recognised and acted on in courts of justice") |
| Roscoe Pound | A social institution for satisfying human wants (social engineering) |
| O.W. Holmes Jr. | The prediction (prophecy) of what the courts will do in fact |

**6 features of law:**

1. **A body of rules:** in many sources (Constitution, statutes, case law), not one book.
2. **Man-made:** values (religion, morality, custom) are not law unless traceable to institutional sources: Constitution, statute, case law, delegated legislation.
3. **Normative:** it guides what we may, ought or ought not to do (shared with moral and religious rules).
4. **Element of coercion:** breach is sanctioned through organised institutions (police, courts, prisons). This is the **main factor distinguishing law from moral or ethical rules**, enforced only by social disapproval.
5. **Territorial limitation:** binds only within its territory.
6. **Dynamic:** it changes as social, political and economic conditions change.

**Explain more**

- A church in Ibadan that suspends members who refuse to fast has a sanction, but its rule is **not law in the technical sense** because it is not traceable to the Constitution, statutes or case law.
- **Territorial limitation:** in *Holman v Johnson* (1775), tea was sold in Dunkirk to a buyer who planned to smuggle it into England. The seller recovered the price: "no country ever takes notice of the revenue laws of another country." In Nigeria the Criminal Code applies in the South and the Penal Code in the North, so a Muslim from Kano who drinks alcohol in Lagos cannot be prosecuted under the Penal Code.
- **Dynamic:** colonial law differed from law after independence in 1960; military-era law differed from law under elected governments.
- **No final definition:** five blind men each touched a different part of an elephant and described it differently: all correct, yet wrong. Each definition of law is shaped by the definer's perspective (judge, legislator, accused). Okunniga: "Nobody ... will ever be able to offer a definition of law to end all definitions." 
- **About the theories in general:** a jurist's ideas may embrace more than one theory; virtually all legal systems have applied the theories in varying degrees (so no theory belongs to one system alone); the theories are often **implicit**, not explicit ("the President has the power to introduce a fuel tax" implicitly reflects Austin).

### Lesson 1.2: Positivism, Hart and the Pure Theory of Law

**Must know**

- **Positive law:** from "posit" (to put or place): law put or imposed by rulers: the Constitution, statutes, case law, administrative rules. Positivists separate law as it is from law as it ought to be; an unjust law properly made is still valid law.
- **John Austin** (*The Province of Jurisprudence Determined*): command theory. **3 elements:**
  1. a **definite sovereign** (one person, a group or many);
  2. the sovereign is **without legal limitation**, above the law: the **"uncommanded commander"** (the highest authority, subject to no one's command);
  3. the subjects are in the **habit of obeying** him because of his power to impose **sanctions**.
- "Law must agree with morality or natural justice" is **not** an element.

**4 criticisms of Austin:**

1. Not all laws are commands: the law on **wills** does not compel anyone to make a will; one may marry under the Marriage Act or customary law.
2. Law is whatever the sovereign says, regardless of its goodness or the lawmaker's character, so it can justify **dictatorship**.
3. No one is above the law, even a military government: *Lakanmi v A.G. (Western State)* (1971), where the Supreme Court declared the Forfeiture of Assets (Validation) Decree No. 45 of 1968 null and void.
4. People do not obey only from fear of sanction: many do not smoke in public or kill because it is not their way of life.

- **H.L.A. Hart** (*The Concept of Law*, 1961): law = union of **primary rules** (rules of obligation: do not steal, pay tax) and **secondary rules** (rules about rules: **recognition**, **change**, **adjudication**). The constitutional rules on how a bill becomes an Act are secondary rules.
- **Hans Kelsen** (1881-1973), **Pure Theory of Law:** law is a **hierarchy of norms**; each norm is valid because a higher norm created it, up to the **grundnorm** (basic norm), which is presupposed, not enacted, and whose validity cannot be traced to any other norm. Law must be studied as a self-contained system, pure of morality, politics and sociology; the moral quality of a rule is irrelevant to its validity. Kelsen called the command theory nonsensical.
- **Kelsen's chain:** murder is forbidden because the Criminal Code says so; the Code is valid because the legislature enacted it (section 4, 1999 Constitution); the legislature is valid because it was constituted under the Constitution; the Constitution is valid because it was promulgated as the Act of the people.

**3 criticisms of Kelsen:**

1. It stresses **formal validity** rather than the content and effects of law; it can serve dictators.
2. The grundnorm is hard to identify (who made the Constitution: the military or the people?).
3. After a successful coup, validity depends on the **effectiveness** of the new legal order, not the old Constitution (*The State v Dosso* (1958); later rejected in *Jilani v Government of Punjab* (1972)); a failed coup means treason charges.

**Explain more**

- "Grossly unjust rules are not law" is a **natural law** claim, never Kelsen's or Austin's.
- A properly enacted levy of ₦5,000 that only benefits the rich is, to a strict positivist, valid law though unjust.
- Hart's **"minimum content of natural law"**: rules every society needs because of human vulnerability, approximate equality, limited altruism, limited resources and limited understanding and strength of will.
- **Relevance today:** positivism explains why courts apply enacted law and why the Constitution is supreme; Kelsen explains the hierarchy of Constitution, Acts and subsidiary legislation, and how courts treated coups as creating a new grundnorm.

### Lesson 1.3: Natural law and the historical school

**Must know**

- **Natural law:** at its simplest, what is **fair, just or right**. There are objective principles in every person, whatever race or colour, telling them what is fair, just or right, deduced by **reason** from the nature of man, society and things, and existing irrespective of human enactment. Man-made law that conflicts with them should not command obedience: natural law tests the **validity** of man-made law.
- **Chief protagonists:** Zeno, Thomas Aquinas, Grotius (also Cicero).
- **Okunniga's illustration:** ten men from different countries, put in separate rooms and asked in their own language whether stealing is good: most say no.
- **Historical school:** led by **Friedrich Carl von Savigny** (1779-1861), a German aristocrat with an interest in the status quo. Developed to **counter natural law**, which was used in 17th and 18th century Europe to overthrow monarchs. Every people has a **volksgeist** (spirit of the people); for law to be valid it must accord with the history, customs and way of life of the people. Law should be a formal restatement of custom and should evolve, not come by revolution.

**Criticisms of natural law:**

1. It stresses what **ought** to be done, not what is done. Courts apply the formal sources; unless its principles are enacted into law, their violation cannot be legally penalised.
2. "Right reason" cannot be verified empirically, so the powerful interpret it.
3. **Multiple conscience problem:** different people hold different ideas of what is fair or just on the same issue; it has defended tyranny, slavery, colonialism and apartheid as well as liberation (hence called "a harlot"), and could lead to anarchy.

**Criticisms of the historical school:**

1. It suggests the past, not the will and objectives of society, decides its law; it can block desirable radical reforms.
2. It assumes customs are rational and fair, yet some customs subject people to servitude and prejudice women in inheritance and labour.
3. Modern diverse societies need a force above custom to reconcile customs.

**3 validity tests for customary law in Nigeria:** a rule must not be

1. repugnant to natural justice, equity and good conscience;
2. contrary to public policy;
3. incompatible, directly or by necessary implication, with any law for the time being in force.

(There is no requirement that it be written down, approved by the National Assembly, or match English law.)

**Explain more**

- **Relevance today:** natural law is the basis of **equality, human rights and democracy**: it was invoked in the American War of Independence, the French Revolution, African independence struggles, and by Niger Delta groups demanding a fairer share of oil revenue or resource control. Chapter IV (fundamental rights) of the 1999 Constitution reflects it.
- **Historical school in practice:** if a state suddenly bans a centuries-old burial custom, people will ignore it or find clever ways round it, exactly as Savigny predicted.

### Lesson 1.4: Sociological, utilitarian, functional and realist schools

**Must know**

- **Sociological school** (mid-19th to 20th century):
  - **Eugen Ehrlich** (1862-1922): law rests on the **"facts of law"**, meaning **how people actually behave**. Society's conduct determines the law, not the sovereign's rules; the **living law** dominates social life. "The centre of gravity of legal development lies not in legislation nor juristic science nor in judicial decisions but the society itself."
  - **Roscoe Pound**, former Dean of Harvard Law School: law satisfies human wants by **balancing competing claims** to limited resources with the least harm or disruption to established institutions (**social engineering**); his main focus was the courts.
- **Utilitarian school:** **Jeremy Bentham** (1748-1832): law should promote the **greatest good (happiness) of the greatest number**. **4 utilities:** security, equality, liberty, abundance; **security first**, liberty second. John Stuart Mill refined it (higher and lower pleasures).
- **Functional school:** an outgrowth of the sociological school, founded on pragmatism. Law is what the court will do in a particular case. **O.W. Holmes Jr.** viewed law from the standpoint of the **"bad man"**, who cares nothing for legal theory and wants only to know what the courts will do. It recognises judges' law-making power.
- **Realist school** (American; began in the 1920s, peaked in the 1930s): focused on **trial courts** to reform the judiciary. Judges and juries are influenced by **extralegal factors**: way of life, social and educational background, likes and dislikes, dress, skin colour, age, attitude to precedent, and the personality of the accused and his lawyer. **Exponents:** Holmes, Jerome Frank, John Chipman Gray, Karl Llewellyn. **Main goal:** make judges conscious of their hidden prejudices. "Law is the prediction of what the courts will do in fact."

| School | Criticism |
|---|---|
| Sociological (Ehrlich) | Law also shapes conduct (car owners register vehicles and stop at roadblocks because the law says so); hard to measure law's effect; denies formal sources creative value; "everyone else is doing it" is no defence |
| Pound | No method to identify and weigh interests; balances differently at different times; favours stability and short-run expediency (liberty may give way to security) |
| Utilitarian | Does not show how to balance individual and communal interests |
| Functional | Looks at courts alone and neglects the legislature and agencies, though courts are themselves created by law |

**Explain more**

- **Ehrlich in Nigeria:** anti-corruption laws exist, yet some police officers openly collect money (₦100) from motorists at checkpoints; the Child's Rights Act 2003 has not ended child labour. To Ehrlich, the real law is what people do.
- **Utilitarian example:** searching every airport passenger without suspicion: a small loss of liberty buys greater security for all.
- **Realist examples:** a lawyer telling his "area boy" client to dress responsibly and drop his dark glasses in court; "Mr A's case may be weak, but a celebrated human rights lawyer and a judge of his faith will make a difference."
- Holmes appears in both the functional and realist schools. Savigny is never a realist.

### Lesson 1.5: Evolution, functions and theories on the functions of law

**Must know**

- **Evolution:** a hermit needs no law, but once two people live together interests conflict. First **self-help** (reprisals, feuds; might was right); then customs administered by monarchs or elders with unorganised sanctions (ostracism, ridicule); today government administers law through courts, police and bureaucracy.

**4 functions of law:**

1. **Definition and regulation of social relationships:** marriage is between a man and a woman; prolonged cohabitation does not make a couple husband and wife; freedom of association (section 40) is regulated (CAMA for companies).
2. **Identification and allocation of official authority:** sections 4, 5 and 6 of the 1999 Constitution vest legislative, executive and judicial powers.
3. **Dispute settlement and remedies:** courts and tribunals; remedies for breach of contract (damages, specific performance, injunction, rescission, indemnity).
4. **Change of law:** section 9 (amending the Constitution), section 58 (making new laws), subsidiary legislation for minor changes.

(Other lists add crime prevention and control, business transactions and interstate engagement.)

**4 theories on the functions of law** (they focus on law's function, not its origin):

| Model | Core idea | Criticism |
|---|---|---|
| **Consensus** (Talcott Parsons) | Law protects values everyone shares; society is unitary; Parliament represents all; law is equal and just to all; conflicts are only personal | No society has one shared value system; "consensus" may hide sectional interests (oil revenue arrangements) |
| **Conflict / pluralist** | No shared values; competing groups with roughly **equal bargaining power**; the state is a **neutral arbiter** | Groups are not equal (Pareto: 10% own 90% of resources); access to justice is skewed (lawyers are costly, courts seem elitist) |
| **Open** (Dahrendorf) | Middle way: conflict continues but is resolved through legitimate processes: negotiation, arbitration, judicial decision, elections, strikes, **never revolution** | (A middle position between the consensus and conflict extremes) |
| **Marxist** (Karl Marx) | Stability comes from the capitalist class exploiting the working class; law is a tool of the ruling class | No guarantee that overthrowing the ruling class brings equality; an invitation to repeated revolution |

**Explain more**

- **Dispute settlement varies with society.** In community-based societies relationships continue, so disputes end in **compromise**. In a complex society, Mr A, paralysed by a stranger company's tanker, wants no future relationship, so the claim is settled on a **"winner takes all"** basis.
- **Why workers accept domination (Marx):** the ruling class controls (1) the instruments of **coercion** (army, police, law) and (2) the **dominant ideas**: "the ideas of the ruling class are in every epoch the ruling ideas."
- Workers and employers who settle wage disputes by negotiation, strikes, arbitration, courts and elections, never revolution, fit the **open model**.

### Lesson 1.6: Law and morality

**Must know**

- **Morality (Phil Harris):** "code of beliefs, values, principles and standards of behaviour, some versions of which are found in all social groups." Moral standards are usually higher than legal ones.
- **Relativity of moral ideas:** groups differ on right and wrong; respect for elders is strict in villages but relaxed in Lagos and Port Harcourt.
- **Lord Devlin:** societies disintegrate when common morality is lost; "The suppression of vice is as much the law's business as the suppression of subversive activities."
- **Crimes without victims** (drug addiction, abortion, homosexuality): the clash between individual liberty and public morality.
- ***Shaw v DPP*** [1962]: Shaw published "The Ladies' Directory" advertising prostitutes and was convicted of **conspiracy to corrupt public morals**. Viscount Simonds: the court has "a residual power to enforce the supreme and fundamental purpose of the law, to conserve not only the safety and order, but also the moral welfare of the State." The wrong was public advertisement, not prostitution itself.
- ***Knuller v DPP*** [1973]: the Sexual Offences Act 1967 decriminalised private homosexual acts between consenting adult males, but the House of Lords held it gave **no licence to publicly encourage** them (Lord Reid); the publishers of *International Times* were convicted.

**Explain more**

- Moral rules become law when pressure groups win over the legislature; the law must then be enforced, or the moral code is "observed only in its breach" (campus dress codes).

### One-minute recap

- Legal method = thinking like a lawyer; law in the technical sense comes from institutional sources.
- Six features: body of rules, man-made, normative, coercive (main mark of law), territorial (*Holman v Johnson*), dynamic.
- Austin: sovereign, no legal limitation, habit of obedience; refuted by wills, *Lakanmi*, non-fearful obedience.
- Hart: primary and secondary rules. Kelsen: hierarchy of norms to the grundnorm; formal, hard to locate, fails after coups.
- Natural law: what is just, tested by reason; basis of human rights; multiple conscience problem.
- Savigny: volksgeist; customary law tests: natural justice, public policy, incompatibility.
- Ehrlich: facts of law, living law; Pound: balancing interests; Bentham: greatest happiness, security first.
- Functional (Holmes's bad man) and realist (Frank, Llewellyn, Gray: extralegal factors).
- Functions: social relationships, official authority, disputes and remedies, change. Models: consensus, conflict, open, Marxist.
- Devlin; *Shaw v DPP*; *Knuller v DPP*.

### Theory practice

**Question:** "Law is like the proverbial elephant; you can only describe it from the perspective you choose." Discuss the schools of thought on the meaning of law and their relevance today.

*Answer outline:*

- **Introduction:** technical meaning of law; the five blind men; Okunniga; theories overlap and are often implicit.
- **Positivism:** Austin's definition and three elements; criticisms (wills, dictatorship, *Lakanmi v A.G. (Western State)* (1971), obedience without fear); Hart's primary and secondary rules. Relevance: supremacy of enacted law.
- **Pure theory:** Kelsen's grundnorm and the Criminal Code chain; criticisms (formalism, locating the grundnorm, coups). Relevance: hierarchy of laws.
- **Natural law:** Aquinas, Cicero, Grotius; criticisms (ought not is, multiple conscience). Relevance: Chapter IV rights, Niger Delta agitation.
- **Historical:** Savigny's volksgeist; criticisms. Relevance: customary law and its three validity tests.
- **Others, one point and one criticism each:** Ehrlich (checkpoint extortion), Pound (balancing), Bentham (airport searches), Holmes and Frank (extralegal factors).
- **Conclusion:** each school sees one part of the elephant; Nigerian law draws on all; offer your own definition joining source, sanction and justice.
