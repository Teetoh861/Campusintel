## Topic 4: Methods of Social Control through Law

**What you must know:** the meaning of social control and who coined it; Pound, Ross, Quinney and Elias on law; formal and informal control; why law is (or is not) the paramount agent; the seven techniques (Summers' five plus Farrar and Dugdale's two), each with its definition, Nigerian examples, cases and alternatives; the limits of law as a tool of social control.

### Lesson 4.1: The idea of social control

**Must know**

- **Social control:** loosely, the control of **social behaviour**, that is, behaviour that **affects others**. Social order is a wider, related idea; order must exist before any system of control.
- The term was coined by sociologists. **Edward A. Ross** first used it for regulative institutions that keep individual behaviour in line with group demands (beliefs, ceremonies, public opinion, morals, art, education, law). **William G. Sumner** used it to show how **folkways** (habits and custom) control conduct.
- **Otite and Ogionwo:** social control is the various means by which a society exercises authority over its members and enforces conformity to its norms.
- **Roscoe Pound:** law is "**social control through the systematic application of the force of politically organised society**"; "in the modern world law has become the **paramount agent** of social control". His **social engineering** idea: law as a tool for **balancing and harmonising competing social interests**.
- **Ross:** law is "the most specialised and highly finished engine of control employed by society".
- **Quinney:** the legal system is the most explicit form of social control. Law consists of **3 elements**: (1) specific rules of conduct; (2) planned use of sanctions to support the rules; (3) designated officials to interpret and enforce the rules. Law grows more important as societies grow more complex.
- **Elias:** the law of a community is "the body of rules regulating human conduct regarded as obligatory or binding by its members" (it covers customary law too).

| Formal control | Informal control |
|---|---|
| Law: rules, sanctions, courts, police, prisons | Ridicule, gossip, ostracism, censure (used spontaneously) |

**Advantages of law** over other instruments (public opinion, religion, morality, custom): **wealth of detailed rules; explicit sanctions; regularised procedure; organised institutions and machinery for enforcement**.

**Functions of law as an instrument of social control:** maintains public order and suppresses deviant behaviour; facilitates co-operative action; constitutes and regulates the principal organs of power; communicates, regulates and reinforces social values.

**Explain more**

- Pound admitted law is not the only agency: household, church, school, trade and professional associations all help, "but the brunt of the task falls on the legal order".
- Villagers refusing to trade with a family or let children mix with them = **informal control by ostracism**, not a legal technique. Imprisonment after trial is formal.
- **Nwabueze:** social control is a means to an end, **social justice**; it prevents undue concentration of wealth and exploitation; "an unregulated freedom of private enterprise is nothing but social anarchy".
- Otite and Ogionwo's sociological sanctions: physical (punishment, expulsion, extermination), economic, psychological and anticipated sanctions.

### Lesson 4.2: The seven techniques; the penal technique and its alternatives

**Must know**

**Robert S. Summers** gave **5** basic techniques: penal, grievance-remedial, private arranging, administrative-regulatory, public-benefit conferral. **Farrar and Dugdale** added **2**: **constitutive** and **fiscal**, and renamed the last one **conferral of social benefits**. The **7**:

1. Penal
2. Grievance-remedial
3. Private arranging
4. Constitutive
5. Administrative-regulatory
6. Fiscal
7. Conferral of social benefits (public benefit conferral)

"Judicial precedent technique", "warning" and "compounding" are **not** among the seven. The techniques are found in **both legislation and judge-made law**.

- **Penal technique:** spells out **prohibited conduct, the penalty for breach, the procedure for determining guilt and the appropriate punishment**. The State plays the active role. The **criminal justice system** applies it: rules on criminal acts and omissions, the **Police** and other enforcement agencies, **prisons** and detention centres. (Insurance companies are not part of it.)
- Substantive law: Criminal Code (South), Penal Code (North), other statutes, Sharia codes in some Northern States. Procedure: Criminal Procedure Act and Criminal Procedure Code (now largely the ACJA 2015).

**Alternatives to the penal system (4):**

1. **Non-intervention:** society cannot and should not criminalise every immoral act; the legislature chooses. *Aoko v Fagbemi* [1961] 1 All NLR 400: adultery not a crime because it was **not defined, with its penalty prescribed, in any written law** (s.36(12) 1999 Constitution). Police also encourage family members in minor matters to settle privately.
2. **Warning or caution:** for child or petty offenders; a judge warns a noisy spectator instead of committing him for contempt; binding-over orders.
3. **Reciprocity and self-help:** "an eye for an eye"; Farrar and Dugdale call it "a symptom of an underdeveloped system". Mob "jungle justice" is dangerous: an **innocent person may be punished without fair hearing**, and the perpetrators are liable civilly and criminally.
4. **Compounding:** receiving property or other consideration in return for an agreement **not to prosecute**. It needs a **compromise between offender and complainant**.

**Explain more**

- How the penal technique controls: the victim reports to the police **instead of taking vengeance**; police investigate; the police or the **Attorney-General** (ss.174 and 211 CFRN) decide whether to prosecute; the accused gets a fair hearing; guilt must be proved beyond reasonable doubt; punishment is fine, imprisonment or death.
- It applies to all, high and low, and the **statute of limitation does not run against crime**: fraud from 12 years ago can still be prosecuted. Civil actions, by contrast, become statute-barred after about 5, 6 or 10 years depending on the claim.
- Compounding examples: relatives of an accident victim drop charges when the driver promises ₦150,000; Mr A drops a burglary case on finding his cousin was the burglar; an employer dismisses a fraudulent employee instead of prosecuting. **Oputa JSC** in *Federal Civil Service Commission v Laoye* [1989] 2 NWLR (Pt 106) 652: justice is reached through finding the truth, and the instrument for finding the truth of a criminal charge is **trial in open court**; prosecute first, dismiss after.
- Problem trap: a cashier refunds stolen co-operative funds and begs, but the society's committee insists on prosecution. Compounding is **unavailable** because there is no compromise with the complainant.

### Lesson 4.3: The grievance-remedial technique and its alternatives

**Must know**

- **Summers:** it "**defines remediable grievances, specifies remedies… and provides for enforcement of remedial awards**". It operates mainly in **civil law**: contract, family law, commercial law, torts, property, equity and trusts, labour law (not criminal law). Procedure: civil procedure, civil remedies, legal advice and aid.
- **Remedies:** general damages, special damages, specific performance, injunction, *restitutio in integrum*. Imprisonment is **not** one.
- **Logical basis:** an adequately remedied person is pacified, which discourages injustice and stops people taking the law into their own hands.

**Alternatives (4):**

1. **Penal technique:** some wrongs are crimes and torts (assault, false imprisonment, defamation). **Rule in *Smith v Selwyn* [1914] 3 KB 98:** where the wrong is a **felony**, no tort action until the defendant has been **prosecuted or a reasonable excuse is shown** for not prosecuting. Applied in *Nwankwo v Ajaegbu* (1978); not followed in *Tika Tore Press Ltd v Umar* [1968] and *Panthinsan v Edet* [1968]; described as "archaic" and "anachronistic".
2. **Private settlement:** e.g. a **liquidated damages clause** stating exactly what a defaulter pays.
3. **Insurance:** drivers with cover refer a collision to their insurers.
4. **Arbitration:** where a contract has an arbitration clause, going to court first is **premature**; courts set aside awards only within narrow limits.

**Worked example (liquidated damages):** clause = ₦50,000 per day late; delivery 3 days late. Payable = 3 × ₦50,000 = **₦150,000**, paid without litigation.

**Explain more**

- **Advantages of arbitration** (Lusk): (1) much quicker disposal; (2) an arbitrator familiar with the technical background can be chosen; (3) informal, so lawyers are not essential; (4) less executive time and lower costs. Also **secrecy and informality**, and amicable resolution that keeps harmony. Trap: it is **not always cheaper**; setting an award aside in court adds cost.

### Lesson 4.4: The private arranging and constitutive techniques

**Must know**

- **Private arranging:** the law provides a **framework of rules for the validity of private transactions**, leaving individuals to arrange their affairs within it. Mainly civil law: contract, family, commercial, property, partnership, company law, trusts. Examples: **marriage, settling a trust, making a will**.
- **Will:** in writing (no special form), **signed by the testator in the presence of at least two witnesses present at the same time**, who attest and subscribe. Otherwise invalid, and property passes under the rules of **intestacy**.
- **Marriage:** free choice of Marriage Act, customary or Islamic marriage, but a person in a **subsisting statutory marriage cannot validly contract another marriage under any system** (Marriage Act).
- **Constitutive technique** (added by Farrar and Dugdale): its distinctive feature is the **creation of a new legal person** separate from its members, which brings **perpetual succession and limited liability**. It is an **offshoot** of private arranging: no one must form a company, but those who do must meet the law's requirements, so it "takes off where private arranging stops".
- ***Salomon v Salomon & Co Ltd* [1897] AC 22 (House of Lords):** a duly incorporated company is a legal person **separate and distinct from its members**.

**Explain more**

- **Salomon facts:** Salomon sold his boot business (1892) to a company formed with his wife and five children (7 members then required) for £39,000: £10,000 in secured debentures, 20,000 £1 shares, £9,000 cash. He held 2,001 of 2,007 shares. On liquidation, assets £10,000, liabilities £17,000 (£10,000 owed to Salomon). The trial court and Court of Appeal called the company a sham; the House of Lords unanimously reversed: the company was a separate person and Salomon, a **secured debenture holder**, was paid first. Lord Halsbury LC: "Either the company was a legal entity or it was not." The principle is not a licence for fraud.
- Shareholders of a failed company are generally not personally liable for its debts (Salomon).
- Used by government too: statutory corporations, universities, agencies; and for religious and cultural bodies as **incorporated trustees** (CAMA). Groups recognised **without** legal personality: partnerships, labour unions, **political parties**, professional bodies.
- **Government intervention in private arranging:** Child Rights Act 2003; sale of goods; **Land Use Act** (Governor's consent to assign or mortgage a right of occupancy); **Hire Purchase Act 1965, s.9**: once **three-fifths (3/5)** of the hire-purchase price is paid, the owner can recover the goods **only by court action**. Before the Act, seizure was lawful in *Atere v Amao* (1957) (£10 of £1,332 unpaid) and *Sanyaolu v Bentworth Finance* (1972) (£100 of £5,486 unpaid).

**Worked example (the 3/5 rule):** price ₦4,000,000; paid ₦2,600,000. Fraction paid = 2,600,000 ÷ 4,000,000 = 0.65 (65%). Threshold = 3/5 × 4,000,000 = ₦2,400,000 (60%). 65% is more than 60% (but less than three-quarters), so the owner may recover only by **court action**. Method: divide amount paid by total price and compare with 0.6. In *Atere*, paid fraction = 1,322 ÷ 1,332 ≈ 99.2%; in *Sanyaolu*, 5,386 ÷ 5,486 ≈ 98.2% (rounded to one decimal place): under s.9 both owners would now need a court order.

### Lesson 4.5: Administrative-regulatory, fiscal and social benefit techniques

**Must know**

- **Administrative-regulatory:** government agencies **adopt regulatory standards, communicate them to private operators and ensure compliance**. Steps: **licensing, inspection, warning letters, revocation of licence**, then administrative proceedings, civil litigation or **criminal prosecution as the last resort**. Examples: Ministry of Education permit for a private school; **NAFDAC** approval before producing consumables; Standards Organisation of Nigeria; DPR; Weights and Measures; **NCC** licences for mobile telephone operators.
- **Fiscal:** taxation's classical aim is revenue; it is also used to **redistribute income, stimulate economic growth and discourage anti-social behaviour**. Examples: the 1998 budget raised the penalty for **gas flaring by 1,900 per cent**; income tax and capital transfer tax narrow the gap between haves and have-nots. Tax administration by the federal and State revenue authorities, and customs and excise laws, also serve the fiscal technique.
- **Conferral of social benefits:** government provides **social amenities and welfare** (roads, education, electricity, housing, health care, social security) **without directly regulating conduct**, expecting improved welfare to bring order and prevent apathy and strife.

| Comparison | Administrative-regulatory | Other technique |
|---|---|---|
| v penal | Regulates **wholesome** activities | Prohibits and sanctions anti-social conduct |
| v grievance-remedial | **Preventive**: acts before a grievance arises | Remedies after the wrong (e.g. consumer sues a negligent manufacturer) |

**Worked example (percentage increase):** an increase of 1,900% means new = old × (1 + 1,900/100) = old × 20. A penalty of 50 kobo per unit would become ₦10.

**Explain more**

- Food regulator inspecting factories = administrative-regulatory; a sick consumer suing = grievance-remedial.
- Social benefit programmes: Nomadic Education, National Mass Literacy, Low Cost Housing, National Primary Health Care, People's Bank, NDE, FEAP, OMPADEC, **NDDC**, NAPEP, NEEDS. **Weakness:** poor implementation and **hijacking by elites**, so only crumbs reach the target group.
- The **AGABA problem** (promoters sell an unapproved baby drug; children die; promoters hide behind the company) turns on the **administrative-regulatory** (no NAFDAC approval) and **constitutive** (*Salomon* is not a shield for fraud) techniques. A Niger Delta unrest problem turns on **conferral of social benefits** and **fiscal** techniques.

### Lesson 4.6: Overlap of techniques and limits of law

**Must know**

- The techniques **overlap and complement** each other; government may **combine** them, choosing the mix that best achieves its aim. They show the two main sources of modern law: legislation and judge-made law.
- **Limits of law** (why some sociologists deny it is paramount): some laws are seen **in breach more than in enforcement**; **dead-letter laws** in the statute books; enforcement does not always prevent the evil and may create new problems; **discretion of enforcement agencies**. Law's explicit sanctions are a strength, not a limit.
- Law cannot punish every immoral act (non-intervention); law is not the only agency (Pound); a law far out of step with popular conduct is "doomed as an instrument of social control" (Ehrlich).

**Explain more**

- **Pound's limits of effective legal action:** difficulty of ascertaining facts; many moral duties (such as gratitude) are too intangible to enforce; some injuries are too subtle (feelings, privacy); many relations (family life) lie beyond legal action; and law needs an external agency or person to set it in motion.
- In Nigeria, rising crime (419 fraud, kidnapping, corruption, trafficking) shows the penal technique's weakness in **enforcement**, not in its rules. Welfare laws fail without funding and honest implementation.

### One-minute recap

- Social control: control of behaviour affecting others; coined by Ross; Sumner's folkways.
- Pound: "social control through the systematic application of the force of politically organised society"; paramount agent; social engineering balances interests.
- Quinney's 3 elements; Ross's "highly finished engine"; Elias's definition.
- Seven techniques: Summers' five plus Farrar and Dugdale's constitutive and fiscal.
- Penal alternatives: non-intervention (*Aoko v Fagbemi*), warning, self-help, compounding (*FCSC v Laoye*).
- Grievance-remedial alternatives: penal (*Smith v Selwyn*), private settlement, insurance, arbitration.
- Private arranging (wills, marriage; HP Act 3/5); constitutive (*Salomon*).
- Administrative-regulatory is preventive (NAFDAC, NCC); fiscal (gas flaring +1,900%); social benefits (NDDC; elite hijacking).
- Techniques overlap; law has limits.

### Theory practice

**Question:** Analysing the role of law as an instrument of social control, discuss any four methods of social control through law, with Nigerian examples.

*Answer outline:*
- **Introduction:** define social control (Ross, Otite and Ogionwo); Pound's definition and "paramount agent"; Quinney's three elements; law's advantages and functions; list the seven techniques (Summers; Farrar and Dugdale).
- **Penal:** prohibited conduct, penalty, procedure, punishment; police, Attorney-General, courts, prisons; Criminal and Penal Codes; alternatives with *Aoko v Fagbemi* and *FCSC v Laoye*.
- **Grievance-remedial:** Summers' definition; remedies; logical basis; *Smith v Selwyn*; arbitration.
- **Constitutive:** new legal person; *Salomon v Salomon* [1897]; incorporated trustees, universities.
- **Administrative-regulatory:** standards, licensing, inspection; NAFDAC, NCC, school permits; preventive, unlike grievance-remedial.
- **Conclusion:** techniques overlap and combine; note limits (dead-letter laws, weak enforcement, elite hijacking of welfare); law remains the most organised instrument of social control.
