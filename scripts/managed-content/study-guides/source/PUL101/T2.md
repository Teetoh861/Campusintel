## Topic 2: Objectives of Law, Law and Justice, Law and the State

**What you must know:** the objectives of law (order, justice, freedom); the definitions of justice and who gave them; every type of justice with examples; the state, its elements and government; ruler and citizens, sovereignty and legitimacy; the rule of law (Dicey) and other limits on state power.

### Lesson 2.1: Objectives of law: order and freedom

**Must know**

- **Law and order:** the **classical function of any government** is to maintain law and order, protect lives and property and ward off external aggression: the foremost justification for modern government. The opposite of order is anarchy and chaos, caused mainly by **non-enforcement** of law, not its absence.
- Even in **war**, law (the Geneva Conventions) sets minimum standards: protection for religious houses, the aged, pregnant women, children, the Red Cross and UN relief workers.
- **Law and freedom:** the desire to be free is innate. Law recognises freedom and regulates it; unrestricted freedom would destroy others' freedom. There is an irreducible minimum of liberty: **fundamental human rights** (Chapter IV, 1999 Constitution).
- **Rights are not absolute:** they are balanced against others' rights. Freedom of speech does not cover malicious falsehood (defamation).

**3 limits on freedom of contract:**

1. a contract is **void** if it contemplates a **crime** or is contrary to **public policy**;
2. the law may **control prices** of essential goods;
3. a trader may be barred from a **trade name** or mark closely resembling an existing one.

**3 limits on political freedom:**

1. some people may not vote, e.g. infants and lunatics (sections 77(2) and 117(2): only citizens aged **18** and above may register);
2. criteria for registering political parties (section 222);
3. disqualification from some offices (sections 66, 107, 137, 182).

**Explain more**

- Forcing anyone to accept a trader's offer is **not** a limit on freedom of contract; freedom includes the right to refuse.
- Seventeen-year-old Ifeoma turned away at voter registration suffers a **lawful limitation on political freedom**: naming "citizens aged eighteen" excludes those below (*expressio unius est exclusio alterius*).
- The degree of freedom depends on the state's political and economic system (socialist or capitalist).

### Lesson 2.2: Law and justice: meaning and definitions

**Must know**

- **Relationship:** in ordinary speech law and justice are synonyms, but **justice is the correct application of a law, as opposed to arbitrariness** (Ross). **Law is the means; justice is the end.**
- A law may be **legal yet unjust**: apartheid in South Africa; racial segregation laws in the US before 1964 (school segregation was declared unconstitutional in *Brown v Board of Education* (1954)).
- **Wole Soyinka:** justice is "the first condition of humanity."
- **Emily Thorne:** justice, like beauty, is **in the eye of the beholder**: the winner says justice was done, the loser calls it a miscarriage of justice.
- **Factors shaping a person's idea of justice:** home background, educational qualification, religious belief, enlightened self-interest, personal idiosyncrasies, socio-political affiliation.

| Thinker | Justice is |
|---|---|
| Ulpian (Roman jurist; Justinian's Institutes) | "The constant and perpetual will to render to everyone his due" |
| Thomas Aquinas | A cardinal virtue: "a habit whereby man renders to each one his due by a constant and perpetual will" |
| Aristotle | Following laid-down rules; two-fold: **conduct in agreement with law** and **equality** |
| Plato | When each person does that for which he or she is **fitted or suited** |
| John Rawls (*A Theory of Justice*, 1971) | **Fairness**; "the first virtue of social institutions, as truth is of systems of thought" |
| Professor Bix | A subset of moral concepts connected with law and politics (Lasswell: politics is "who gets what, when and how") |
| *Josiah v The State* [1985] | In criminal law, a **three-way traffic**: justice to the **state (society)**, the **victim** and the **accused** |

**Explain more**

- **Justice as equality according to law:** treating like cases alike (legal proceedings, universal adult suffrage); it reduces discrimination. But strict equality can work injustice: a mother who shares exactly equally among three children, one physically challenged.
- So **unequal treatment can itself be justice:** special treatment for insanity, infancy or poverty (legal aid for the poor; scholarships for the poorest). Justice as **fairness or equity** fixes this, but what is fair is subjective.
- **Plato's view abused:** India's caste system; the Osu system in Eastern Nigeria; claims of entitlement to rule; gender ideology.
- Society cannot satisfy everyone; someone wins and someone loses. Law must choose which interest prevails and balance the rest (Pound's "interests pressing for recognition").

### Lesson 2.3: Typologies of justice

**Must know**

| Type | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Commutative** (Aquinas) | Fairness in dealings between two persons; private law; voluntary transactions; **justice of coordination** | Enforcing a contract |
| **Distributive** (Aquinas, Aristotle) | State shares honours, rewards and common goods proportionately; public law; **justice of subordination**; **prospective** | Federal Character (section 14(3)); fuel-subsidy palliatives; derivation; local government autonomy |
| **Corrective** (Aristotle) | Rectifies a wrong where one took from or harmed another (contract, tort, crime); **retrospective** | Return of a stolen generator plus penalty; King Solomon and the two women; *FRN v P&ID* [2023] |
| **Procedural / technical** | Fairness of the **process**, not the outcome; "justice must be seen to be done" | Fair hearing, section 36 |
| **Formal** | Judge applies the law **strictly**, ignoring extra-legal factors, with **no discretion to soften harshness** | Awolowo treasonable felony trial: "my hands are tied" |
| **Substantive / substantial** | Where strict law gives absurdity or injustice, judge considers extra-judicial factors for a just **result** | *Chinwendu v Mbamali*: justice not sacrificed on the altar of technicalities |

- **Aquinas:** commutative and distributive. **Aristotle:** corrective and distributive.
- **Formal justice is justified because it:** (1) ensures **clarity and certainty** (Lord Eldon in *Sheddon v Goodrich* (1803): "It is better that law should be certain than that every judge should speculate upon the improvement in it"); (2) promotes **impartiality and independence** in adjudication.
- **Judicial activism:** a judge liberally interpreting the law to advance substantive justice. This attitude produced **equity**.
- **Dennis Lloyd's 3 ways to substantial justice:** judicial discretion; legislative reforms; political or affirmative action (gender mainstreaming, youth empowerment).
- **Standard of substantial justice:** that of a **reasonable man** (*Isiyaku Mohammed v Kano Native Authority*).

**Explain more**

- **Other types:** **natural justice** (the rules of fair hearing: *audi alteram partem*, hear the other side; *nemo judex in causa sua*, no one judges his own cause); **legal justice** (justice according to the law as it stands); **social justice** (a fair spread of welfare and opportunity in society; section 17 of the Constitution founds the social order on freedom, equality and justice).
- **Traps:** letting the judge soften harshness is **not** a justification of formal justice. Chika, whose clear ₦450,000 claim is struck out for using the wrong form, is denied **substantive** justice. A customary court that convicts Emeka without telling him the charge breaches **section 36** (procedural justice).
- Procedural justice in Nigerian practice: long-running prosecutions such as Senator Danjuma Goje's, and locus standi challenges (the petition against Oshiomhole). *Amaechi v INEC* (2008) favours substantial over technical justice.
- The classic Nigerian illustration of formal justice is the treasonable felony trial of Chief Obafemi Awolowo, a popular political leader of the old Western Region, where the trial judge said "my hands are tied": whatever his sympathies, the law compelled conviction.

### Lesson 2.4: Law and the state

**Must know**

- **State (Greig, adapting the Montevideo Convention 1933):** "a territorial unit, containing a stable population under the authority of its government, and recognised as being capable of entering into relations with other entities with international personality."
- **4 elements:** (1) a definite **territory**, (2) a **population**, (3) a **government**, (4) **sovereignty**. A written constitution or state religion is **not** an element. (Montevideo's fourth criterion is capacity to enter into relations with other states.)
- **Government:** the structure through which human agents act for the state, which is an artificial entity; the machinery for administering the state (legislature, executive, judiciary, ministries, courts, armed forces, police).
- **Section 2(1), 1999 Constitution:** Nigeria is "one indivisible and indissoluble sovereign State to be known by the name of the Federal Republic of Nigeria"; section 2(2): a federation of states and a Federal Capital Territory.
- The **state is created by society through law**; law defines each organ's powers (*A.G. Ogun State v A.G. Federation* (1982)); acts beyond power are null and void.
- **Monopoly of legitimate use of force:** the state alone ordinarily uses or authorises coercion (police, military), channelled through law.
- **Separation of powers:** legislature makes, executive enforces, judiciary interprets (sections 4, 5, 6); originated with John Locke, developed by **Montesquieu** (*The Spirit of the Laws*). **Checks and balances:** e.g. the Supreme Court voiding an Act inconsistent with the Constitution.
- **Westphalian model** (treaties of 1648): **sovereign equality** of states, territorial integrity, **non-interference**.
- **3 modes of extinction:** merger (states unite); dissolution (breaks into successors); **annexation** (absorbed by another state).

**Explain more**

- **Recognition** is politically important but **not strictly conclusive** of statehood: a breakaway region with territory, population and government but no recognition is contested, not automatically excluded.
- A state does **not** lose its personality because its government is oppressive, fascist or racist; international law, as a rule, ignores internal affairs unless other states are affected.
- Internationally a state is **one legal person**; federal units have no separate international personality.
- Reality check: the law proclaims lofty aims ("do justice to all manners of men"), yet a dominant class often manipulates state machinery.

### Lesson 2.5: Ruler and citizens: sovereignty, legitimacy and the rule of law

**Must know**

- **Absolute rule:** Louis XIV interrupted a judge who said "the king and the state" with **"I am the state"** (Dulaure). Rulers, and African kings seen as demi-gods, wielded absolute power based on **divine right**.
- **Jean Bodin**, a French writer, introduced the doctrine of **sovereignty** into Western political theory.
- **4 theories to curtail rulers' power:** (1) **social contract** (Hobbes, Locke, Rousseau); (2) the **rule of law** (A.V. Dicey, *Law of the Constitution*); (3) **separation of powers**; (4) the **principle of legality** (*nullum crimen sine lege*). Divine right of kings is **not** one.
- **Popular sovereignty, section 14(2)(a):** "Sovereignty belongs to the people of Nigeria from whom government through this Constitution derives all its powers and authority." Its ultimate expression is the power to change leaders.
- **Legitimacy:** once charismatic or traditional, now a matter of law. **Section 1(2):** no person or group may take control of the government "except in accordance with the provisions of this Constitution." A failed coup is **treason** (sections 37 and 38, Criminal Code), punishable by death; the provision cannot stop a successful coup.
- **Farrar and Dugdale:** modern legitimacy rests not on mere legality but on **"impersonal rational authority"**: the rule of law accepted both by those who run the system and by the people.
- **4 sources of legitimacy:** constitutionalism; democratic consent; rule of law; **performance legitimacy** (security, roads, hospitals delivered).
- **Kelsen:** a law made by constitutional procedure is legitimate because it follows from higher norms, even if many think it unjust.

**Dicey's rule of law, 3 pillars:**

1. **Supremacy of regular law:** no one is punishable except for a distinct breach of law established before the ordinary courts; no arbitrary power.
2. **Equality before the law:** everyone, whatever his rank, is subject to the ordinary law and courts.
3. The constitution is the **result of the ordinary law**: rights are secured by court decisions.

**Explain more**

- **Social contract:** Hobbes (*Leviathan*, 1651): without government life is "solitary, poor, nasty, brutish and short", so people surrender power to a sovereign. Locke (1632-1704): government exists to protect life, liberty and property and may be resisted if it fails. Rousseau (1712-1778): sovereignty lies in the **general will** of the people.
- **Legality:** section 36(8): no one is guilty of an act that was not an offence when done; section 36(12): offences must be defined in written law (*Aoko v Fagbemi* (1961)). Bayo charged for a then-lawful act is protected by legality.
- **Rule of law cases:** detaining a trader for three weeks without charge breaches the rule of law (section 35 requires being brought before a court within a reasonable time). *Military Governor of Lagos State v Ojukwu* (1986): government must obey court orders. *Lakanmi* (1971): even military decrees were tested against law.
- **Louis XIV essay:** contrast absolute rule with today's position: sovereignty in the people (section 14(2)(a)), power limited by the Constitution, separation of powers and the rule of law; citizens hold rights (Chapter IV) and duties; legitimacy depends on consent (Osipitan and the call for a national conference).

### One-minute recap

- Order is the foremost objective; anarchy comes from non-enforcement.
- Rights are not absolute: limits on contract (crime, prices, trade names) and politics (age 18, parties, disqualifications).
- Law is the means, justice the end; justice is in the eye of the beholder (Thorne); first condition of humanity (Soyinka).
- Ulpian: constant and perpetual will; Rawls: fairness; Plato: what one is fitted for; Aristotle: law plus equality.
- Aquinas: commutative and distributive; Aristotle: corrective (retrospective) and distributive (prospective).
- Formal ("my hands are tied") vs substantive (*Chinwendu v Mbamali*; judicial activism); procedural = section 36; *Josiah*: three-way traffic.
- State: territory, population, government, sovereignty; recognition not conclusive; section 2(1).
- Bodin (sovereignty), Montesquieu (separation of powers), Dicey (rule of law); section 14(2)(a) and section 1(2).

### Theory practice

**Question:** "Justice, like beauty, is in the eye of the beholder" (Emily Thorne). Discuss the various perceptions and types of justice.

*Answer outline:*

- **Introduction:** law is the means, justice the end; Ross (correct application, not arbitrariness); Soyinka; explain the quote with winner and loser in court; list factors shaping one's view.
- **Perceptions:** Aristotle (law and equality), equality and its limits (the mother's three children, legal aid), Ulpian and Aquinas (render each his due), Plato (fitted for: caste, Osu), Rawls (fairness), Bix and Lasswell.
- **Types:** commutative and distributive (Aquinas); corrective and distributive (Aristotle) with Federal Character and palliatives; procedural (section 36); formal (Awolowo's treasonable felony trial, *Sheddon v Goodrich*) against substantive (*Chinwendu v Mbamali*, judicial activism, Dennis Lloyd, reasonable man test); *Josiah v The State*.
- **Conclusion:** justice is relative, yet law must balance competing interests; a just law commands obedience; apartheid shows law and justice can part.
