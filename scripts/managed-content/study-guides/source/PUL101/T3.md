## Topic 3: Aspects and Classifications of Law

**What you must know:** the four types of law by origin; the six classifications (public/private, civil/criminal, substantive/procedural, municipal/international, written/unwritten, common law/equity) with definitions, examples and Nigerian statutes; burden and standard of proof; the history of equity (*Earl of Oxford's case*, Judicature Acts 1875); why no classification is watertight and why classification still matters.

### Lesson 3.1: Types of law by origin

**Must know**

Montesquieu (*The Spirit of the Laws*): laws in the widest sense are "any necessary relation arising from the nature of things"; all beings have their laws. So "law" has many uses. Classified by origin, there are **4 types**:

1. **Eternal law:** constant, everlasting, universal; it has always existed and never changes. **St Thomas Aquinas** (1224-1274): law is "the exemplar of divine wisdom as directing the motions and acts of everything". Eternal law is the foundation of all other laws: God-given rules governing all creation.
2. **Divine law:** the law of God or from God. Perfect example: the **Ten Commandments** in the Holy Bible (Exodus 20:1-17). Based on the premise that man, being sinful, cannot make a valid and just law, so he must turn to God.
3. **Natural law:** a body of moral rules and principles of human conduct deduced from nature and **discoverable by reason**. Lord Lloyd: "a body of objective moral principles based on the nature of the universe and discoverable by reason". Aquinas: natural law is "a participation of the eternal law in a rational creature".
4. **Human or positive law:** law posited (laid down) by an authority of society, which is its sole source of validity. Studied by **legal positivism**, which does not concern itself with morality.

**Explain more**

- Natural law has a **descriptive** sense (laws of gravity, planetary motion, the law that makes us sleep or hunger) and a **prescriptive** sense (a universal precept regulating human conduct). Lawyers use the prescriptive sense. Two conceptions: an ideal guide to positive law, or a **higher law** to which positive law must conform to be valid.
- **Lon Fuller:** no separation of law and morals; law is "the enterprise of subjecting human conduct to the governance of rules"; it must have an internal morality, be clear and certain and not retroactive. **Cicero** (1st century BC): "True law is right reason in agreement with Nature… one eternal and unchangeable law… valid for all nations for all times."
- Positivists: **Jeremy Bentham** (father of legal positivism: law is a command of a sovereign to subordinates), **John Austin** (law is "a rule laid down for the guidance of an intelligent being by an intelligent being having power over him", backed by sanction). **H.L.A. Hart** insisted there is no necessary connection between law as it is (*lex lata*) and law as it ought to be (*de lege ferenda*). In *A.-G. Federation v Guardian Newspapers Ltd*, Uwaifo JSC rejected judging a law's validity by ethics, morality or religion. Critics say positivism can lead to dictatorship and tyranny.
- Exam trap: "Procedural law" is **not** a type of law by origin. A student who says CAMA is valid simply because the National Assembly enacted it, whatever its morality, is taking the **positivist** (human law) view.
- Eternal law works impartially for kings and servants; divine law depends on those (popes, kings) who claim authority to interpret it and may be twisted to oppress. Natural law's big contribution: it rejected the idea that man cannot make just law, made law a matter of reason not fatalism, and inspired equality, democracy and human rights.

### Lesson 3.2: Public and private law; civil and criminal law

**Must know**

- **Public law:** concerned with the protection of the state and its organs; governs the relationship between the **state and citizens**, where the state is **'higher'** in legal status. Examples: constitutional law, administrative law, criminal law, international law.
- **Private law:** concerned with relationships **between individuals** and the protection of individual rights. Examples: contract, torts, family law, succession, trusts, law of evidence.
- **Civil law** (in this context): defines the rights and duties of persons to one another and gives an injured person a way to be **compensated** for another's wrongful act. Examples: contract, torts, land law, family law. Aim: recovery of money or property, or enforcement of a right.
- **Criminal law:** protects the interest of the **public at large** by **punishing** conduct believed harmful to society. Punishment: imprisonment, fine or both. The **State** prosecutes on behalf of society.

| Point | Criminal case | Civil case |
|---|---|---|
| Who sues | The State (prosecution) | The injured person (plaintiff/claimant) |
| Aim | Punishment | Compensation or enforcing a right |
| Standard of proof | Beyond reasonable doubt: **s.135 Evidence Act 2011** | Balance of probabilities: **s.134 Evidence Act 2011** |
| Burden of proof | Stays on the prosecution; does not shift | May shift between the parties |

**Explain more**

- "Civil" has other meanings: law that is not criminal; the law of a state as opposed to international law; to the **armed forces**, everything not peculiar to the military (a civil court as opposed to a court martial).
- **Balance of probabilities:** judgment goes to the party who proves its case more convincingly. The higher criminal standard exists **to protect the accused from the awesome power of the state and the danger of wrongful conviction**; this is the presumption of innocence, "the one golden thread" of criminal law (*Woolmington v DPP* (1935); s.36(5) 1999 Constitution).
- Serious crimes (murder, rape, burglary) are normally tried in the **High Court**; minor offences (minor traffic offences, minor assault) are tried **summarily in the Magistrate Court**. Criminal law is codified in the **Criminal Code** (Southern States) and the **Penal Code** (Northern States).
- **Overlap:** assault, false imprisonment and defamation are both crimes and torts. If A steals B's bicycle, A is guilty of **stealing** and liable to B in the tort of **conversion**. Breach of contract is **not** a crime.
- **France vs Nigeria:** France has a separate body of rules and courts for matters involving the state. Nigeria has **no** separate system; state-citizen litigation follows the same rules as private litigation. The only distinction is **procedural**: special procedures apply to enforce public duties, e.g. fundamental rights enforcement (Fundamental Rights (Enforcement Procedure) Rules) and **judicial review**. If the special procedure is not followed, the case **may be struck out**.

### Lesson 3.3: Substantive and procedural law; municipal and international law

**Must know**

- **Substantive law:** the rules of law themselves; it states **rights, duties and liabilities**, defines a code of conduct and prescribes a penalty for breach. Examples: contract, torts, criminal law, constitutional law; **s.419 Criminal Code** (obtaining goods by false pretences, with imprisonment).
- **Procedural (adjectival) law:** the methods of proceeding by which substantive rights and duties are **enforced in court**. It covers: how an action is initiated; mode of proof; giving evidence; examination of witnesses; manner of giving judgment; **enforcement of judgment**. Whether conduct amounts to an offence is **substantive**, not procedural.
- Sources of procedural law: Rules of Court (e.g. High Court of Lagos State (Civil Procedure) Rules), Criminal Procedure Act (South) and Criminal Procedure Code (North), now largely replaced by the **Administration of Criminal Justice Act 2015** (procedural law to the Criminal and Penal Codes), the Evidence Act, the Sheriffs and Civil Process Act.
- **Municipal (national, domestic) law:** the internal law of a particular country, regulating relations between individuals and between individuals and the State. Example: the 1999 Constitution.
- **International law:** governs relations between **States inter se** or between States and **international organisations**. Sources (Art. 38(1) Statute of the International Court of Justice): **treaties; international custom; general principles of law recognised by civilised nations; judicial decisions and teachings of the most highly qualified publicists**.

**Explain more**

- International law divides into **private international law (conflict of laws)**, which handles cases inside a domestic legal system where **foreign elements** arise (two Nigerians contract in England to sell goods in London; a Nigerian court applies English law to its validity), and **public international law**, which is a **separate system** altogether.
- Examples of international law: the **African Charter on Human and Peoples' Rights**, the Universal Declaration of Human Rights, the UN Charter. A local government bye-law is **not** a source of international law.
- **s.12(1) 1999 Constitution:** a treaty has no force of law in Nigeria until **enacted by the National Assembly**. Ratification alone is not enough.
- Barrister consulting Civil Procedure Rules to start a claim and serve the defendant = procedural law.

### Lesson 3.4: Written and unwritten law

**Must know**

- **Written law** (technical meaning): a rule **formally enacted** into a statute by the legislature, after debate and scrutiny and assent by the chief executive. Names: **Codes, Statutes, Acts, Decrees, Edicts, Laws, Rules, Ordinances**. Judicial precedents are **not** written law.
- **Unwritten law** means two things: (1) rules not written down at all, such as **customary law and conventions**; (2) **unenacted** rules even if reduced into writing, such as **case law**.
- Example of an unwritten constitution: the **British Constitution**. The bulk of a country's unwritten law is its customary law.

**Explain more**

- Written down is not the same as written. The neighbour principle in *Donoghue v Stevenson* (1932) is printed in the law reports but is **unwritten law** because no legislature enacted it.
- **s.36(12) 1999 Constitution:** a person shall not be convicted of a criminal offence unless the offence is defined and its penalty prescribed in a **written law** (an Act of the National Assembly, a Law of a State, or subsidiary legislation). So a conviction under unwritten custom is unlawful: *Aoko v Fagbemi* [1961] 1 All NLR 400 (adultery not an offence). This effectively abolished customary criminal law.

### Lesson 3.5: Common law and equity

**Must know**

- **Common law** (as a division of law): the law developed by the old common law courts of England: **King's Bench, Court of Common Pleas, Court of Exchequer**. The Court of Chancery was **not** one of them.
- Origin: after the **Norman Conquest of 1066**, royal judges went on circuit applying local customs; meeting at Westminster, they compared customs and kept the more sensible ones, producing uniform rules for the whole realm: judge-made law found in decided cases.
- **Defects:** strict, formal, full of legalism; every action began by **royal writ**; no writ, no remedy; main remedy **damages**, often ineffective.
- **Rise of equity:** unsatisfied plaintiffs petitioned the King; the **Lord Chancellor** handled the petitions; by the end of the 15th century petitions went straight to him, and his court became the **Court of Chancery**.

**Key events**

1. ***Earl of Oxford's case* (1615):** an assignee of a lease built a house and planted trees; the owner ejected him; the common law court gave judgment for the owner. **Lord Ellesmere**, the Chancellor, granted an **injunction** restraining the owner: "by the law of God, he that builds a house must live in it". **Coke CJ** protested.
2. **Lord Bacon** (Attorney General, later Lord Chancellor, under James I) resolved the clash for Chancery: **where common law and equity conflict, equity prevails**.
3. **Judicature Acts** (in force **1875**): **fused the administration** of both in the same courts but did **not** abolish the distinction between legal and equitable rights. Nigerian courts likewise administer both.

**Explain more**

- Before 1875 a litigant with a common law right who needed an injunction had to sue **first at common law** to establish the right, **then in Chancery** for the remedy: common law courts could not grant **injunctions, specific performance or rescission**, and Chancery could not award **damages**. Damages is a common law remedy, not equitable.
- Example: Uche signs over his car to Lola for ₦500,000. At law the contract is enforceable, but if he was induced by **fraud, mistake or misrepresentation**, **equity** can rescind it. Equity mitigates the harshness of common law.
- Ashburton's famous image: the two streams of jurisdiction now run in the same channel but do not mingle their waters.

### Lesson 3.6: No watertight classification; why classify

**Must know**

- Classifications **overlap**. Land law has become largely public law since the **Land Use Act**; tax law is as much private as public; assault is a crime and a tort.
- A real problem may need several subjects: an employee dismissed after a workplace accident needs **contract, labour law and tort** combined.
- **Importance of classification:**
  1. Subject classification: coherent study of related rules; analysing a case's issues and the applicable rules.
  2. Common law/equity: understanding how they evolved; separating legal from equitable rights and remedies to get an adequate remedy.
  3. Public/private: in France, the applicable law and court; in Nigeria, the right **procedure** (fundamental rights, winding-up petitions, judicial review).
  4. Civil/criminal: separating private injuries from public order; choosing whether to **sue, set the criminal process in motion, or wait for the criminal case to end before suing**.

**Explain more**

- Classifications should be "the lawyer's servants, not his masters". Matching pairs to remember: substantive v procedural = the rules themselves v how they are enforced; municipal v international = law within a country v law between states; written v unwritten = enacted v not enacted.

### One-minute recap

- By origin: eternal (Aquinas), divine (Ten Commandments), natural (reason; Lloyd, Fuller, Cicero), positive (Bentham, Austin, Hart).
- Public law: state is 'higher'; private law: between individuals.
- Civil: compensation, balance of probabilities (s.134 EA 2011); criminal: punishment, beyond reasonable doubt (s.135), burden stays on prosecution.
- Substantive = rights and duties; procedural = enforcement in court (ACJA 2015, Rules of Court).
- International law sources: treaties, custom, general principles, judicial decisions and publicists; treaties need enactment (s.12(1) CFRN).
- Written = enacted; case law is unwritten; s.36(12) needs written law for a crime.
- Common law courts: King's Bench, Common Pleas, Exchequer. *Earl of Oxford's case* (1615): equity prevails. Judicature Acts 1875 fused administration only.
- No classification is watertight, but each has practical importance.

### Theory practice

**Question:** "Distinguishing in practice between one classification of law and another may not be as clear-cut as it appears in theory." Discuss, with reference to any four classifications of law.

*Answer outline:*
- **Introduction:** law can be classified in many ways; list the six classifications; thesis: useful tools that overlap in practice.
- **Public v private:** define with examples; the state as 'higher' party; Nigeria has no separate courts (contrast France), only special procedures (fundamental rights, judicial review); Land Use Act shows land law moving into public law; tax is both.
- **Civil v criminal:** define; standards of proof (ss.134-135 Evidence Act 2011); overlap: assault, false imprisonment, defamation; stealing a bicycle = stealing plus conversion.
- **Written v unwritten:** technical meaning; case law is written down yet unwritten (*Donoghue v Stevenson*); s.36(12) and *Aoko v Fagbemi*.
- **Common law v equity:** history, *Earl of Oxford's case* (1615), Lord Bacon, Judicature Acts 1875 fused administration, not rights.
- **Practical importance:** choice of procedure and remedy; the dismissed injured employee (contract, labour, tort).
- **Conclusion:** the classifications overlap and are the lawyer's servants, not his masters, but they remain essential for choosing the right court, procedure and remedy.
