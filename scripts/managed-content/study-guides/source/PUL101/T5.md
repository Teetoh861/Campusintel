## Topic 5: Fact Finding and Dispute Resolution

**What you must know:** adjudicatory and non-adjudicatory methods; adversarial and inquisitorial fact finding with their merits and demerits; burden and standard of proof, res ipsa loquitur, shifting of fact and law; the four forms of ADR plus negotiation; old and customary methods.

### Lesson 5.1: Settling disputes; the adjudicatory method

**Must know**

- "Bringing an end" to a dispute differs from "settling" or "resolving" it. A parent who confiscates a toy two boys are fighting over ends the quarrel but resolves nothing.
- **Old methods:** in the Middle Ages disputes were "resolved" by **ordeal** (burning hands in fire, throwing a baby of doubtful legitimacy into a river); surviving unscathed proved innocence. This is **"proof"** as opposed to **trial**. **Trial by ordeal is prohibited by the Criminal Code.**
- Decisions are respected when based on **objective standards and standard procedures**, not the adjudicator's whims.
- **2 modern methods:** (1) **adjudicatory** (litigation); (2) **non-adjudicatory** (Alternative Dispute Resolution, ADR).
- **Adjudicatory method:** the dispute goes to a **court** or tribunal established by law; a judge, magistrate or adjudicator **assigned by the state** decides for one party. It is **formal**: parties and witnesses appear before formal institutions. The decision is **binding and self-executing** (needs no other tribunal to give it force of law).
- The judge must **first ascertain the facts, then apply the law.** The **2 methods of fact finding:** **adversarial** and **inquisitorial**.

**Explain more**

- Regular courts normally use the adversarial method; many Nigerian **tribunals and panels** (disciplinary, examination malpractice, benefits tribunals) are **semi-inquisitorial**.
- **Ajegunle co-tenants problem:** Mrs A wants to find who stole her jewellery by "tying the Holy Book with a key" and calling each name; Mrs C refuses, so the others call her the thief. Advice: the method is like the old ordeals and is **outlawed**; Mrs C was right to refuse and **her refusal is not proof of guilt**. Report to the police (proof beyond reasonable doubt in court), or, as neighbours with a continuing relationship, use mediation by elders.

### Lesson 5.2: The adversarial method

**Must know**

- **Adversarial (accusatorial) method:** **each party and his lawyer** control how facts are collected and presented; the parties appear as **adversaries**.
- **A witness's evidence:**
  1. **Examination-in-chief:** a party calls his own witness to establish evidence material to his case.
  2. **Cross-examination:** by the other side, to **weaken, neutralise and demolish** the case of the party who called the witness. **A witness not cross-examined is taken as admitted.**
  3. **Re-examination:** lets the witness **explain any seeming inconsistency** arising from cross-examination.
- **Judge = umpire**, enforcing **ground rules**: (1) hearsay is inadmissible; (2) no leading questions to **your own** witness; (3) no taking the other side by surprise.
- The judge **must not descend into the arena** to find evidence, even facts he knows. If a vital witness is not called, he can do nothing; he cannot call his own witnesses and may question a witness **only to clarify an ambiguity**.
- **Level playing ground:** same rules for both; civil cases decided on the **balance of probability**: if the scale of justice tilts **51%** to one party, he wins. **2 modifications:** criminal procedure; res ipsa loquitur.

**Explain more**

- A judge in a ₦2,500,000 contract suit who privately knows of a decisive witness that neither counsel called must **decide on the evidence presented**.
- **Bassef Orji** (charged with murder, found with roasted human flesh, says it came from accident victims): the prosecution did not cross-examine him on this defence or call a vital witness. The judge **cannot call the witness**; the **unchallenged defence stands** and doubt favours Bassef.

### Lesson 5.3: Standard of proof; criminal cases; res ipsa loquitur

**Must know**

- **Civil:** balance of probabilities. **Criminal:** **beyond reasonable doubt** (Evidence Act 2011: s.134 civil, s.135 criminal).
- In criminal cases the prosecution discloses its evidence before trial; the accused need not disclose his defence.
- **Section 35(2), 1999 Constitution:** "Any person who is arrested or detained shall have the right to remain silent or avoid answering any question until after consultation with a legal practitioner or any other person of his own choice."
- **Section 36(5), 1999 Constitution:** "Every person who is charged with a criminal offence shall be presumed to be innocent until he is proved guilty." (Formerly s.33(5), 1979 Constitution.)
- The burden stays on the prosecution and **never shifts**; it must prove every ingredient and negate available defences, except matters peculiarly within the accused's knowledge.
- *Woolmington v DPP* (1935): there is no burden on the prisoner to prove his innocence; "it is sufficient for him to raise a doubt as to his guilt."
- *State v Keji Amusan* (1982): "suspicion, no matter how grave, can never ground a conviction."
- **Res ipsa loquitur = "the facts speak for themselves"**: a rule of evidence affecting the burden of proof, to help accident victims. Classic statement by **Erle CJ** in *Scott v London and St Katherine Docks Co* (1865).
- **Effect:** the **defendant** must show **a specific cause or that he used reasonable care**. The plaintiff proves only that an accident happened and he was the victim, not its exact cause or the person responsible.

**Explain more**

- Policy: better nine guilty go free than one innocent be punished; the presumption of innocence is a **declaration of social policy**.
- Kunle, last seen near the till after a quarrel, charged with stealing ₦450,000: mere suspicion, so **acquit**; he need not prove he did not take it.
- Refusing to answer police questions until a lawyer arrives = **s.35(2)**, not s.36(5).
- Res ipsa examples: an aircraft crash, collapsing scaffolding, a **swab left inside a patient after an abdominal operation** (*Mahon v Osborne*, 1939). In such a case the **hospital** must explain.

### Lesson 5.4: Facts; shifting of fact and law

**Must know**

- **Fact** (Evidence Act; s.258(1) Evidence Act 2011, formerly s.2(1)) includes: (a) anything, state of things or relation of things capable of being perceived by the senses; (b) **any mental condition of which any person is conscious.**
- Whoever wants judgment on facts he asserts must prove they exist (s.131(1) Evidence Act 2011; *Elemo v Omolade*, 1968).
- **Nwadialo's 2 senses of "burden of proof":** (1) **legal burden**: to establish the whole case to the required standard; (2) **evidential burden**: to adduce evidence on a particular fact or issue. **Shifting of fact = shifting of the evidential burden**; in civil cases it moves with the **pleadings**.
- **Facts in issue (civil):** the facts a plaintiff must prove to establish his claim, **unless admitted** expressly or by implication (Aguda).
- ***Affirmanti non neganti incumbit probatio*:** the burden lies on the one who **affirms**, not the one who denies.
- **Confessing and avoiding:** the defendant admits the allegation but pleads **new facts** changing its complexion; **he** must prove them.
- **4 exceptions shifting a burden to the accused:** (1) defence of **exemption or qualification** from the law creating the offence; (2) a **statute** places the onus on him; (3) **special facts within his knowledge**; (4) **intoxication or insanity**.
- **Shifting of law:** ***ignorantia facti excusat, ignorantia juris non excusat*** = ignorance of fact excuses; ignorance of law does not. If a party relies on a law unaware it was amended or repealed, the burden of informing the court falls on **the person who knows**.
- ***Jura novit curia*** = "it is for the court to know the law": if counsel fail to cite a relevant law, the burden **shifts to the judge**.

**Explain more**

- Emeka sues Bayo for the ₦60,000 balance of a ₦150,000 laptop. Bayo **admits** the promise: Emeka is **discharged** from proving it. Bayo **denies** it: Emeka must prove it. Emeka produces a **memorandum signed by Bayo**: the evidential burden shifts to **Bayo** to show it is not genuine.
- A tenant sued for ₦300,000 rent who admits it was due but says he paid the agent is **confessing and avoiding**; the **tenant** proves payment.
- *Adenle v Oyegbade* (1967): where both sides accept land was originally family land, the onus is on whoever claims **exclusive** entitlement.
- *Okagbue v COP* (1965): the accused need not prove innocence. Simply pleading not guilty shifts nothing.
- Shift of fact occurs at the hearing; shift of law at the **address stage**, between counsel. ***Ignorantia judicis est calamitas innocentis***: the ignorance of the judge is the calamity of the innocent.

### Lesson 5.5: Adversarial versus inquisitorial

**Must know**

**Adversarial advantages (5):** (1) reduces adjudicator **bias**, and a perverse judgment can be reversed on appeal; (2) each party presents his case and discredits the other's; (3) more **acceptable**: equal chance to win; (4) **develops the law** through ingenious arguments; (5) binding and self-executing.

**Adversarial disadvantages (5):**
1. **"Sporting theory" of justice:** those with the best lawyers (often the rich) win.
2. **Acrimonious.**
3. Temptation to **manufacture evidence** (*Whitehouse v Jordan*, 1980: experts' evidence "doctored").
4. **Delay tactics**: *Ajani v Giwa* (1986), Oputa JSC deplored 16 years to settle land ownership.
5. **Long delay impairs fact finding:** witnesses die, relocate or forget; evidence is lost.

- **Inquisitorial method:** trials as **inquiries**; main feature: the **adjudicator has considerable control over how evidence is collected and presented**. He investigates, questions parties and witnesses, may appoint assessors or rely on **experts who never appear before it**.

**Inquisitorial advantages (4):** (1) **speedy**, usually with a prescribed time limit; (2) **simpler, less formal, cheaper** (a prescribed form, not a writ); (3) **equal access to justice**, since success depends on the body, not on representation; (4) binding and self-executing.

**Inquisitorial disadvantages (3):** (1) **hasty trial**: "justice delayed is justice denied" but **"justice rushed is justice crushed"**; (2) quality depends on the body's **ability and effort**; (3) often **in camera**, escaping public scrutiny (officers convicted under the Abacha regime for **phantom coups**).

**Explain more**

- *R v Deputy Industrial Injuries Commissioner, ex parte Moore* (1965): reliance on two absent doctors' opinions was **not a breach of natural justice**; Diplock LJ stressed the tribunal's inquisitorial nature.
- *Baba v Nigerian Civil Aviation Training Centre* (Supreme Court): no breach of fair hearing where the person had a reasonable opportunity to defend himself, though cross-examination was refused.
- **Titi** countersigned the invigilator's irregularity report; the Examination Malpractices Panel, being inquisitorial, may rely on it though he is abroad. Her appeal will likely **fail**.
- Judges now lean investigative in **divorce and child guardianship** cases. Trap: speedy disposal is an **inquisitorial** advantage.

### Lesson 5.6: ADR and customary methods

**Must know**

- **Non-adjudicatory method (ADR):** disputes resolved **without the adjudicator finding facts**; no formal institutions, witnesses or apportionment of blame, yet recognised and regulated by law.
- **4 main forms:**
  1. **Reconciliation:** the parties confer and agree to restore cordiality and harmony; **only the disputants** take part. Impossible if one refuses.
  2. **Conciliation:** a **third party as intermediary**; it differs from reconciliation in **the number of parties**. The conciliator meets the parties **together**, discusses the issues, proposes compromise terms and maps agreement and disagreement. His decision is **merely a guide**.
  3. **Mediation:** the mediator **rarely inquires into facts, does not apportion blame**, merely **offers suggestions** the parties may accept or reject; meets them **separately**. **Not binding even in a trade dispute.**
  4. **Arbitration:** the parties agree to have an **independent third party** settle the dispute **and to be bound by his decision**; the **only** form where the third party **decides on the merits**.
- **Negotiation:** the parties settle it themselves; **once a third party comes in, it is no longer negotiation.**
- **3 essentials of arbitration:** (1) an existing or envisaged dispute; (2) **voluntary agreement** to refer it; (3) a **binding award**. The arbitrator need not be a judge.
- **Binding conciliation:** in a **trade dispute** under the **Trade Disputes Act**, a conciliator appointed by the Minister has **seven days**; a settlement is reported to the **Minister** with a **memorandum signed by the parties' representatives**, which then **binds** the parties (breach is an offence).

| Adjudicatory | Non-adjudicatory |
|---|---|
| Formal institutions | Generally informal |
| Fact finding | No fact finding |
| Apportions blame | No blame |
| Looks back and applies the law | Suggests solutions for future harmony |

**ADR advantages (6):** avoids the **"win-lose"** character of litigation; saves time; parties **control their own fate**; free from **rules of evidence**; specialist arbitrator with **technical knowledge**; **not state-financed**, so parties pay and feel a sense of belonging.

**ADR disadvantages (5):** costs of rooms and arbitrators' fees; awards subject to the court's **overriding powers** (to remit or set aside for error of law on its face); **no consolidation** of multi-party disputes, since it is voluntary; reconciliation and mediation **do not punish wrongdoers**; decisions **may not be binding**.

**Explain more**

- In *M.V. Lupex v Nigerian Overseas Chartering and Shipping Ltd* (2003) the Supreme Court held a party **cannot approbate and reprobate**: suing in court despite an arbitration clause reprobates it, and courts must act on the agreement. Once accepted, arbitration cannot be abandoned (*Larbi v Kwasi*, 1950).
- Divorce proceedings may be adjourned for **reconciliation**; short "reconciliation periods" of resumed cohabitation do not defeat a later divorce.
- **Customary methods:** family heads, elders and chiefs mediate or arbitrate to restore harmony (the traditional attitude that "you do not return from court and resume friendship"). Customary arbitration binds where the parties voluntarily submit and agree to be bound by a decision under their custom (*Agu v Ikewibe*, 1991).
- **Trend:** English courts penalise in costs litigants who shun ADR; Justice Eso's group settled the **Mint Company** dispute with about **2,000** ex-workers by mediation within a year after over twelve years of fighting. Multi-door courthouses bring ADR into the courts.

### One-minute recap

- Adjudicatory (binding, self-executing) versus non-adjudicatory (ADR); trial by ordeal is a crime.
- Adversarial: parties control, judge as umpire; inquisitorial: adjudicator controls evidence.
- Examination-in-chief, cross-examination (demolish), re-examination (explain).
- Civil 51% balance; criminal beyond reasonable doubt; ss.35(2) and 36(5) 1999 Constitution.
- Res ipsa loquitur: defendant shows specific cause or reasonable care.
- Shifting of fact = evidential burden; affirmanti non neganti; confessing and avoiding; four exceptions; jura novit curia.
- Adversarial 5/5 (sporting theory); inquisitorial 4/3 (justice rushed is justice crushed).
- Reconciliation (parties only), conciliation (together), mediation (separately, never binding), arbitration (binding award); negotiation has no third party.

### Theory practice

**Question:** Critically examine the adversarial and inquisitorial methods of fact finding. Which do you prefer?

*Answer outline:*
- **Introduction:** adjudication requires the judge to find facts before applying law, by the adversarial or inquisitorial method.
- **Adversarial:** parties control evidence; witness sequence; judge as umpire; balance of probability, modified by criminal rules (ss.35(2), 36(5); *Woolmington v DPP*; *State v Keji Amusan*) and res ipsa loquitur (*Scott v London and St Katherine Docks*). Five merits; five demerits (*Whitehouse v Jordan*; *Ajani v Giwa*).
- **Inquisitorial:** adjudicator controls evidence (*Ex parte Moore*; *Baba v NCATC*). Four merits; three demerits (phantom coup trials).
- **Conclusion:** state a reasoned preference, or argue for a blend as in divorce and custody cases.
