## Topic 7: Legislation

**What you must know:** meaning of legislation and reasons for new laws; legislative proposals and drafting; how a bill becomes an Act (civilian and military); types and forms of legislation, subsidiary legislation, codification; the rules of statutory interpretation with cases; aids, presumptions and maxims.

### Lesson 7.1: Legislation, reasons for it and legislative proposals

**Must know**

- **Legislation (strict sense):** law made by the **legislature**, the body whose primary duty is to make law (not ministries, councils or courts).
- **Definition:** the **formal and express promulgation or declaration of legal rules, proscriptions or directions into statutes** on the subjects to which they relate.
- **Section 4(1), 1999 Constitution:** federal legislative power is vested in the National Assembly. **Section 4(2):** it legislates for the **peace, order and good government of the Federation**.

**5 reasons for new legislation:**

1. **Dynamic nature of society:** new attitudes and values; e.g. laws against dumping refuse in public places and indiscriminate slaughtering of animals.
2. **Change in political and economic ideologies of government:** "A change of government must bring new changes in the law" (Atiyah); e.g. a civilian regime repealing Decrees and abolishing the Petroleum Trust Fund.
3. **Global shifts in value systems:** laws on women's and children's rights, environment, endangered species, from membership of international organisations and NGOs.
4. **Scientific and technological breakthroughs:** computers and the Internet spread private information "to all and sundry"; tape recorders forced copyright changes for broadcast music; hence IT laws.
5. **Arrest of anti-social behaviour:** Failed Banks Decree 1994, Advance Fee Fraud ("419") Decree, Money Laundering Act, NDLEA Act 1989.

- **Who may initiate:** citizens, interest groups, communities, NGOs (e.g. WOTCLEF), ministries, corporate bodies, legislators, the executive.
- **Form:** **no stereotyped form**, but **logically and structurally presented**, titled by subject (e.g. "Secret Cultism: The Menace of Cultism in Institutions of Learning in Nigeria").
- **Order of contents:** (1) **present position**, (2) **anomalies**, (3) **suggested changes**, (4) **concrete proposals** for the law.
- **Channels:** memoranda to government departments, to the legislature or its **committee**, or to the **Law Reform Commission**; opinions in **print and electronic media**; **pressure groups** (e.g. the NUT on professionalising teaching). A court judgment is not a channel.

**Explain more**

- Scenario matching: Internet privacy = technology; new regime repealing Decrees = ideology; women's rights after joining international bodies = global values; public-health refuse law = dynamic society; anti-fraud law = anti-social behaviour. "Preserving every custom whatever the change in values" is never a reason.

### Lesson 7.2: Drafting, language defects and the structure of a statute

**Must know**

- **Draftsman's instructions** must state: **object** of the law, **background**, the **mischief or need** to cure, **implementation bodies**, **procedures and instruments**, **possible exemptions**.
- **Qualities:** flair; high intellect; **analytical, diagnostic** mind; good memory and foresight; patience, **attention to detail**; mastery of language; **amenable to corrections and criticisms**; familiar with the country's **socio-cultural and political peculiarities**. **Knowledge of law is indispensable**, so appoint a **lawyer**.

| Principle | Meaning | Breach |
|---|---|---|
| Mastery and familiarity of language | Common English; no tribal or Latin expressions | "rank pari passu", "inter alia" |
| Direct and explicit expression | Not winding | Long self-defence clause; better "Every person has a right to defend himself when attacked." |
| Economy of language | No needless synonyms | "fails, refuses, neglects, omits or avoids" |
| Orderliness | Logical sections and paragraphs | Patchwork draft |
| Avoid archaism | Drop words that add nothing | "above", "hereinafter", "aforesaid" |
| Consistency of terms | Same word for the same idea | "lessor" in s.2, "landlord" in s.5 |
| Connective words | will/shall, and/or, from/to | "from" and "to" may include or exclude stated periods |

- **Section 55:** National Assembly business is in **English, and in Hausa, Ibo and Yoruba when adequate arrangements have been made**; in practice English.
- **Gibson:** be wary of metaphors; use the same word always for the same idea (elegant variation confuses); avoid repetitions.
- **Ambiguity:** **two or more mutually exclusive meanings**, each deducible from the text. Types: **lexical, syntactic, contextual**.
- **Vagueness:** cloudy, indeterminate meaning creating **borderline cases**; **worse than ambiguity** because it can **hardly be resolved by the context**.
- **Open texture:** built-in **generality or flexibility**, since no statute foresees every case. **Farrar and Dugdale:** the drafter must make a rule **general enough** yet **detailed and clear**; "breadth of scope and precision are not always compatible objectives".

**Structure (Commonwealth order):**

1. **Preliminary:** long title, preamble (not always), short title, enacting clause, commencement clause.
2. **Principal:** substantive and administrative (enforcing agencies) provisions.
3. **Miscellaneous:** offences and supplementary provisions.
4. **Final:** savings, transitional provisions, repeals, schedules, perhaps an interpretation section.
5. **Executive endorsement:** signature of the President or Governor.

- **Punctuation** is part of a statute; changing it alone can change meaning. **Schedules** hold elaborate technical information; **marginal notes** act as bookmarks.

**Explain more**

- Spot the defect: "large gatherings" with no number = **vagueness**; "bank" (lender or riverbank) = **ambiguity**.
- **Semantics:** words like "consent", "husband", "reasonable" decide liberty and liability. Drafters use **definition sections** and **interpretation clauses** to extend or narrow ordinary meaning: if a Law says "money means paper currency (notes) only", coins are not money under it. **Section 318** defines words in the Constitution; the **Interpretation Act** fills gaps.

### Lesson 7.3: The legislative process

**Must know**

- The National Assembly is **bicameral: Senate and House of Representatives** (section 47). A bill (except a money bill) may start in either House. A bill is **gazetted** to give the public notice so people can weigh in; this does not make it law.

**Stages in the House of origin:**

1. **First reading:** the **Clerk** reads it to inform members; **no questions**.
2. **Second reading:** **general principles debated, questions asked**.
3. **Committee stage:** text examined **clause by clause, even word by word**, to make it acceptable.
4. **Report stage:** bill returns **in amended form**; **success or failure becomes evident**.
5. **Third reading:** final vote by **simple or two-thirds majority depending on subject matter**.

- Then the **other House** repeats the process, then **assent** by the President (Governor for a State). Assent turns the bill into an **Act** (federal) or **Law** (State).
- **Standing committees** are permanent, by subject; **ad hoc committees** handle one issue and dissolve after reporting.
- **Section 58(5):** if the President withholds assent, the bill becomes law **without assent** if **passed again by two-thirds in each House** (State equivalent: section 100(5)).
- **Military regime:** legislative and executive powers **fused**; no readings or debates. **Decree** = signed by the Head of the Federal Military Government; **Edict** = signed by a State Military Governor. Weakness: **lack of rigorous scrutiny**, hence **endless amendments**.

**Explain more**

- Trap: Senate two-thirds plus House simple majority does **not** override a veto. Debate starts at second reading, not first.
- Surviving Decrees and Edicts are deemed Acts or Laws under **section 315**. A hurried bill meets implementation problems; legislative power also checks the executive.

### Lesson 7.4: Types and forms of legislation, subsidiary legislation and codification

**Must know**

| Type | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Public General Act | Applies to everyone, everywhere | Criminal Code, Matrimonial Causes Act |
| Local Act | One locality or community | Curfew in one area; NDDC Act; Act setting up one university |
| Private Act | A particular person or body | Pardon for a named convict; proscribing a union |
| Consolidating Act | Re-enacts earlier related **statutes**, with modifications, as one coherent whole | Four aviation Acts in one |
| Code | Existing statutes **plus common law and equity** on a subject | Criminal Code, Penal Code, CAMA 1990 |

- Also: **amending** and **repealing** Acts; **money bills**; **executive**, **member's** and **private** bills; **primary** (by the legislature; the Constitution is the foundation) vs **subsidiary**. In a further classification (municipal, primary, judicial, executive, subordinate), a **practice direction** by a Chief Judge is **judicial legislation**.

| Form | Made by |
|---|---|
| Ordinance | Central legislature before 1 October 1954 |
| Act / Law | National Assembly / State House of Assembly |
| Decree / Edict | Federal Military Government / State Military Governor |

- **Subsidiary (delegated) legislation:** made by bodies **other than the legislature** under a statute: **bye-laws**, **regulations** of public corporations, ministers' **statutory instruments**. The parent law is the **enabling statute**.
- **Why:** the legislature **cannot provide every detail** and lacks **technical expertise**.
- **Limit:** exceeding the power makes it **ultra vires, null and void**; it ranks below statutes.

**Codification:**

- **For:** one source instead of scattered, overlapping law; more accessible; encourages reform; simplifies judges' work. Proposed for land law (Land Use Act, conveyancing laws, customary tenure).
- **Against:** **Von Savigny** favoured unconscious, evolutionary growth of law, not drastic change; a code cannot foresee all cases; it breaks continuity, risks **fossilisation**, is expensive.
- **Customary law:** impracticable because of **ethnic diversity**, **near irreconcilable differences**, and loss of **flexibility and adaptability**.
- **Safeguard:** review the code **every 10 or 20 years**.

**Explain more**

- One named person = private; one region or institution = local; everyone = public general. A minister's regulation that adds an unauthorised tax is void to that extent.
- Atiyah: law is "an immensely complex social machine", always needing repair; "we must not look for perfection or for finality".

### Lesson 7.5: Rules of statutory interpretation

**Must know**

- **Obilade:** the primary duty of the courts in interpreting statutes is **to find the intention of the legislature**, as expressed in the statute, though judges were not present when it was made.

| Rule | What the court does | Case |
|---|---|---|
| Literal | Natural, ordinary meaning, whatever the result | *Sussex Peerage case* (1844), Tindal C.J. |
| Golden | Departs from ordinary meaning to avoid absurdity, inconsistency or repugnance, **no further** | *Becke v Smith* (1836); *University of Ibadan v Adamolekun* (1967) |
| Mischief | Finds the defect in the earlier law and reads the Act to cure it | *Heydon's case* (1584) |
| Purposive | Reads the words to achieve the statute's purpose | *Nafiu Rabiu v State* (1981) |

- **Tindal C.J.:** "If the words of the statute are in themselves precise and unambiguous, then no more can be necessary than to expound those words in their natural and ordinary sense."
- **Heydon's case asks:** (1) the law before the Act; (2) the **mischief** it did not cover; (3) the **remedy** Parliament chose; (4) the **true reason** for it.
- **Literal rule criticisms:** words have no meaning outside context; absurd results; **Dias:** ill-suited to modern social legislation in a developing society. (Too much judicial freedom is **not** one.)
- **Mischief rule limits:** many modern laws meet new needs rather than cure defects; no test for the actual words; no access to parliamentary debates (Hansard).
- **Status:** **rules of practice, not law**. **Fatayi-Williams CJN** (*Awolowo v Shagari*): they are "of little practical assistance in settling doubts". **Bennion:** "there is no golden rule nor is there a mischief rule or a literal rule ... there are a thousand and one interpretative criteria."

**Explain more**

- ***Awolowo v Shagari* (1979):** on the 1979 presidential election, justices applying the **literal rule** to the same words reached **opposing conclusions**.
- ***Adegbenro v Akintola* (1963):** the Governor could remove the Premier "if it appears to him" he lacked majority support; the Privy Council held he could decide **without any limit on the material** used (in effect, it could even come to him in a dream).
- ***Adamolekun*:** an Edict inconsistent with a Federal Decree was void, but a literal reading of an ouster clause would have left it standing; the Supreme Court called that absurd and struck it down: **golden rule**. A hawker inside the Secretariat caught by "in the vicinity of" is the same reasoning (*Adler v George* (1964)).
- In *Magor and St Mellons RDC v Newport Corporation*, Lord Denning urged judges to fill gaps; **Lord Simonds** called it "a naked usurpation of the legislative function under the thin disguise of interpretation".
- **Best approach:** a **combination** of canons and aids, read against the legislative purpose.

### Lesson 7.6: Aids, presumptions and maxims

**Must know**

- **Context:** an Act is **read as a whole**; words take meaning from context.
- **Intrinsic aids** (inside the Act): **preamble, long title**, definition and interpretation sections, schedules.
- **Extrinsic aids** (outside): **Interpretation Act**, **dictionaries** (e.g. Black's Law Dictionary), **textbooks**, precedent.
- **Section 318(4):** the Interpretation Act applies to interpreting the Constitution. Its purpose: **avoid needless and burdensome repetition**.

**Presumptions:**

1. A new Act does **not alter existing law** unless it says so.
2. Against **interfering with vested rights** and against **retrospective** operation.
3. In criminal law, against **liability without fault** unless strict liability is clear.
4. Against **ousting courts' jurisdiction**: construed **strictly**, choosing the meaning that **preserves** jurisdiction (*Barclays Bank v CBN* (1976)).

| Maxim | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Ejusdem generis | General words after specific ones cover only the **same class** |
| Expressio unius est exclusio alterius | Express mention of one **excludes** others |
| Noscitur a sociis | A word takes meaning from **associated words** |
| Lex non cogit ad impossibilia | Law does not compel the **impossible** |
| Ut res magis valeat quam pereat | Prefer the reading that makes it **valid and effective** |

- **Precedent:** **Lord Diplock, *Carter v Bradbeer* (1975):** a ratio on words in one statute is only **persuasive** for the same words in another statute.

**Explain more**

- "Bread, meat pie, cake, doughnut, sausage and other snacks": "other snacks" covers pastries such as chin-chin, not jollof rice, fish or zobo.
- A penalty on "the Principal and/or Vice-Principal" absent without excuse does not reach teachers (expressio unius).
- Scenarios: levy charged on earlier bookings = against **retrospectivity**; offence silent on knowledge = against **liability without fault**; "no court shall entertain any action" = **preserve jurisdiction**.

### One-minute recap

- Legislation = formal, express promulgation of legal rules by the legislature (s.4(2)).
- 5 reasons: dynamic society, ideology, global values, technology, anti-social behaviour.
- Draftsman must be a lawyer; ambiguity is less serious than vagueness; open texture is deliberate.
- Statute order: preliminary, principal, miscellaneous, final, executive endorsement.
- Bill: first, second, committee, report, third reading, other House, assent; override needs two-thirds in each House.
- Consolidation = statutes only; code adds common law and equity; ultra vires subsidiary legislation is void.
- Literal (*Sussex Peerage*), golden (*Adamolekun*), mischief (*Heydon's case*), purposive: rules of practice only.
- Four presumptions and five maxims guide intention.

### Theory practice

**Question:** "The primary duty of the courts in interpreting statutes is to find the intention of the legislature" (Obilade). Discuss the rules of statutory interpretation with cases.

*Answer outline:*

- **Introduction:** s.6(6)(b) judicial power; statutes suffer ambiguity, vagueness, open texture; courts seek intention from the words.
- **Literal:** *Sussex Peerage*; weaknesses shown by *Awolowo v Shagari* and *Adegbenro v Akintola*; Dias.
- **Golden:** absurdity, no further; *Adamolekun*.
- **Mischief:** *Heydon's case* four questions; limits.
- **Purposive:** *Nafiu Rabiu v State*; Lord Simonds' warning in *Magor*.
- **Assessment:** rules of practice; Fatayi-Williams CJN; Bennion; supplement with aids, presumptions (*Barclays Bank v CBN*) and maxims.
- **Conclusion:** a combination of canons and aids, against the legislative purpose, best finds legislative intention.
