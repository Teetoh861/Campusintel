## Topic 9: Partial Equilibrium Analysis

**What you must know:** the meaning of partial equilibrium; its six assumptions (ceteris paribus at the centre); partial vs general equilibrium (Marshall vs Walras); equilibrium from equations; the effect of a per-unit tax, a subsidy, a price ceiling and a price floor in one market, with numbers; limitations; real-world examples.

### Lesson 9.1: Meaning of partial equilibrium

**Must know**

- **Partial equilibrium:** an economic analysis that examines a **single market or industry** while assuming that **all other markets remain unchanged**. It looks at demand and supply in one sector, not the whole economy.
- **Classic definition:** "A partial equilibrium is one which is based on only a **restricted range of data**; a standard example is the **price of a single product**, the prices of all other products being **held fixed** during the analysis."
- **Examples:** the equilibrium of a **single consumer**, a **single producer**, a **single firm** or a **single industry**.
- It studies the effect of a policy only in the sector **directly affected**, ignoring effects on other markets on the assumption that they are small. So it is most useful for **small (constricted) markets**.
- **Alfred Marshall** (English economist) is the **master of partial equilibrium analysis**. **Antoine Augustin Cournot** (French) and Marshall built the workable single-market models.
- **Léon Walras** first formalised **general equilibrium** (equilibrium of the whole economic system).

**Explain more**

- Think of it as a camera zoomed in on one market, say garri, with everything else in the economy frozen. You change one thing (a tax, a price control) and watch only what happens to the price and quantity of garri.
- **Trap:** options often list Marshall, Pigou, Keynes and Mill. Marshall = partial equilibrium (one market). Walras = general equilibrium (all markets). Keynes = the whole economy in aggregate (macroeconomics). Pigou = welfare and taxes on externalities. Mill = utilitarianism.

### Lesson 9.2: Assumptions of partial equilibrium analysis

**Must know**

The **6 assumptions:**

1. **Independence of markets:** the market studied is independent of other markets; prices in related markets (substitutes and complements) are held **constant**.
2. **Price-taking behaviour:** buyers and sellers are **price takers** who cannot influence the market price; this fits **perfect competition**, where no single agent has market power.
3. **Ceteris paribus (other things constant):** income, tastes, technology and input prices do not change; only **one variable** (price or quantity) is adjusted at a time.
4. **Single market focus:** only one good or service; no link to macroeconomic variables such as **inflation** or **exchange rates**.
5. **No general equilibrium feedback effects:** a change in one market does **not** affect other markets.
6. **Constant returns to scale:** no economies or diseconomies of scale; firms can change output without much change in cost.

**Explain more**

- **Ceteris paribus** is the heart of the method. It is what lets you draw one demand curve and one supply curve and treat everything else as fixed.
- Example of assumption 5: a rise in the price of wheat is assumed **not** to change wages or the prices of other crops. In real life it might, which is why this is an assumption, not a fact.

### Lesson 9.3: Partial vs general equilibrium

**Must know**

| Feature | Partial equilibrium | General equilibrium |
|---|---|---|
| Associated with | **Alfred Marshall** | **Léon Walras** |
| Scope | Focuses on **one market** | Considers **all markets** in the economy |
| Assumption | Other markets remain unchanged | All markets are **interconnected** |
| Complexity | Simpler, easier to analyse | More complex; needs **many equations** solved together |
| Example | Effect of a tax on petrol | Effect of a tax on all industries |

- **General equilibrium:** all markets interact and reach equilibrium **simultaneously**. Its key idea: **"everything in the economy depends on everything else."**
- **Partial equilibrium** says the price of a good (or a factor) is set by the demand and supply **of that good (or factor) alone**.

**Explain more**

- Partial equilibrium suits policy inside **one industry**; general equilibrium shows how **all industries** affect one another.
- **Trap:** "The price of a good is set by the demand and supply of that good alone" and "the price of a factor is set by the demand and supply of that factor alone" are **partial** equilibrium statements. Only "everything depends on everything else" describes **general** equilibrium.

### Lesson 9.4: Equilibrium, a per-unit tax and a subsidy

**Must know**

- **Market equilibrium:** where **Qd = Qs** at the equilibrium price **P\***.
- **Method:** set Qd = Qs, solve for P\*, then put P\* into either equation for Q\*.
- **Per-unit tax on producers (₦t):** sellers keep only P − t, so replace P with **(P − t)** in the supply function. Supply shifts **left (up)**: price **rises**, quantity **falls**.
- **Per-unit subsidy to producers (₦s):** sellers receive P + s, so replace P with **(P + s)**. It lowers production cost and shifts supply **right (down)**: price **falls**, quantity **rises**.

**Explain more**

**Market for corn:** Qd = 500 − 5P and Qs = 2P + 100 (P in ₦ per unit).

*Equilibrium:* 500 − 5P = 2P + 100 → 400 = 7P → **P\* = ₦57.14**; Q\* = 500 − 5(57.14) = **214.3 units**.

*A ₦10 per-unit tax on producers:* new supply Qs = 2(P − 10) + 100 = 2P + 80.
500 − 5P = 2P + 80 → 420 = 7P → **P = ₦60**; Q = 500 − 5(60) = **200 units**.

- Buyers now pay ₦60; sellers keep ₦60 − ₦10 = **₦50**.
- Buyers bear ₦60 − ₦57.14 = **₦2.86** of the tax; sellers bear ₦57.14 − ₦50 = **₦7.14**. Sellers bear more because buyers here are more responsive to price (5 units per ₦1) than sellers (2 units per ₦1); the less responsive side bears more of a tax.
- Tax revenue = ₦10 × 200 = **₦2,000**.

*A ₦10 per-unit subsidy instead:* new supply Qs = 2(P + 10) + 100 = 2P + 120.
500 − 5P = 2P + 120 → 380 = 7P → **P = ₦54.29** (about ₦54.3); Q = 500 − 5(54.29) = **228.6 units** (rounding P to 54.3 first gives 228.5).

- Buyers pay ₦54.29; sellers receive ₦54.29 + ₦10 = **₦64.29**. Cost to government = ₦10 × 228.57 ≈ **₦2,285.71**.

| Case | Supply function | Price (₦) | Quantity |
|---|---|---|---|
| No intervention | 2P + 100 | 57.14 | 214.3 |
| ₦10 tax | 2(P − 10) + 100 | 60.00 | 200.0 |
| ₦10 subsidy | 2(P + 10) + 100 | 54.29 | 228.6 |

- **Curves:** demand slopes down, supply slopes up. The tax moves the supply curve to the left (up by ₦10 at every quantity), so the new crossing point is higher and further left. The subsidy moves supply to the right (down by ₦10), so the crossing point is lower and further right. Demand does not move in either case.
- **Trap:** taxes raise prices and **reduce** output; subsidies lower prices and **increase** output. The price rises by **less** than the tax (₦2.86, not ₦10) because sellers absorb part of it.
- **Trap:** a tax on producers means P − t inside the supply function, not P + t. Swapping the signs gives the subsidy answer.

### Lesson 9.5: Price ceiling and price floor

**Must know**

- **Price ceiling:** a government-imposed **maximum** price that sellers cannot exceed (e.g. **rent control**, **fuel price limits**). Set **below** equilibrium, it causes a **shortage**: Qd > Qs.
- **Price floor:** a government-imposed **minimum** price, set **above** equilibrium, usually to support producers (e.g. farmers, **minimum wage**). It causes a **surplus**: Qs > Qd.
- **Method:** put the controlled price into **both** equations. Shortage = Qd − Qs; surplus = Qs − Qd.

**Explain more**

**Price ceiling (corn market above):** ceiling = ₦50, below the equilibrium of ₦57.14.

- Qd = 500 − 5(50) = **250**; Qs = 2(50) + 100 = **200**.
- **Shortage = 250 − 200 = 50 units.** Expect queues, rationing and a black market.

**Price floor in the wheat market:** Qd = 500 − 50P, Qs = 200 + 30P (P in ₦ per kg). The government sets a floor of ₦6 per kg to support farmers.

*Step 1, equilibrium:* 500 − 50P = 200 + 30P → 300 = 80P → **P = ₦3.75 per kg**; Q = 500 − 50(3.75) = 500 − 187.5 = **312.5 kg** (check: 200 + 30(3.75) = 312.5).

*Step 2, at the floor:* Qd = 500 − 50(6) = **200 kg**; Qs = 200 + 30(6) = **380 kg**.

- **Surplus = 380 − 200 = 180 kg.** Government may have to buy and store the excess.

| Control | Price | Qd | Qs | Result |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Ceiling on corn | ₦50 | 250 | 200 | Shortage of **50** |
| Floor on wheat | ₦6/kg | 200 kg | 380 kg | Surplus of **180 kg** |

- **Curves:** draw a horizontal line at the controlled price. A ceiling line lies **below** the crossing point: read across to demand (further right) and supply (further left); the gap is the shortage. A floor line lies **above** it: supply is now further right than demand; the gap is the surplus.
- **Trap:** a ceiling above equilibrium or a floor below it has **no effect** (it does not bind). Ceiling → shortage; floor → surplus. Never swap them.

### Lesson 9.6: Limitations and real-world examples

**Must know**

**Limitations of partial equilibrium analysis:**

1. It is **restricted to one portion** of the economy.
2. It **cannot study the interrelations** of all the parts of the economy.
3. It **fails if its unrealistic assumptions**, which cut the market off from the rest of the economy, do not hold.
4. It **cannot explain how a disturbance spreads** from one market to another, setting off **second- and third-order waves of change** across the whole economy.

**Real-world examples:**

1. **Fuel subsidies in Nigeria:** government intervention in the fuel market.
2. **Minimum wage laws:** a wage floor in the labour market.
3. **Import tariffs:** taxes on imports and their effect on domestic production.

**Explain more**

- **Fuel subsidy:** a subsidy keeps the pump price below the free-market level and keeps quantity higher (Lesson 9.4). When Nigeria removed the petrol subsidy in 2023, the pump price rose sharply, just as the model predicts. But the analysis of the petrol market alone misses the knock-on rise in transport fares and food prices: limitation 4 in action.
- **Minimum wage:** a floor above the equilibrium wage gives a **surplus of labour** (unemployment), like the wheat surplus above.
- **Import tariff:** a per-unit tax on imported rice raises its price and cuts the quantity imported, which helps local rice farmers. Partial analysis of the rice market shows this, but not the effects on other markets.

### One-minute recap

- Partial equilibrium = one market studied, all other markets held unchanged.
- **Marshall** = master of partial equilibrium; **Walras** = general equilibrium ("everything depends on everything else").
- Six assumptions: independence of markets, price-taking, ceteris paribus, single market, no feedback, constant returns to scale.
- Equilibrium: set Qd = Qs; corn gives P\* = ₦57.14, Q\* = 214.3.
- Tax on producers: P − t in supply → price up (₦60), quantity down (200).
- Subsidy: P + s in supply → price down (₦54.29), quantity up (228.6).
- Ceiling below equilibrium → shortage (250 − 200 = 50); floor above → surplus (380 − 200 = 180 kg).
- Limitations: one portion only, no interrelations, unrealistic assumptions, misses ripple effects.
- Examples: Nigerian fuel subsidy, minimum wage, import tariffs.

### Theory practice

**Question:** In the market for yam, Qd = 800 − 4P and Qs = 100 + 3P (P in ₦ per tuber). (a) Find the equilibrium. (b) The government imposes a tax of ₦35 per tuber on sellers. Find the new price paid by buyers, the price kept by sellers and the new quantity.

*Worked solution:* (a) 800 − 4P = 100 + 3P → 700 = 7P → **P\* = ₦100**; Q\* = 800 − 400 = **400 tubers**. (b) New supply: Qs = 100 + 3(P − 35) = 3P − 5. Then 800 − 4P = 3P − 5 → 805 = 7P → **buyers pay ₦115**; sellers keep ₦115 − ₦35 = **₦80**; Q = 800 − 4(115) = **340 tubers** (check: 100 + 3(80) = 340). Price rises by ₦15 (less than the tax) and quantity falls by 60.
