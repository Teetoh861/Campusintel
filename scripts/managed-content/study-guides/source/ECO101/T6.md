## Topic 6: Elasticity of Demand and Supply, and Tax Incidence

**What you must know:** the meaning of elasticity; the price elasticity of demand formula with the point and arc (midpoint) methods; the five types and what each curve looks like; determinants; the total revenue test and marginal revenue; income elasticity, Engel curves and normal, inferior, necessity and luxury goods; cross elasticity (substitutes, complements, unrelated goods); elasticity of supply, its types and determinants; tax incidence, the tax wedge, and tax base, rate, yield, avoidance and evasion.

### Lesson 6.1: Elasticity and how to compute price elasticity of demand

**Must know**

- **Elasticity of demand:** the degree of responsiveness of quantity demanded to a change in price, income or the price of a related good. Changes are measured in **percentages**. Alfred Marshall developed the concept.
- **Three kinds:** (1) **price elasticity** (response to the good's own price); (2) **income elasticity** (response to income); (3) **cross elasticity** (response to the price of another good).
- **Price elasticity of demand (PED, Ed or Ep):** the responsiveness of quantity demanded of a commodity to a change in the price of the **same** commodity.
- **Basic formula:** Ed = % change in quantity demanded ÷ % change in price.
- **Point method (from a schedule):** Ed = (ΔQ/Q) ÷ (ΔP/P) = **(ΔQ/ΔP) × (P/Q)**, using the initial P and Q.
- **Point method (from a demand function):** Ed = (dQ/dP) × (P/Q). Find Q at the given price first.
- **Arc (midpoint) method:** uses the **average** of the two prices and two quantities: Ed = (ΔQ/average Q) ÷ (ΔP/average P) = (ΔQ/ΔP) × (P1 + P2)/(Q1 + Q2). It gives the same answer whichever direction price moves.
- **Sign:** PED is negative (law of demand), so we quote it in **absolute value** (drop the minus sign).
- Price elasticity measures a **movement along** the demand curve. Income and cross elasticity measure responses that **shift** it.

**Explain more**

*Worked example 1 (simple percentages).* Price rises from ₦300 to ₦350 and quantity demanded falls from 6 to 4.

- % change in Qd = (6 − 4)/6 × 100 = 33.33%. % change in P = (350 − 300)/300 × 100 = 16.67%.
- Ed = 33.33 ÷ 16.67 = **2.0**. Since Ed > 1, demand is elastic.
- Arc method on the same data: ΔQ/average Q = 2/5 = 40%; ΔP/average P = 50/325 = 15.38%; Ed = **2.6**. Different method, different number, same verdict (elastic).

*Worked example 2 (demand function).* Q = 200 − 4.5P; find Ed at P = ₦10.

- Q = 200 − 4.5(10) = 155. dQ/dP = −4.5.
- Ed = 4.5 × 10/155 = **0.29** (inelastic). Another: Q = 100 − 3P at P = ₦20 gives Q = 40 and Ed = 3 × 20/40 = **1.5** (elastic).

- **Trap:** the formula is (ΔQ/ΔP) × (P/Q). Writing (ΔP/ΔQ) × (Q/P) gives the reciprocal; dividing by P/Q instead of multiplying is also wrong.
- **Trap:** the method that uses averages is the **arc** method; point elasticity uses one point.

### Lesson 6.2: Types of price elasticity and what the curves look like

**Must know**

| Type | Coefficient | What happens | Demand curve |
|---|---|---|---|
| Perfectly elastic | Ed = ∞ | A tiny price change causes an infinitely large change in Qd | **Horizontal**, parallel to the quantity axis |
| Elastic | 1 < Ed < ∞ | % change in Qd **greater** than % change in P | Gentle (flat) slope |
| Unitary (unit) elastic | Ed = 1 | % change in Qd **equals** % change in P | **Rectangular hyperbola** |
| Inelastic | 0 < Ed < 1 | % change in Qd **smaller** than % change in P | Steep slope |
| Perfectly inelastic | Ed = 0 | Qd does not change at all when P changes | **Vertical**, parallel to the price axis |

**Explain more**

- **Perfectly elastic:** consumers buy any amount at one price, and nothing at all if price rises even slightly.
- **Perfectly inelastic:** if price rises, quantity demanded is **unchanged**. A curve parallel to the **price axis** is vertical, hence perfectly inelastic. If Qd stays exactly the same when price falls, Ed = 0.
- **Rectangular hyperbola:** P × Q is the same at every point, so Ed = 1 everywhere and total spending does not change when price changes.
- If the percentage rise in Qd is smaller than the percentage fall in price, Ed is **less than one**.
- **Elasticity is not slope.** On a straight-line demand curve, elasticity changes from point to point:

| Point on a straight-line demand curve | Elasticity |
|---|---|
| Where it cuts the price axis | ∞ (perfectly elastic) |
| Upper half | Elastic (Ed > 1) |
| Midpoint | Unit elastic (Ed = 1) |
| Lower half | Inelastic (Ed < 1) |
| Where it cuts the quantity axis | 0 (perfectly inelastic) |

- **Trap:** "parallel to the price axis" = vertical = perfectly inelastic; "parallel to the quantity axis" = horizontal = perfectly elastic.

### Lesson 6.3: Determinants of PED, the total revenue test and marginal revenue

**Must know**

**Determinants of price elasticity of demand:**

1. **Number and closeness of substitutes** (one of the most important): more close substitutes, more elastic. Bournvita (Milo, Ovaltine are close) is elastic; food as a whole is inelastic.
2. **Proportion of income spent on the good:** a large share of income gives elastic demand; a tiny share (salt, matches) gives inelastic demand.
3. **Time:** demand is more elastic in the long period, as consumers need time to adjust (switching from an electric cooker to a gas cooker).
4. **Number of possible uses:** many uses (sugar: drinks, baking, jam) gives more elastic demand.
5. **Habit forming:** habit-forming goods (cigarettes) are inelastic.
6. **Luxuries and necessities:** luxuries can be done without, so elastic; necessities are inelastic.

**Total revenue (TR) = Price × Quantity** (equals consumers' total expenditure).

| Demand | Price rises | Price falls |
|---|---|---|
| Elastic (Ed > 1) | TR **falls** | TR **rises** |
| Inelastic (Ed < 1) | TR **rises** | TR **falls** |
| Unit elastic (Ed = 1) | TR unchanged | TR unchanged |

**Marginal revenue:** MR = P(1 − 1/Ed). Where Ed = 1, **MR = 0** and TR is at its maximum (the midpoint of a straight-line demand curve). MR is positive on the elastic upper half and negative on the inelastic lower half.

**Explain more**

*TR test with ₦ figures.*

| Case | Old P × Q | New P × Q | TR | Ed |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Elastic | ₦500 × 100 = ₦50,000 | ₦400 × 150 = ₦60,000 | rises after price cut | 50% ÷ 20% = 2.5 |
| Inelastic | ₦500 × 100 = ₦50,000 | ₦600 × 95 = ₦57,000 | rises after price rise | 5% ÷ 20% = 0.25 |

- Reverse the logic too: if a **fall** in price leads to a **fall** in total expenditure, demand is **inelastic**. A producer with Ed > 1 who cuts price by 5% sees TR **rise**. If electricity demand is inelastic, a price rise **increases** revenue.
- *MR check:* demand Q = 100 − 2P. At the midpoint, P = ₦25 and Q = 50, so Ed = 2 × 25/50 = 1 and MR = 25(1 − 1) = 0.
- **Inelastic necessities and the budget:** a consumer earns ₦200 a month and spends ₦60 on food. If food's price rises and its demand is inelastic, he keeps roughly the same quantity of food and spends **less on other goods**.
- **Income effect and elasticity:** a price change works through a substitution effect and an income effect. If a good takes a big share of a poor buyer's budget (for example, addictive drugs among addicts with low, unsteady incomes), a price rise cuts real income sharply. The **larger income effect** makes demand more elastic than expected.

### Lesson 6.4: Income elasticity, Engel curves and cross elasticity

**Must know**

- **Income elasticity of demand (YED, Ey):** the ratio of the percentage change in quantity demanded of a commodity to the percentage change in income. Ey = %ΔQd ÷ %ΔY = (ΔQ/ΔY) × (Y/Q). Arc form: (ΔQ/ΔY) × (Y1 + Y2)/(Q1 + Q2). **The sign matters.**

| Ey | Good | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| Positive (Ey > 0) | **Normal (superior)** | Consumption rises as income rises |
| Greater than 1 | **Luxury** | Demand rises more than in proportion to income |
| Between 0 and 1 | **Necessity** | Demand rises, but less than in proportion |
| Negative (Ey < 0) | **Inferior** | Demand falls as income rises; rises as income falls |
| Zero | Income has no effect | Quantity unchanged when income changes |

- Inferior goods are defined by **income**, not price. A Giffen good is an inferior good (negative Ey).
- **Engel curve:** the curve that relates the quantity of a good bought (consumed) to the consumer's **income** (named after Ernst Engel; "Angel curve" is a wrong spelling; the Lorenz curve is about income distribution).
    - **Necessities:** as income keeps rising, expenditure first increases **rapidly** but becomes **less proportional** to the rise in income (the curve flattens).
    - **Luxuries:** expenditure first increases **slowly** but later increases **more rapidly** than income.
    - **Inferior goods:** the curve rises at low incomes, then bends back as quantity falls when income rises further.
- **Cross elasticity of demand (XED, Exy):** the responsiveness of quantity demanded of one commodity to a change in the price of **another** commodity. Exy = %ΔQd of X ÷ %ΔP of Y.

| Exy | Relationship | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Positive | **Substitutes** (competitive demand) | Coke and Pepsi; yam and potato |
| Very high (towards ∞) | Close or perfect substitutes | |
| Negative | **Complements** (joint demand) | Cars and petrol; bread and butter |
| Zero | **Unrelated** (independent) | Bread and cement |

**Explain more**

*Income elasticity.* Income rises from ₦50,000 to ₦70,000 and quantity bought from 200 to 250.

- Point: (50/200) ÷ (20,000/50,000) = 0.25 ÷ 0.4 = **0.625**. Arc: (50/225) ÷ (20,000/60,000) = **0.67**. Positive and below 1: a **normal good, a necessity**.
- Income rises 20% (₦100,000 to ₦120,000) and restaurant meals rise from 10 to 14 (40%): Ey = 40 ÷ 20 = **2.0**, a **luxury**.
- Same 20% income rise, garri falls from 20 to 18 cups (−10%): Ey = −10 ÷ 20 = **−0.5**, an **inferior** good (the household moves to rice). Ey = 0.4 is a necessity.

*Cross elasticity.* Price of good B rises from ₦50 to ₦60 (20%); quantity of good A falls from 300 to 200 (−33.33%). Exy = −33.33 ÷ 20 = **−1.67**: complements. If Coke rises from ₦300 to ₦360 (20%) and Pepsi sales rise from 100 to 130 (30%), Exy = **+1.5**: substitutes.

- **Trap:** if the quantity of one good is unchanged when the other's price changes, Exy = 0 and the goods are **not related**. Jointly demanded goods are complements (negative), not substitutes.

### Lesson 6.5: Elasticity of supply

**Must know**

- **Elasticity of supply (PES, Es):** the responsiveness of quantity supplied to a change in the good's own price. Es = %ΔQs ÷ %ΔP = (ΔQ/ΔP) × (P/Q). Arc: (ΔQ/ΔP) × (P1 + P2)/(Q1 + Q2). Es is **positive** (no minus sign) because supply slopes upward.

| Type | Es | Supply curve |
|---|---|---|
| Perfectly elastic | ∞ | **Horizontal** (parallel to the quantity axis): sellers offer any amount at one price, nothing below it |
| Elastic | > 1 | Straight line cutting the **price axis** (above the origin) |
| Unit elastic | = 1 | Straight line **through the origin** (any slope) |
| Inelastic | < 1 | Straight line cutting the **quantity axis** |
| Perfectly inelastic | 0 | **Vertical**: quantity fixed whatever the price |

**Determinants of elasticity of supply:**

1. **Time period (gestation period):** inelastic in the short run; more elastic in the long run as all factors can change. Crops have a lag between planting and harvest.
2. **Availability and mobility of factors (resources):** readily available, mobile factors make supply elastic.
3. **Production flexibility:** flexible processes give elastic supply.
4. **Storage capacity and perishability:** easily stored goods are elastic; highly perishable goods (tomatoes, fresh fish) are inelastic.
5. **Cost structure and cost of production:** if output can be raised without costs rising much, supply is elastic; if costs rise sharply as output expands, supply is inelastic.
6. **Weather:** output controlled by weather (farm produce) is inelastic.
7. **Level of technology:** advanced technology makes supply more elastic.

**Explain more**

*Worked example.* Price rises from ₦10 to ₦12 (20%); quantity supplied rises from 200 to 260 (30%). Es = 30 ÷ 20 = **1.5**: elastic. Arc method: (60/230) ÷ (2/11) = **1.43**, still elastic.

- **Why the axis rule works:** a line through the origin is Q = cP, so Es = c × P/(cP) = **1** at every point. A line cutting the price axis at k is Q = c(P − k), so Es = P/(P − k), always **above 1**. A line cutting the quantity axis at k is Q = k + cP, so Es = cP/(k + cP), always **below 1**.

### Lesson 6.6: Tax incidence and basic tax terms

**Must know**

- **Tax:** a compulsory levy paid to government without any direct service in return.
- **Tax base:** the **object** to which tax is applied (income for income tax; spending for VAT).
- **Tax rate:** the proportion of the base paid as tax. A 5% tax on income: 5% is the **rate**, income is the **base**.
- **Tax yield:** the revenue government actually collects.
- **Tax avoidance:** a perfectly **legal** way of keeping down the tax you pay (allowances, exemptions). **Tax evasion:** **illegal** refusal to pay or hiding of income.
- **Tax incidence:** how the burden of a tax is shared between buyers (consumers) and sellers (producers).
- **Tax wedge:** the difference between the price buyers pay and the price sellers receive after a tax; it equals the tax per unit.
- **Rule: the less elastic side bears more of the tax.**

| Situation | Who bears the tax |
|---|---|
| Demand perfectly inelastic | **Buyer** bears all; price rises by the full tax |
| Demand perfectly elastic | **Seller** bears all |
| Demand less elastic than supply | Mostly buyers |
| Supply less elastic than demand | Mostly **producers** |
| Demand and supply equally elastic | **Both**, shared equally |
| Supply perfectly inelastic | Seller bears all |
| Supply perfectly elastic | Buyer bears all |

**Explain more**

*Worked ₦ example.* Demand Qd = 100 − 2P, supply Qs = −20 + 4P (P in ₦, Q in thousand units).

1. Before tax: 100 − 2P = −20 + 4P, so 6P = 120, **P = ₦20, Q = 60**.
2. Government charges sellers ₦6 per unit. Sellers now need ₦6 more at each quantity: Qs = −20 + 4(P − 6) = −44 + 4P. Supply shifts **upward (left)** by ₦6.
3. New equilibrium: 100 − 2P = −44 + 4P, so 6P = 144, **P = ₦24, Q = 52**.

| Item | Value |
|---|---|
| Price buyers pay | ₦24 (up ₦4) |
| Price sellers keep | ₦24 − ₦6 = ₦18 (down ₦2) |
| Wedge | ₦24 − ₦18 = ₦6 = the tax |
| Buyers' share | ₦4 of ₦6 (two-thirds); ₦4 × 52 = ₦208 thousand |
| Sellers' share | ₦2 of ₦6 (one-third); ₦2 × 52 = ₦104 thousand |
| Tax yield | ₦6 × 52 = ₦312 thousand |

- Why buyers pay more here: at P = ₦20, Q = 60, Ed = 2 × 20/60 = 0.67 and Es = 4 × 20/60 = 1.33. Demand is less elastic, so buyers carry the larger share. Buyers' share = Es ÷ (Es + Ed) = 1.33 ÷ 2 = two-thirds.
- **Trap:** the wedge is the price gap; incidence is who bears the burden. Avoidance is legal; evasion is not.

### One-minute recap

- Ed = %ΔQd ÷ %ΔP = (ΔQ/ΔP) × (P/Q); the arc method uses averages; quote PED without its sign.
- Horizontal = perfectly elastic (∞); vertical = perfectly inelastic (0); rectangular hyperbola = unit elastic.
- Substitutes, share of income, time, uses, habit and luxury vs necessity decide PED.
- Elastic: price and TR move opposite ways. Inelastic: same way. Unit: TR unchanged. MR = 0 where Ed = 1.
- Ey: positive = normal; above 1 = luxury; 0 to 1 = necessity; negative = inferior. Engel curve links quantity to income.
- Exy: positive = substitutes; negative = complements; zero = unrelated.
- Supply lines: through origin = 1; cut price axis = elastic; cut quantity axis = inelastic.
- The less elastic side bears more tax; the wedge equals the tax per unit; avoidance is legal, evasion is not.

### Theory practice

**Question:** Explain four determinants of price elasticity of demand, and state what happens to total revenue when the price of a good with inelastic demand rises.

*Answer outline:* Define PED (%ΔQd ÷ %ΔP). Then 1-2 lines each: availability of close substitutes (more substitutes, more elastic); proportion of income spent (large share, elastic; salt, inelastic); time (more elastic in the long run); habit forming or necessity (inelastic). Total revenue **rises**, because quantity falls by a smaller percentage than price rises (₦500 × 100 = ₦50,000 becomes ₦600 × 95 = ₦57,000).
