## Topic 1: Nature of Economic Science

**What you must know:** the five groups of definitions and who gave each; scarcity, choice and the scale of preference; economic agents, incentives, self-interest and social interest; opportunity cost and how to compute it; marginal benefit and marginal cost; the what, how and for whom questions; economic systems; the production possibility frontier.

### Lesson 1.1: Definitions and scope of economics

**Must know**

- **Economics:** the social science that studies how people use scarce resources to produce goods and services to satisfy unlimited wants. The most widely accepted modern summary: **the science of making choices under scarcity**, or the study of the choices individuals, businesses, governments and societies make **to cope with scarcity**. Its subject matter is **scarcity and choice**.
- It studies the **production, exchange (distribution) and consumption** of goods and services, and how society decides **what, how and for whom** to produce.
- It is a **social science**: it studies people's relationships with, and interactions within, society.

| Definition | Economist (book, year) | Key words | Weakness |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Wealth** | **Adam Smith** ("Father of Economics"), *The Wealth of Nations*, 1776; also **J.B. Say** | "An inquiry into the nature and causes of the wealth of nations." Say: the science of creating, preserving and increasing wealth | Too much on wealth, ignores human welfare |
| **Welfare** | **Alfred Marshall**, *Principles of Economics*, 1890 | "A study of mankind in the ordinary business of life"; material welfare; "on the one side a study of wealth, and on the other, and more important side, a part of the study of man" | Ignores scarcity and choice |
| **Scarcity** | **Lionel Robbins**, *An Essay on the Nature and Significance of Economic Science*, 1932 | "The science which studies human behaviour as a relationship between ends and scarce means which have alternative uses" | Ignores growth, welfare, social context |
| **Growth** | **Paul A. Samuelson**, *Economics*, 1948; also **Simon Kuznets** | How society chooses, with or without money, to employ scarce resources with alternative uses to produce commodities **over time** and distribute them **now or in the future** | (Strength: brings in time and growth) |
| **Modern** | **J.M. Keynes** (1936), **Joseph Schumpeter**, **Amartya Sen** | Keynes: scarce resources and the determinants of **income and employment**; Sen: capabilities, poverty, inequality, quality of life | |

**Robbins's scarcity definition** (the most widely quoted) has four ideas:

1. **Ends** = human wants, which are **unlimited**.
2. **Scarce means** = the limited resources (factors of production: land, labour, capital, entrepreneurship) available to satisfy wants. "Means" simply means **available economic resources**.
3. **Alternative uses** = each resource can be used in different ways, so we must choose.
4. Wants differ in urgency, so they are ranked on a **scale of preference**. The scale of preference belongs to the **scarcity definition**.

**Explain more**

- **Scope:** economics splits into **microeconomics** (individual consumers, firms, markets: price, consumer behaviour, production, costs, market structures) and **macroeconomics** (the whole economy: national income, unemployment, inflation, growth, fiscal and monetary policy). The terms "micro" and "macro" were coined by **Ragnar Frisch**. Its fields include development, environmental, international and public economics.
- **Goods and services** are all the objects we value and are willing to pay for. Goods are physical (rice, cars); services are not (banking, medical care, transport).
- **Economic goods** are scarce, need scarce resources to produce and therefore **command a price**. **Free (non-economic) goods** are unlimited in supply and cost nothing: air, sunshine, stream water. The **opportunity cost of a free good is zero**.
- The word **"economic"** refers to something **scarce, limited and commanding a price** (all three). It is most closely linked with the word **scarce**.
- **Consumption** = using goods and services to satisfy wants. **Producer** = anyone who provides goods and services. **Utility** = satisfaction from consuming.
- Trap: "the study of mankind in the ordinary business of life" and "the science of material welfare" are **Marshall** (welfare), not Robbins. "Economics is the science of wealth" is **Smith/Say**. The growth definition is about **economic growth over time**, and it is **Samuelson's**, not Friedman's.

### Lesson 1.2: Scarcity, choice, agents and incentives

**Must know**

- **Scarcity:** resources are **limited relative to wants**; people cannot satisfy all their wants. It arises from **limited resources (land, labour, capital, entrepreneurship) and unlimited wants**. It is **the basic, fundamental or central economic problem of every society**, and every economic question is, at root, about **how to cope with scarcity**.
- **Choice:** because of scarcity, people **must make choices**. Making a choice means giving up something to get something else.
- **Scale of preference:** a list of wants arranged in **order of priority or importance**. A rational agent satisfies the most pressing wants first.
- **Economic agents** (decision-makers):
  1. **Consumers (households):** buy goods and services to **maximise utility** within their income (budget constraint).
  2. **Firms:** produce goods and services to **maximise profit**.
  3. **Government:** regulates, provides **public goods** (defence, street lights), redistributes income, corrects market failures (e.g. pollution).
- **Incentive:** a **reward or a penalty** that encourages a person to take a particular action. It can be either a reward or a penalty.
- **Self-interest:** choices you think are best **for you**. **Social interest:** choices that are best **for society as a whole**.

**Explain more**

What scarcity does and does not do:

| Scarcity... | True or false |
|---|---|
| Is permanent and **cannot be eliminated** (not by markets, new resources or careful use) | True |
| Affects **both the rich and the poor**, and **all economies, developed or not** | True |
| Applies to **both money and time** | True |
| Makes economic goods command prices; forces choices; creates the need to study economics | True |
| Means wants will always **exceed** what can be obtained (effective demand) | True |
| Makes resources readily available | **False** |
| Means only the bare necessities can be bought | False |
| Gives rise to opportunity cost (not the other way round) | True |

- There would be **no economic problem** only in a society whose wants could be **more than fully satisfied** by its resources. One man on a desert island still faces scarcity.
- A wealthy Lagos businessman who cannot get a ticket to a sold-out concert faces **scarcity**. The CBN Governor choosing between meeting the Minister of Finance or a National Assembly committee in one afternoon faces **scarcity** (of time).
- Economic agents make choices **because the resources at their disposal are scarce**, not because goods are readily available.
- **Incentives at work:** Lagos State promising a tax cut if a firm stays in Lagos is an **incentive** designed to make the **marginal benefit of staying exceed its marginal cost**. A fine for late tax filing is a penalty incentive.
- In a market economy, what people do in pursuit of their **self-interest usually promotes the social interest** (Adam Smith's "invisible hand"), but not always. When an economist says a petrol tax is "in the social interest", she means it benefits **society as a whole**, not just most people, everyone, or some groups.

### Lesson 1.3: Opportunity cost and trade-offs

**Must know**

- **Opportunity cost:** the value of the **next best alternative forgone** (the **highest-valued alternative given up**) when a choice is made. It is also called **real cost** or **economic cost**. In short: the **alternative forgone**.
- It measures the cost of anything we have chosen **relative to the alternatives** that could have been chosen, i.e. the cost of alternatives **not consumed now**.
- **Trade-off:** giving up one thing to get another. Every choice involves a trade-off and an opportunity cost. There is **no such thing as a free lunch**.
- Link: **opportunity costs arise because of scarcity**. Rich economies also face opportunity costs.
- Formulas:
  - Opportunity cost of A (in terms of B) = **quantity of B given up ÷ quantity of A gained**
  - Opportunity cost of A in terms of B = **price of A ÷ price of B** (this ratio is the **relative price** of A)

**Explain more**

Rules for picking the opportunity cost:

1. Rank the options. The opportunity cost is **only the next best one**, never the sum of all the others. Chidi ranks library, gym, breakfast with friends, sleep. If he chooses the library, his opportunity cost is **the gym** alone.
2. If the next best option is not stated, the answer is "whichever he would have done instead". Tunde choosing a holiday gives up **working or repainting, whichever he would have done**.
3. Free tickets still carry an opportunity cost: the **time** could have gone elsewhere. Two people doing the same thing can have **different** opportunity costs.
4. A gain that involves no sacrifice is **not** an opportunity cost (Halima earning more and buying a car while moving to a bigger flat gives up nothing).

**Worked examples (computation).**

- **Choice between goods:** Ada buys a ₦2,000 cinema ticket instead of two novels costing ₦2,000. Opportunity cost = **the two novels**, not the ticket.
- **Choice of job:** Seun takes a job at ₦4.5m. His other offers: consulting ₦4.2m (preferred) and supermarket ₦3.8m. Opportunity cost = **₦4.2m** only.
- **Changing choices:** Dayo can play football (₦200m) or coach (₦5m); he plays, so the opportunity cost of football = **₦5m**. Later he quits football for acting at ₦300m; the opportunity cost of acting = **₦200m** (football, which he gives up).
- **University:** the opportunity cost of one year at university includes **at least one year of earnings** from paid employment.
- **Score:** Bisi partied and scored 60%; studying would have given 70%. Opportunity cost = **10 percentage points**.
- **Money spent plus earnings lost:** Adaeze's holiday costs ₦200,000 and she misses ₦75,000 of pay. Opportunity cost = 200,000 + 75,000 = **₦275,000**.
- **Count only what changes because of the choice.** Kemi's holiday courses:

| Item | If she takes courses | If she skips | Cost of choosing courses |
|---|---|---|---|
| Fees and books | ₦120,000 | 0 | ₦120,000 |
| Accommodation | ₦40,000 | 0 (home) | ₦40,000 |
| Food | ₦30,000 | ₦10,000 | ₦20,000 |
| Earnings | ₦100,000 | ₦500,000 | ₦400,000 lost |
| **Opportunity cost** | | | **₦580,000** |

- **Relative price:** the **ratio** of one price to another (not the difference). Jollof rice ₦1,000, chocolate ₦200: relative price of rice = 1,000 ÷ 200 = **5 chocolate bars**; of chocolate = **1/5 plate of rice**. Meat pie ₦500, shawarma ₦1,000: relative price (opportunity cost) of a meat pie = 500 ÷ 1,000 = **1/2 shawarma**; of a shawarma = **2 meat pies**.
- **Production:** with the same resources Bola can make 2 cakes or 3 clothes. Opportunity cost of 1 cake = 3 ÷ 2 = **1.5 clothes**; of 1 cloth = 2 ÷ 3 = **2/3 of a cake**.
- **Society:** resources used to build a dam cannot build a highway; tax revenue spent on national security gives up education and health. Both are **opportunity cost**.
- Trap: opportunity cost is **not** money paid (that is accounting or money cost), **not** "the sum of all alternatives", and trade-offs do not let you avoid it. "Fallacy of composition" (what is true for one is true for all) and "post hoc fallacy" (A came before B so A caused B) are reasoning errors, not opportunity cost. Comparative advantage means producing at a **lower opportunity cost** than others.

### Lesson 1.4: Marginal analysis: benefit and cost at the margin

**Must know**

- **Choosing at the margin:** deciding whether to do **a little more or a little less** of an activity (one more hour, one more unit), not comparing totals.
- **Marginal benefit (MB):** the benefit that comes from **increasing** an activity; the **extra gain from one more unit**.
- **Marginal cost (MC):** the cost that comes from **increasing** an activity; the **cost of a small increase** in it. MC = change in total cost ÷ change in quantity.
- **Decision rule:** take an action **whenever MB exceeds MC**. If MC > MB, an alternative action is chosen.
- If MC **falls** or MB **rises**, people do **more** of the activity.
- **Benefit/cost analysis:** comparing the gains and sacrifices of a decision (e.g. visiting a friend instead of studying).

**Explain more**

- **Pens:** 5 pens cost ₦150, 6 pens cost ₦175. MC of the 6th pen = 175 − 150 = **₦25**.
- **Overtime:** normal hours pay ₦1,500; each hour beyond 40 pays ₦1,800. MB of each overtime hour = **₦1,800** (not the ₦300 difference).
- **Study:** 2 hours a day gives 85%; 3 hours gives 90%. MB of the third hour = the **5-point** rise (the extra hour is the cost, not the benefit). The MB is the benefit of **the extra hour**, not of all 3 hours.
- **Firm decision:** a Lagos phone assembler can add 4,000 handsets a month, raising revenue by ₦10m and costs by ₦9m. MB ₦10m > MC ₦9m, so **go ahead**. The total profit of ₦30m is irrelevant; marginal decisions compare **extra revenue with extra cost**, not totals and not average cost.
- **Hiring:** the MC of one more worker depends on **what is paid to that new worker**, not the whole wage bill.
- **Shop hours:** a Yaba shop open 8 a.m. to 4 p.m. thinking of one more hour compares the benefit of **that hour** with its cost; if it stays open, MB must be **at least equal to** MC.
- **Extra credit:** Obinna will do a 10-hour task for 20 extra points only if he values **the 20 points more than the 10 hours**.
- If you move from 1 to 2 hours of study a day, it is because the **MB of an hour exceeded its MC**. Zainab, after 5 hours of cleaning, deciding on one more hour is **choosing at the margin**.
- Trap: "act whenever MB is positive" or "only if MC is zero" are wrong; the rule is **MB > MC**.

### Lesson 1.5: The basic economic questions and economic systems

**Must know**

Scarcity forces every society to answer:

1. **What** (and how much) to produce: which goods and services, in what quantities.
2. **How** to produce: which methods, resources and technology (labour-intensive or capital-intensive).
3. **For whom** to produce: how output and income are shared among people.
4. (Often added) **How to provide for future growth.**

All of them together are **the central economic problem**; trade-offs arise in each. "Why", "when" and "where" are **not** basic questions.

**Economic system:** the mechanism by which a society allocates scarce resources. Common systems:

1. **Traditional:** custom and tradition decide; mostly subsistence.
2. **Command** (socialist, centrally planned): **government makes all decisions** on production and consumption; state ownership of land and capital; prices fixed by the state; limited market mechanism.
3. **Pure market** (capitalist, free enterprise, laissez-faire): private ownership; questions answered by the **price mechanism** (free interaction of demand and supply).
4. **Mixed:** **government and private sector interact**; the state **intervenes in market transactions**. Most real-world economies, including Nigeria's, are mixed.

**Explain more**

Classify by keyword:

| Decision | Question |
|---|---|
| More houses, fewer typewriters; shawarma instead of burgers; fried chicken rather than pounded yam; another season of a reality show | **What** |
| Road built with few machines and much labour; dam built mainly with labour; few skilled workers instead of many unskilled; online booking instead of phone | **How** |
| Lawyer earns ₦50,000 an hour, mechanic ₦5,000; ₦100m signing bonus; higher tax on top earners | **For whom** (economists study **income differences**) |

- **Market economy features:** individuals act in their **self-interest**, **incentives** drive behaviour, prices set by **demand and supply**, consumer sovereignty, competition, activities governed by a **legal framework**. Markets with no government intervention are **free markets**. In a market economy the state provides **laws of contract, laws of ownership (property rights) and stable-valued money**, but **not laws controlling prices**. Prices are **never centrally controlled** in a market economy.
- In the **classical** (free-market) view, the basic problems are solved by **the price mechanism**, not government.
- **Command economy:** priorities (even in farming) are set by **the state, not farmers**.
- **Market economy:** + efficiency, innovation, consumer choice, growth; − monopoly, inequality, production of luxuries for the rich, unemployment and booms and slumps. **Command economy:** + less inequality, no wasteful competition; − misallocation, loss of consumer sovereignty, bureaucracy, poor quality. **Mixed economy** tries to combine the strengths of both, but can suffer from an inefficient public sector.

### Lesson 1.6: The production possibility frontier (PPF)

**Must know**

- **PPF:** a curve showing the **maximum** combinations of two goods an economy can produce when it uses **all its resources fully and efficiently** with given technology; the maximum of one good for every level of the other. Also called **PPC**, **PPB** (boundary) or **transformation curve**.
- **Assumptions:** (1) only **two goods** are produced; (2) resources are **fixed (limited)**; (3) technology is **fixed**; (4) resources are **fully and efficiently employed**. It is **not** assumed that resources are sufficient for all production.
- It illustrates **scarcity, choice and opportunity cost**.

| Point | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **On** the curve | Full, efficient use; **could be optimal** |
| **Inside** the curve | Some resources **unemployed or used inefficiently**; output can rise by **using resources more efficiently** |
| **Outside** the curve | **Unattainable** with present resources and technology |

**Explain more**

- **Shape:** slopes **downward** (more of one means less of the other) and is **concave (bowed out)** from the origin, because resources are not equally good at both goods, so **opportunity cost rises** as you produce more of one good. If opportunity cost is **constant**, the PPF is a **straight line**. The slope is the **marginal rate of transformation (MRT)** and measures opportunity cost.

**Worked schedule (rice and cement):**

| Point | Rice (m bags) | Cement (m bags) | Opportunity cost of the extra 1m bags of rice |
|---|---|---|---|
| A | 0 | 100 | |
| B | 1 | 95 | 5 cement |
| C | 2 | 85 | 10 cement |
| D | 3 | 70 | 15 cement |
| E | 4 | 50 | 20 cement |
| F | 5 | 0 | 50 cement |

Opportunity cost of rice rises (5, 10, 15, 20, 50), so the curve is bowed out. Moving from B to D: 2m bags of rice gained, 25m bags of cement given up, so each bag of rice costs 25 ÷ 2 = **12.5 bags of cement** on average. Point (2, 60) is **inside** (inefficient: 85 was possible). Point (4, 80) is **outside** (unattainable: only 50 is possible).

- **Shifts:**
  1. **Outward (rightward)**: more resources, better technology, more education or skills, new discoveries. This is **economic growth**.
  2. **Inward (leftward)**: depletion of resources, war, natural disaster, emigration of workers.
  3. **Pivot (rotation)**: a change that affects **one industry only**, e.g. better rice technology moves only the rice end outward.
- Moving along the curve is a trade-off; moving from inside to the curve needs **no** sacrifice; moving beyond the curve needs growth.
- Trap: a point inside is **less** efficient than a point on the curve, not more. A point outside is not "achievable" or "desirable"; it is **unattainable**. The PPF is **not** an indifference curve or a demand curve.

### One-minute recap

- Definitions: wealth (Smith, Say), welfare (Marshall), scarcity (Robbins), growth (Samuelson, Kuznets), modern (Keynes, Schumpeter, Sen).
- Robbins: ends (unlimited wants), scarce means (resources) with alternative uses; scale of preference.
- Scarcity = limited resources, unlimited wants; permanent, affects rich and poor, money and time; it forces choice.
- Opportunity cost = next best alternative forgone; count only costs and lost earnings caused by the choice; free good = zero.
- Opportunity cost of A in terms of B = qty of B given up ÷ qty of A gained = P(A) ÷ P(B).
- Marginal analysis: act when MB > MC; incentives are rewards or penalties.
- What, how, for whom (plus future growth); answered by the price mechanism in a market economy, by the state in a command economy, by both in a mixed economy.
- PPF: on = efficient, inside = idle resources, outside = unattainable; concave because opportunity cost rises; shifts out with growth.

### Theory practice

**Question:** Using the table, find the opportunity cost of producing the 3rd unit of guns and say whether the combination 2 guns and 10 bags of rice is attainable and efficient.

| Guns | 0 | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Rice (bags) | 20 | 18 | 14 | 8 | 0 |

*Worked solution:* Going from 2 to 3 guns cuts rice from 14 to 8 bags, so the 3rd gun costs 14 − 8 = **6 bags of rice**. At 2 guns the economy can make up to 14 bags, so 2 guns with 10 bags is **attainable but inefficient** (a point inside the PPF: some resources are idle or badly used). Opportunity costs of 2, 4, 6, 8 bags rise, so the PPF is bowed out.
