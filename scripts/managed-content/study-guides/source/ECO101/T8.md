## Topic 8: Production, Costs and the Rewards of Factors

**What you must know:** the four factors of production and their rewards; human capital and the entrepreneur; the production function, short run and long run; TP, AP and MP from a table; the law of diminishing returns and the three stages of production; isoquants and MRTS; returns to scale; internal and external economies and diseconomies of scale; fixed, variable, total, average and marginal costs; explicit and implicit costs, normal and economic profit; short-run and long-run cost curves; TR, AR and MR; marginal productivity theory, MRP and VMP, wages, economic rent and transfer earnings.

### Lesson 8.1: Production and the factors of production

**Must know**

- **Production:** combining inputs to create goods and services that satisfy human wants. The **purpose of production is to satisfy people's wants**. Kinds: **primary** (extraction: farming, mining), **secondary** (manufacturing: wheat to flour to bread), **tertiary** (services: teachers, doctors, retailers).
- **Factors of production (inputs, resources):** the inputs used to produce goods and services. Early (classical) economists named **three: land, labour and capital**; today there are **four**:

| Factor | Meaning | Reward (factor payment) |
|---|---|---|
| **Land** | All gifts of nature: soil, minerals, water, forests, wildlife, the air above. Crude oil in the Niger Delta is land. | **Rent** |
| **Labour** | Human effort, physical and mental, skilled or unskilled. | **Wages** (salary) |
| **Capital** | Man-made aids to production: tools, machines, buildings, vehicles. A car factory's assembly line; the bottling machine Kunle operates. | **Interest** |
| **Entrepreneurship** | The special human effort that organises the other three factors and **bears the risk** of production. | **Profit** |

- **Human-related factors:** **labour and entrepreneurship**. Resources can also be grouped as land resources, capital resources, **human** resources and innovators (entrepreneurs).
- **Factor payments:** the general term for the money payments made to factors. **Factor markets** exchange a factor service for a **monetary payment (price)**, and so allocate factor services among productive activities.
- **Human capital:** the **accumulated knowledge and skill** of workers, built through education, training and experience.

**Explain more**

- **Land:** fixed in supply, geographically immobile, but its use can change. **Labour:** mobile; the worker cannot be separated from his labour. **Capital:** **fixed capital** (machines, buildings: not used up) and **circulating (working) capital** (raw materials, work in progress: used up). Capital depreciates. Its key role is to **make labour more productive**.
- **The entrepreneur** organises and combines land, labour and capital, bears risk (profit or loss), makes the key decisions and innovates (new ideas on what and how to produce). He does **not** have to own all the other resources, and he does **not** directly decide **for whom** goods are produced (that is settled by incomes in the economy).
- **Investment in human capital:** university training to become an engineer; Joy training as a chef; a firm sending its sales staff on courses; a student learning programming. **Trap:** a student **buying a laptop** is physical capital, not human capital.
- **Trap, not factors:** **money** (financial capital only buys factors) and **wages paid** (a payment to a factor). Water cooling a generator is land; a business owner's management skill is entrepreneurship; overtime by a supermarket assistant is labour earning wages.
- **Division of labour:** breaking production into small tasks, each done by a different worker (specialisation of tasks). It raises skill and productivity, saves time, encourages machines, and paved the way for **mass production** at falling unit cost.
- **Labour supply** in a country depends on the **total population**, the **proportion available for employment**, and the **hours worked** per person per year (all of these). **Labour productivity** = **output per worker employed**.

### Lesson 8.2: The production function, TP, AP, MP and diminishing returns

**Must know**

- **Production function:** the technical relationship showing the **maximum output** obtainable from given inputs with a given technology: **Q = f(L, K, T)** (labour, capital, technology). It is purely technical, reflects the state of technology, contains **no prices**, and is a **flow** (output per period). Cost functions are **derived from** the production function.
- **Short run:** a period in which **at least one input is fixed** (e.g. plant) and others are variable; too short to change all inputs. **Long run:** a period long enough to change **all** inputs, including plant size and capacity.
- **Total product (TP):** total output from the variable input with the fixed inputs held constant.
- **Average product:** **AP = TP ÷ L** (output per worker).
- **Marginal product:** **MP = ΔTP ÷ ΔL**, the change in TP from **one more unit of labour, other inputs held constant**.
- **Law of diminishing (marginal) returns** (also called the **law of variable proportions**): as more units of a variable input are added to a fixed input, **MP may rise at first but eventually falls**. It applies **only when at least one factor is fixed** (a short-run law).

**Three stages of production:**

1. **Stage I (increasing returns):** from zero to the **maximum of AP**; MP > AP and TP rises (at first at an increasing rate).
2. **Stage II (diminishing returns):** from maximum AP to **maximum TP (MP = 0)**; TP rises at a decreasing rate. The rational stage to produce in.
3. **Stage III (negative returns):** **MP is negative** and TP falls.

**Explain more**

Worked table: a maize farm with fixed land, adding workers.

| Workers (L) | TP (bags) | MP | AP |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | 0 | – | – |
| 1 | 10 | 10 | 10 |
| 2 | 26 | 16 | 13 |
| 3 | 45 | **19** (max) | 15 |
| 4 | 62 | 17 | **15.5** (max) |
| 5 | 75 | 13 | 15 |
| 6 | 84 | 9 | 14 |
| 7 | 84 | **0** | 12 |
| 8 | 80 | **−4** | 10 |

- Method: MP = new TP − old TP (e.g. 4th worker: 62 − 45 = 17); AP = TP ÷ L (62 ÷ 4 = 15.5).
- Diminishing marginal returns set in **after the 3rd worker**. Stage I ends at L = 4 (AP max), Stage II ends at L = 7 (MP = 0, TP max), Stage III follows.
- **Shapes and rules:**
  - MP **rising** → TP rises at an **increasing** rate (gains from specialisation: a rising marginal physical product).
  - MP at its **maximum** → TP is still increasing (its point of inflexion).
  - MP **falling but positive** → TP rises at a **decreasing** rate. This is diminishing returns.
  - **MP = 0 → TP is at its maximum.** MP negative → TP falling.
  - MP > AP → AP rising; MP < AP → AP falling; **MP = AP at the maximum of AP** (MP cuts AP from above at its peak). When MP = AP, TP is positive and rising.
  - AP rises and falls but stays positive while TP is positive.
- **Why returns diminish:** each extra worker has **less land and capital to work with**, so the workplace becomes crowded and harder to manage. It is **not** because later workers are less skilled (the law assumes identical units).
- **In percentages:** with diminishing returns to labour, a 3% rise in employment (capital fixed) raises output by **less than 3%**; a 4% rise in capital alone raises output by **less than 4%**. Raising capital per worker in equal steps raises output per worker by **successively smaller amounts**; a fall in capital with workers fixed **reduces output**.
- **Linear case Y = A × N** (A constant): labour productivity Y/N = **A**; extra workers needed for one more unit = **1/A**; with wage W, **marginal cost = W/A**. Example: A = 5 bags per worker, W = ₦10,000 → MC = ₦2,000 per bag.
- **Isoquant:** the locus of all combinations of two inputs (labour and capital) that give the **same output** (the technically efficient methods for that output). Its **slope** is the **marginal rate of technical substitution (MRTS)**: the rate at which labour can replace capital with output unchanged. The **principle of substitution** is using some of one factor in place of another. The least-cost (profit-maximising) mix is where **MRTS = ratio of the prices of labour and capital** (w/r, the slope of the **isocost** line). Methods of production change when the **relative** prices of factors change.

### Lesson 8.3: Returns to scale and economies of scale

**Must know**

- **Returns to scale:** how output responds in the **long run** when **all inputs** change in the same proportion.

| If all inputs rise by x% and output rises by | Name |
|---|---|
| **more** than x% | **Increasing returns to scale** |
| **exactly** x% | **Constant returns to scale** |
| **less** than x% | **Decreasing returns to scale** |

- **Economies of scale:** cost advantages of large scale: **cost per unit (LRAC) falls** as the scale of output rises. **Diseconomies of scale:** **cost per unit rises** as scale grows too large. Both are **long-run** concepts, and together they explain why the **LRAC curve is U-shaped**.

**Internal economies** (enjoyed by one firm as **it** grows):

1. **Technical:** large, specialised machines, standby machines, fuller use of indivisible equipment.
2. **Marketing:** bulk buying with discounts, bulk distribution, lower advertising cost per unit.
3. **Financial:** loans at lower interest (collateral), easy share issues, supplier credit.
4. **Managerial:** specialist managers, mechanised administration.
5. **Research and development:** profits set aside to develop products.
6. Welfare (better staff facilities) and risk-bearing (diversification).

**External economies** (enjoyed by all firms as the **industry** grows or concentrates in one area): skilled labour pool and training institutions; shared research; **common services** (roads, power, water, transport, telecoms, banks, marketing arrangements); subsidiary supplier industries.

**Internal diseconomies:** management and coordination problems, bureaucracy and red tape, slow decisions, low worker morale and alienation.

**External diseconomies:** rising raw-material prices, rising wages of scarce skilled labour, excess marketing costs, congestion and pollution as the industry grows beyond its optimum.

**Explain more**

- Computation: inputs +60%, output +50% → **decreasing**. Inputs +50%, output +50% → **constant**. Inputs +25%, output +33% → **increasing**. Inputs +20%, output +20% → constant. Under constant returns, both L and K up 2% → output up **2%**; both down 3% → output down **3%**.
- Doubling inputs and **more than doubling** output is increasing returns; where that option is missing, choose **economies of scale** (with factor prices unchanged, increasing returns produce falling unit cost).
- A firm enjoying economies of scale over a range has a **falling** LRAC there. A wider gas pipeline lowering the average cost per unit carried is an economy of scale (technical).
- **Trap:** economies of scale are **long run** (all inputs change). Diminishing returns are **short run** (one input fixed). Do not mix them.
- Example: Dangote cement plants buying gypsum in bulk (internal marketing) versus firms in Aba gaining from a pool of trained tailors and shared suppliers (external).

### Lesson 8.4: Cost concepts and the short-run cost table

**Must know**

- **The firm's goal** in conventional theory is **profit maximisation**, which implies **minimising the cost of each output level**; output maximisation is subject to a **cost constraint**. Profit = revenue − cost.
- **Cost to the economist = opportunity cost** of the resources used (the value of the next best alternative forgone).
- **Explicit costs:** money payments to hire or buy factors (wages, rent paid, interest, materials); the money costs an **accountant records**.
- **Implicit costs:** the value of resources **owned and used by the owner**, with no cash paid: the salary the owner gives up, rent forgone on his own building.
- **Accounting cost = explicit costs. Economic cost = explicit + implicit costs.**
- **Fixed cost (FC):** does not change with output; paid **even at zero output** (overhead, unavoidable): rent under a lease, insurance, a lawyer's retainer, permanent salaries. **Variable cost (VC):** changes with output; incurred **only when producing** (avoidable): raw materials, fuel, wages paid per hour.
- **Sunk cost:** already paid and unrecoverable.

| Formula | |
|---|---|
| **TC = FC + VC** | total cost |
| **AFC = FC ÷ Q** | average fixed cost |
| **AVC = VC ÷ Q** | average variable cost |
| **ATC (AC) = TC ÷ Q = AFC + AVC** | average total cost, cost per unit |
| **MC = ΔTC ÷ ΔQ** | marginal cost: the addition to TC from one more unit |

**Explain more**

Worked table: a pure-water factory, FC = ₦600.

| Q | FC | VC | TC | AFC | AVC | ATC | MC |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | 600 | 0 | 600 | – | – | – | – |
| 1 | 600 | 400 | 1,000 | 600 | 400 | 1,000 | 400 |
| 2 | 600 | 700 | 1,300 | 300 | 350 | 650 | 300 |
| 3 | 600 | 900 | 1,500 | 200 | 300 | 500 | **200** |
| 4 | 600 | 1,160 | 1,760 | 150 | **290** | 440 | 260 |
| 5 | 600 | 1,580 | 2,180 | 120 | 316 | **436** | 420 |
| 6 | 600 | 2,280 | 2,880 | 100 | 380 | 480 | 700 |

- MC reaches its minimum (Q = 3) **before** AVC (Q = 4), which is before ATC (Q = 5). At zero output TC = FC.
- **Curve shapes:** FC is a horizontal line. **AFC falls continuously** (a rectangular hyperbola) and is **never U-shaped**. AVC, ATC and MC are **U-shaped**. The gap between ATC and AVC is AFC, which narrows as output grows.
- **MC cuts AVC and ATC from below at their lowest points.** While MC < AC, AC is **falling**; when MC > AC, AC is **rising**; MC = AVC at the **minimum** of AVC. If AVC is rising, MC is above AVC.
- **Why the curves are U-shaped:** MC is the mirror of MP. Rising MP makes MC fall; when **MP falls (diminishing returns), MC rises**, so each extra unit needs more input and cost per unit eventually rises. The U-shaped short-run AC is due to the **law of diminishing returns (law of variable proportions)**. An upward-sloping MC is the sign of diminishing returns.
- **Computation drills:**
  - ATC ₦500 at 20 units and ₦520 at 21 units: TC = 10,000 and 10,920, so **MC of the 21st unit = ₦920**. Always convert averages to totals first.
  - At Q = 10, AFC = ₦40 and AVC = ₦300: TC = (40 + 300) × 10 = **₦3,400**.
  - FC = ₦900, ATC = ₦12, AVC = ₦10.50: AFC = 1.50, so Q = 900 ÷ 1.50 = **600 units**.
- Better technology shifts **MC (and AVC) down**; it does not touch AFC. **AFC is irrelevant** to the short-run output decision, which uses MC, MR and VC.

### Lesson 8.5: Long-run costs, revenue and profit

**Must know**

- **In the long run no cost is fixed**: all costs are variable.
- **LRAC (LAC):** the lowest cost per unit of each output when the firm can choose any plant size. It is the **envelope** of the short-run average cost (SAC) curves and a **planning curve**. It is U-shaped because of **economies then diseconomies of scale**.
- **Minimum efficient scale (MES):** the smallest output at which LRAC reaches its lowest point. A firm producing at the lowest unit cost possible is at its **optimum** scale.
- **Revenue:** the receipts a firm gets from selling its output at a given price.
  - **TR = P × Q**
  - **AR = TR ÷ Q = price** (the AR curve is the demand curve)
  - **MR = ΔTR ÷ ΔQ**, the addition to TR from selling one more unit.
- **Accounting profit = TR − explicit costs. Economic profit = TR − explicit − implicit costs** (TR − total economic cost).
- **Normal profit:** the minimum return that keeps the entrepreneur in the business; it is counted as a cost. When **economic profit = 0** (TR just covers all opportunity costs), the firm earns **normal profit**. Economic profit above zero is **abnormal (supernormal) profit**.
- **Retained earnings:** after-tax profit ploughed back into the business instead of paid as dividends. **Inventories:** goods held in stock for future sale.

**Explain more**

- **LRAC as envelope:** a small, medium and large plant each have their own U-shaped SAC. In the long run the firm picks, for each output, the plant with the lowest SAC; joining those lowest stretches (with many possible plant sizes) gives a smooth U-shaped LRAC that touches every SAC from below.
- **Revenue for a price-maker** (demand slopes down):

| Q | P = AR (₦) | TR (₦) | MR (₦) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 1,000 | 1,000 | 1,000 |
| 2 | 900 | 1,800 | 800 |
| 3 | 800 | 2,400 | 600 |
| 4 | 700 | 2,800 | 400 |
| 5 | 600 | 3,000 | 200 |
| 6 | 500 | 3,000 | **0** |
| 7 | 400 | 2,800 | −200 |

- **TR is at its maximum when MR = 0.** With a straight-line downward demand curve, MR is also a straight line with a **negative** slope, falling twice as fast as AR. For a **price-taker** (perfect competition), **P = AR = MR** (a horizontal line) and TR is a straight line from the origin.
- **Profit example:** a Yaba restaurant has TR ₦5,000,000, explicit costs ₦3,800,000, and implicit costs of ₦1,300,000 (owner's forgone salary ₦900,000 plus forgone rent on his own shop ₦400,000). Accounting profit = **₦1,200,000**; economic profit = **−₦100,000** (an economic loss). So a firm may earn an **accounting profit yet suffer an economic loss**, which happens when price is below the opportunity cost of the inputs.
- Profit is maximised where **MR = MC** (developed with market structures in Topic 10).

### Lesson 8.6: Factor pricing, wages and economic rent

**Must know**

- **Demand for factors is derived demand**: a factor is wanted for what it produces.
- **Wage:** the **price of labour**, determined by the **market demand for and supply of labour**. The **factor supply curve** shows the quantity of a factor supplied at alternative factor prices.
- **Marginal revenue product (MRP):** the addition to total revenue from employing one more unit of a factor: **MRP = ΔTR ÷ ΔL** (= MP × MR).
- **Value of marginal product (VMP):** **VMP = MP × P**. Under perfect competition in the product market, P = MR, so MRP = VMP.
- **Marginal productivity theory:** in equilibrium a firm pays each factor a price equal to its **marginal revenue product**. A profit-maximising firm employs a factor up to where **MRP = MRC** (marginal resource cost; in a competitive labour market, the wage). Hence the **MRP curve is the firm's demand curve for labour** (it slopes down because MP diminishes).
- **Transfer earnings:** the minimum payment needed to keep a factor in its present use, equal to what it could **earn in its next best employment**.
- **Economic rent:** any surplus a factor earns **above its transfer earnings** (above the minimum needed to bring it into that use). **Economic rent = actual earnings − transfer earnings.**

**Explain more**

Worked table: a Lagos printing shop whose price falls as it sells more.

| L | TP | MP | P (₦) | TR (₦) | VMP = MP × P | MRP = ΔTR |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 20 | 20 | 10 | 200 | 200 | 200 |
| 2 | 36 | 16 | 9 | 324 | **144** | **124** |
| 3 | 48 | 12 | 8 | 384 | 96 | 60 |

- For the 2nd worker, VMP = 16 × 9 = ₦144, but MRP = 324 − 200 = ₦124, lower because the price had to fall. At a wage of ₦124, the shop hires **2 workers** (MRP = wage).
- **Pay depends on** the demand for the product, the worker's **MPP** and the **price of the good**: all of these.
- **A wage change has two effects:** the **substitution effect** (a higher wage makes leisure dearer, so people work more) and the **income effect** (higher income lets people afford more leisure, so they may work less). When the income effect wins, the labour supply curve bends backward.
- **Economic rent example:** a footballer willing to work for ₦200 a week but paid ₦1,000 earns **₦800** economic rent; his transfer earnings are ₦200. **Trap:** economic rent is not the rent paid on land, and transfer earnings are not "transfer payments" (pensions, grants).
- When a factor's supply is perfectly inelastic (land), all its earnings are economic rent; when perfectly elastic, all are transfer earnings.

### One-minute recap

- Land → rent; labour → wages; capital → interest; entrepreneurship → profit. Money and wages paid are not factors.
- Human capital = knowledge and skill; buying equipment is physical capital.
- Short run: at least one input fixed; long run: all inputs variable, no fixed costs.
- MP = ΔTP/ΔL; AP = TP/L; MP = AP at max AP; MP = 0 at max TP; MP negative in Stage III.
- Diminishing returns (variable proportions) → rising MC → U-shaped short-run AC. Economies then diseconomies of scale → U-shaped LRAC.
- Returns to scale: output % more, equal, less than input % → increasing, constant, decreasing.
- TC = FC + VC; ATC = AFC + AVC; MC = ΔTC/ΔQ; MC cuts AVC and ATC from below at their minimums; AFC never U-shaped.
- Economic cost = explicit + implicit; economic profit zero = normal profit.
- AR = P; TR max where MR = 0; price-taker P = AR = MR.
- Hire labour until MRP = MRC (wage); MRP curve = demand for labour; economic rent = earnings − transfer earnings.

### Theory practice

**Question:** A firm has fixed costs of ₦1,200. Its total costs are ₦2,000 at 4 units and ₦2,500 at 5 units. Find (a) AFC, AVC and ATC at 5 units, and (b) the MC of the 5th unit. (c) Explain why MC eventually rises in the short run.

*Worked solution:* (a) AFC = 1,200 ÷ 5 = **₦240**; VC = 2,500 − 1,200 = 1,300, so AVC = **₦260**; ATC = 2,500 ÷ 5 = **₦500** (= 240 + 260). (b) MC = 2,500 − 2,000 = **₦500**. (c) With at least one input fixed, the law of diminishing returns makes the marginal product of the variable input eventually fall, so each extra unit of output needs more input and costs more.
