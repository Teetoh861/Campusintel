## Topic 4: Historical development of economic thought

**What you must know:** mercantilism and the physiocrats as background; the classical school (Adam Smith, Ricardo, Malthus) and its theories of income distribution; the neoclassical school (Marshall, Walras) and marginalism, including the paradox of value; utilitarianism (Bentham, J. S. Mill); welfare economics (Pigou, Pareto) and Pareto optimality.

### Lesson 4.1: Background: mercantilism and the physiocrats

**Must know**

- **Mercantilism** (about the 16th to 18th centuries): a nation's wealth is its stock of **gold and silver**. Aim: a **favourable balance of trade** (exports greater than imports). Tools: tariffs on imports, subsidies for exports, colonies, strong state control of trade. Names: **Thomas Mun** (England), **Jean-Baptiste Colbert** (France).
- **Physiocrats** (18th-century France): **land (agriculture) is the only source of wealth**; only farming yields a surplus, the **net product** (*produit net*). Industry and trade are "sterile". They favoured free trade (**laissez-faire, laissez-passer**) and a **single tax on land**. Names: **François Quesnay** (*Tableau économique*, 1758, which traced how income circulates among classes), **Turgot**.

**Explain more**

- The classical school grew up **as a response to mercantilism and feudal economic systems**. Smith attacked the mercantilist idea that gold is wealth: real wealth is the goods and services a nation produces.
- Trap: "laissez-faire" began with the physiocrats; Smith made it the centre of classical economics. "Land is the only productive factor" is physiocratic, never mercantilist.

### Lesson 4.2: Classical economics: Smith, Ricardo, Malthus

**Must know**

- **Classical economics:** late 18th and early 19th centuries. Free markets, self-regulation, competition, economies tending naturally to equilibrium, **limited government intervention**, focus on production and growth. Core idea: the **labour theory of value** (value is set by the labour needed to produce a good).

| Economist | Key work | Main ideas |
|---|---|---|
| **Adam Smith** (1723–1790), **"Father of Economics"** | ***An Inquiry into the Nature and Causes of the Wealth of Nations* (1776)**; also *The Theory of Moral Sentiments* (1759) | **Invisible hand**; **laissez-faire** (government limited to defence, justice, public works); **division of labour** (pin factory); productive vs unproductive labour; capital accumulation; canons of taxation; value in use vs value in exchange |
| **David Ricardo** (1772–1823) | ***On the Principles of Political Economy and Taxation* (1817)** | **Comparative advantage**; theory of **rent**; labour theory of value; wages tend to **subsistence** |
| **Thomas Malthus** (1766–1834) | ***An Essay on the Principle of Population* (1798)** | Population grows **geometrically** (1, 2, 4, 8...), food **arithmetically** (1, 2, 3, 4...), so population outstrips food, causing poverty and famine unless checked |

- **Invisible hand:** individuals pursuing their own self-interest unintentionally benefit society; the free market allocates resources efficiently.
- **Malthus's 2 checks:** (1) **preventive** = **moral restraint** (delayed marriage, fewer births); (2) **positive** (natural) = **famine, disease, war**.
- **Smith's 4 canons of taxation:** equality, certainty, convenience, economy.

**Explain more**

- **Division of labour** raises output through more skill and dexterity, less time lost switching tasks, and labour-saving machines (Smith's pin factory).
- **Comparative advantage worked example.** One day of labour produces:

| | Rice (bags) | Cloth (bolts) | Opportunity cost of 1 cloth |
|---|---|---|---|
| Nigeria | 10 | 5 | 10 ÷ 5 = 2 bags of rice |
| Ghana | 4 | 4 | 4 ÷ 4 = 1 bag of rice |

Nigeria has the **absolute** advantage in both goods, yet Ghana gives up less rice per bolt of cloth, so Ghana has the **comparative** advantage in cloth and Nigeria in rice (0.5 cloth per bag against Ghana's 1). Both gain by specialising and trading. Trap: comparative advantage compares **opportunity costs**, not output levels.
- **Malthus worked example.** If population doubles every 25 years while food rises by one fixed amount:

| Years | 0 | 25 | 50 | 75 | 100 | 200 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Population (geometric) | 1 | 2 | 4 | 8 | 16 | 256 |
| Food (arithmetic) | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 9 |

After two centuries the ratio is 256 to 9. Malthus warned against unchecked optimism about resources.

### Lesson 4.3: Classical theories of income distribution

**Must know**

The classical economists explained how output is shared among land (rent), labour (wages) and capital (profit or interest). The **early classical theories** are:

1. **Subsistence theory of wages** (the **iron law of wages**; Ricardo, Malthus): wages tend to the **minimum needed to keep workers alive**. Wages above subsistence make population grow, more workers push wages back down; wages below subsistence shrink the workforce and push wages up.
2. **Wages fund theory** (best known through **J. S. Mill**): employers set aside a **fixed fund of capital** for wages. **Average wage = wages fund ÷ number of workers.** Wages rise only if the fund grows or workers fall.
3. **Ricardian theory of rent:** rent is a payment for the **"original and indestructible powers of the soil"**, a **surplus** arising from differences in fertility. Marginal (worst) land earns **no rent**.
4. **Residual claimant theory** (**Francis A. Walker**): rent, interest and profit are fixed first; **workers receive what is left** (the residue), so wages rise with productivity.

- **Marginal productivity theory** is **not** classical. It came later from the **neoclassical** economists (notably **J. B. Clark**): each factor is paid according to what its **last unit adds to output**.

**Explain more**

- **Wages fund worked example.** A Lagos manufacturer's wages fund is ₦60,000,000 for 1,200 workers: average wage = 60,000,000 ÷ 1,200 = **₦50,000**. If workers rise to 1,500 with the same fund: 60,000,000 ÷ 1,500 = **₦40,000**. More workers mean a lower wage.
- **Ricardian rent worked example.** Three plots each cost the same to farm. Rent is each plot's output minus the output of the marginal plot:

| Plot | Output (bags) | Rent (bags) |
|---|---|---|
| A (most fertile) | 100 | 100 − 60 = 40 |
| B | 80 | 80 − 60 = 20 |
| C (marginal) | 60 | 0 |

As population grows, poorer land is farmed, and rent on the better plots rises. Landlords gain, widening inequality.
- Trap: "which is **not** an early classical theory of income distribution?" Answer: **marginal productivity theory**.

### Lesson 4.4: Neoclassical economics and the paradox of value

**Must know**

- **Neoclassical economics:** late 19th century; refined classical ideas with **marginal analysis** and **mathematics**. Focus: consumer behaviour, **utility**, and **equilibrium** of supply and demand. Value comes from **utility** (demand) as well as cost (supply), replacing the labour theory of value.
- **Marginalism:** decisions depend on the **marginal** (extra) benefit or cost of one more unit, not totals. **Marginal utility diminishes** as more is consumed.
- **Marginal revolution (1870s):** **W. S. Jevons** (*Theory of Political Economy*, 1871), **Carl Menger** (1871) and **Léon Walras** independently built value on marginal utility.

| Economist | Key work | Main ideas |
|---|---|---|
| **Alfred Marshall** (1842–1924) | ***Principles of Economics* (1890)** | Supply and demand curves; **price elasticity of demand**; **short run vs long run**; **partial equilibrium** (one market, ceteris paribus); consumer surplus; **welfare definition** ("a study of mankind in the ordinary business of life"); both demand (utility) and supply (cost) set price, like the **two blades of a pair of scissors** |
| **Léon Walras** (1834–1910) | ***Elements of Pure Economics* (1874)** | **General equilibrium**: all markets adjust **simultaneously** to equilibrium; mathematical models; competitive markets allocate resources efficiently under certain conditions |

- **Paradox of value** (the **diamond-water paradox**, raised by Smith): things **essential to life** (water, air) have little or no price, while **non-essentials** (diamonds) command a great price.
- **Resolution:** price reflects **marginal utility**, not total utility. Water and air are **abundant**, so the last unit adds little; diamonds are **scarce**, so the last unit is highly valued.

**Explain more**

- Smith described the paradox as **value in use** (water is high) against **value in exchange** (diamonds are high). The classical school could not settle it; marginalism did.

| Unit consumed | 1st | 2nd | ... | 1,000th |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Marginal utility of water | very high | high | ... | almost zero |

Total utility of water is huge, but its **marginal** utility (and so its price) is tiny; diamonds stay scarce, so their marginal utility stays high.
- Traps: "Principles of Economics (1890)" is **Marshall**, not Keynes, Samuelson or Pigou. Other "who wrote what" pairs: **Keynes**, *The General Theory of Employment, Interest and Money* (1936), father of modern **macroeconomics**; **Lionel Robbins**, *An Essay on the Nature and Significance of Economic Science* (1932), **scarcity** definition; **Paul Samuelson**, *Economics* (1948). Partial equilibrium = Marshall; general equilibrium = Walras.

### Lesson 4.5: Utilitarianism and welfare economics

**Must know**

- **Utilitarianism:** the best actions or policies are those that **maximise happiness (utility) for the greatest number**.
  - **Jeremy Bentham** (1748–1832), *An Introduction to the Principles of Morals and Legislation* (1789): behaviour is driven by seeking **pleasure** and avoiding **pain**; the **greatest happiness principle** ("the greatest good for the greatest number"); measured pleasure and pain by a **felicific (hedonic) calculus**. Little concern for distribution.
  - **John Stuart Mill** (1806–1873), *Principles of Political Economy* (1848), *On Liberty* (1859), *Utilitarianism*: extended Bentham to the **overall well-being of society**; quality of pleasures matters; supported **individual freedom**; argued for **reducing inequality of wealth** to raise social welfare.
  - Utilitarian thinking underlies **cost-benefit analysis**: a policy is justified if benefits exceed costs.
- **Welfare economics:** how resources should be allocated to improve the well-being of individuals and society; combines efficiency with ethics (fairness).
  - **Arthur C. Pigou** (1877–1959), Marshall's student, *The Economics of Welfare* (1920): **market failure** from **externalities** (pollution) and public goods; government should correct them with **taxes and subsidies**. **Pigovian tax** = a corrective tax equal to the external cost.
  - **Vilfredo Pareto** (1848–1923), Italian, *Manual of Political Economy* (1906): **Pareto efficiency (Pareto optimality)**.
- **Pareto optimality:** an allocation where it is **impossible to make someone better off without making someone else worse off**.
- **Pareto improvement:** a change that makes **at least one person better off and nobody worse off**. At the Pareto optimum no Pareto improvement is left.
- **Social welfare function:** combines individual utilities into one measure of society's well-being, taking account of distribution and equity.

**Explain more**

- **Pigovian tax worked example.** A cement plant in Ogun State has a private cost of ₦500 per bag, and its dust causes ₦150 of damage per bag to nearby residents. Social cost = 500 + 150 = **₦650**. A Pigovian tax of **₦150** per bag makes the firm pay the full social cost, so it cuts output to the socially efficient level.
- **Pareto worked example.** Utilities of Ada and Bayo under four allocations:

| Allocation | Ada | Bayo | Compared with A |
|---|---|---|---|
| A | 5 | 5 | starting point |
| B | 7 | 5 | Pareto improvement (Ada up, Bayo same) |
| C | 8 | 3 | **not** a Pareto improvement (Bayo worse off) |
| D | 7 | 6 | Pareto improvement over A and over B |

If no allocation beats D for one person without hurting the other, D is Pareto optimal.
- Traps: Pareto optimality is about **efficiency**, not **equity** (fairness); a very unequal allocation can be Pareto optimal. **Arrow's impossibility theorem** (Kenneth Arrow) is different: no voting rule can turn individual rankings into a social ranking that meets every fairness condition at once.

### One-minute recap

- Mercantilism: wealth = gold and silver, favourable trade balance. Physiocrats (Quesnay): land is the only source of wealth, net product, laissez-faire.
- Adam Smith, father of economics: *Wealth of Nations* (1776), invisible hand, division of labour, laissez-faire.
- Ricardo (1817): comparative advantage, theory of rent. Malthus (1798): population geometric, food arithmetic; preventive and positive checks.
- Early classical distribution theories: subsistence (iron law), wages fund (average wage = fund ÷ workers), Ricardian rent, residual claimant. Marginal productivity is neoclassical.
- Neoclassical: marginalism, utility, equilibrium. Marshall, *Principles of Economics* (1890), partial equilibrium, elasticity. Walras (1874), general equilibrium.
- Paradox of value: water or air cheap, diamonds dear; solved by marginal utility and scarcity.
- Utilitarianism: Bentham's greatest happiness for the greatest number; Mill adds liberty and fairer distribution.
- Pigou: externalities and corrective (Pigovian) taxes. Pareto optimality: no one better off without someone worse off.

### Theory practice

**Question:** A wages fund of ₦45,000,000 is shared among 900 workers. (a) Find the average wage. (b) Find the new average wage if the workforce rises to 1,000. (c) Name the theory and one other early classical theory of income distribution.

*Worked solution:* (a) 45,000,000 ÷ 900 = **₦50,000**. (b) 45,000,000 ÷ 1,000 = **₦45,000**; the wage falls by ₦5,000 because a fixed fund is shared among more workers. (c) The **wages fund theory** (J. S. Mill); others are the subsistence (iron law) theory, Ricardian rent theory and the residual claimant theory.
