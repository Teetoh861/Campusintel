## Topic 7: Consumer Behaviour: Utility and Indifference Curves

**What you must know:** utility, total and marginal utility, diminishing marginal utility and the saturation point; the cardinal (Marshallian) approach and its equilibrium conditions; the ordinal (Hicks and Allen) approach: indifference curves, their properties, MRS and special shapes; the budget line and consumer equilibrium, including U = XY problems; price-consumption, income-consumption and Engel curves; income and substitution effects; consumer surplus; revealed preference.

### Lesson 7.1: Utility, total and marginal utility

**Must know**

- **Theory of consumer behaviour:** explains how consumers allocate their limited income among goods to **maximise satisfaction (utility)**.
- **Utility:** the **want-satisfying power** of a commodity; the satisfaction or pleasure derived from consuming a good or service. It is **subjective**: it differs from person to person and cannot be measured in physical units.
- **Forms of utility (4):**
  1. **Form utility:** from the physical form or features of a good (wheat processed into flour, then bread).
  2. **Place utility:** from having the good where it is needed (a pharmacy near your hostel; water in a desert).
  3. **Time utility:** from having the good when needed (umbrellas in the rainy season).
  4. **Possession utility:** from owning or being able to use the good (owning a car).
- **Total utility (TU):** the total satisfaction from consuming a given quantity of a good. TU = f(Q).
- **Marginal utility (MU):** the **change in total utility** from consuming **one more unit**. MU = ΔTU/ΔQ, e.g. MU of the 2nd unit = TU₂ − TU₁.
- **Law of diminishing marginal utility (Gossen's first law):** as more and more units of a good are consumed in a given period, the extra satisfaction from each additional unit **falls**, other things being equal.
- **Saturation point:** where **MU = 0** and **TU is at its maximum**.
- **TU and MU rules:** MU positive → TU rising; MU = 0 → TU maximum; MU negative → TU falling. The **slope of the TU curve is MU**.

**Explain more**

Ada's sachets of water after a long walk:

| Sachets | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 | 7 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| TU (utils) | 10 | 18 | 24 | 28 | 30 | 30 | 28 |
| MU (utils) | 10 | 8 | 6 | 4 | 2 | 0 | −2 |

- MU falls with each sachet (diminishing MU). The 6th sachet adds nothing: MU = 0, TU = 30 is the maximum, so 6 is the **saturation point**. The 7th makes her worse off: MU negative, TU falls.
- **Shapes:** the TU curve rises at a decreasing rate, flattens at the peak, then turns down. The MU curve slopes downward and cuts the quantity axis at the saturation point.
- **Diminishing MU explains the downward-sloping demand curve:** each extra unit gives less satisfaction, so you buy more only at a lower price.
- **Real choices are about a little more or a little less**, so they are influenced by **marginal utilities**, not total utility. That is why MU, not TU, decides equilibrium.
- **A "bad":** a good of which **less is preferred to more** (smoke, refuse). An **economic good** is one of which more is preferred.
- **Trap:** switching goods does **not** mean MU has gone negative. Bayo stops after three Pepsis and switches to Coke because the **MU per naira of Coke is now higher** than that of Pepsi; Pepsi's MU may still be positive.

### Lesson 7.2: The cardinal (marginal utility) approach

**Must know**

- **Cardinalists (neo-classical school):** utility is **measurable** in cardinal numbers called **utils** (or in money). Associated with **Alfred Marshall**: **Marshallian utility analysis** is the cardinal approach to demand.
- **Assumptions of the cardinal approach:**
  1. **Rationality:** the consumer aims to maximise utility.
  2. **Cardinal utility (measurability):** utility can be measured in utils or money.
  3. **Constant marginal utility of money.**
  4. **Diminishing marginal utility.**
  5. **Additivity:** the total utility of a bundle is the sum of the separate utilities: U = U₁(X₁) + U₂(X₂) + ... The utilities of the goods are **independent** of one another.
  6. **Budget constraint:** a limited money income.
- **Consumer equilibrium:** the consumer cannot raise total utility by shifting spending from one good to another.
  - **One good:** **MUx = Px**. Buy until the satisfaction from the last unit equals its price.
  - **Free good (price zero):** consume until **MU = 0** (the saturation point).
  - **Two or more goods (law of equi-marginal utility, Gossen's second law):** **MUx/Px = MUy/Py = ... = MU of money (λ)**, **subject to** the budget **M = PxX + PyY**.
- Both conditions must hold together: the equal ratios **and** spending exactly the budget.

**Explain more**

**Worked example (finding a missing MU):** MUx = 100, Px = ₦10, Py = ₦5, consumer in equilibrium. MUx/Px = 100/10 = 10, so MUy/5 = 10 and **MUy = 50**.

**Worked example (table):** Chidi has ₦1,000 for meat pies (₦200 each) and soft drinks (₦100 each).

| Unit | MU pie | MU/P pie | MU drink | MU/P drink |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 1,600 | 8 | 900 | 9 |
| 2 | 1,200 | 6 | 700 | 7 |
| 3 | 1,000 | 5 | 600 | 6 |
| 4 | 700 | 3.5 | 500 | 5 |
| 5 | 400 | 2 | 300 | 3 |

The ratios are equal at two points: 2 pies with 3 drinks (6 = 6) and 3 pies with 4 drinks (5 = 5). Check the budget: 2(200) + 3(100) = ₦700, which leaves money unspent; 3(200) + 4(100) = **₦1,000**. Equilibrium: **3 pies and 4 drinks**, TU = 6,500 utils, the highest he can afford.

- **Why demand slopes down (cardinal proof):** if the price of pies falls, MUpie/Ppie rises above MUdrink/Pdrink. To restore equality MUpie must fall, and by diminishing MU that happens only if he buys **more pies**. Lower price, larger quantity: the law of demand.
- The marginal utility theory assumes a budget constraint, diminishing MU **and** utility maximisation: "all of the above".
- **Trap:** "increasing marginal utility" and "increasing marginal utility of money" are **not** cardinal assumptions; **rationality** is. For one good the condition is MUx = **Px**, never MUx = MUy.

### Lesson 7.3: The ordinal approach and indifference curves

**Must know**

- **Ordinalists (J.R. Hicks and R.G.D. Allen):** utility cannot be measured, only **ranked** (first, second, third). **Indifference curve analysis** is associated with **Hicks**. Its purpose: to derive a theory of consumer behaviour **without requiring cardinal measurement of utility**.
- The consumer's ordering is **weak ordering**: he can prefer A to B or be **indifferent** between them.
- **Objective factors:** **price and income** (seen in the market). **Subjective factors:** tastes and preferences.
- **Assumptions:** rationality; ordinal utility; more is preferred to less (non-satiety); consistency and transitivity (A over B and B over C means A over C); diminishing MRS.
- **Indifference curve (IC):** the locus of combinations of two goods that give the consumer the **same level of satisfaction**. Along an IC the **combination varies but utility stays constant**, so moving along it leaves satisfaction **unchanged**.
- **Indifference map:** a **set of indifference curves** in commodity space; the curves rank the consumer's preferences.
- **Marginal rate of substitution (MRSxy):** the amount of Y the consumer will give up for one more unit of X while keeping the same satisfaction. **MRSxy = −ΔY/ΔX = MUx/MUy.** It is the **slope of the IC** (ignoring the minus sign).
- **Diminishing MRS:** MRSxy **falls as you move down (eastward) along the IC**. This gives the curve its **convexity**.

**Properties of indifference curves:**

1. **Negative slope** (downward from left to right): less of one good must be made up by more of the other.
2. **Convex to the origin**, reflecting **diminishing MRS**. Convexity implies **substitution**: the goods substitute for each other, but less and less readily.
3. **Never intersect:** a crossing point would stand for two different satisfaction levels.
4. **Higher IC = higher satisfaction:** curves farther from the origin are **preferred**.
5. **Density:** between any two ICs another can always be drawn; the map is dense.
6. A standard IC does **not touch either axis** (the consumer buys both goods).

**Explain more**

| Bundle | A | B | C | D | E |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| X | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 |
| Y | 12 | 8 | 5 | 3 | 2 |
| MRSxy | | 4 | 3 | 2 | 1 |

All five bundles give equal satisfaction. Moving from A to E, the consumer gives up 4, then 3, 2, 1 units of Y per extra X: **diminishing MRS**, hence a curve bowed in towards the origin.

**Special shapes (X on the horizontal axis):**

| Case | Shape of IC | MRS | Utility function |
|---|---|---|---|
| Perfect substitutes | Straight line, negative slope | **Constant** | U = aX + bY |
| Perfect complements | **L-shaped** (right angle) | Not defined at the corner | U = min{aX, bY} |
| X is a neutral good | **Horizontal** | Zero | U depends on Y only |
| Y is a neutral good | **Vertical** | Infinite | U depends on X only |
| One good is a bad | Upward sloping | | |

- **Perfect complements in a fixed ratio:** Paul wants **two vanilla scoops for every chocolate scoop** (v = 2c). Write U(c, v) = **min{2c, v}**. Check: c = 1, v = 2 gives 2c = 2 and v = 2, balanced; an extra scoop of either alone adds nothing. Trick: the bigger number multiplies the good you need **fewer** of.
- **Ordinal meaning:** Ugo's U = X + Y and Idowu's V = 3X + 3Y rank every bundle in the **same order** (V is just 3 × U), so their **preferences are identical**. Multiplying a utility function by a positive number changes nothing ordinal; you cannot say Idowu is "happier".
- **Trap:** the slope of an IC is the **MRS**, not the **marginal rate of technical substitution (MRTS)**, which is the slope of an **isoquant** (a producer's equal-output curve). Isoquants share the same four properties: **negative slope, non-intersection, density and convexity**. If a question lists those four and "indifference curve" is not an option, choose **isoquant**.
- **Trap:** "it has no shape for some commodities" is **not** a property of an IC; nor is a positive slope, concavity, or crossing curves.

### Lesson 7.4: Budget line and consumer equilibrium

**Must know**

- **Budget line (budget constraint):** shows all combinations of two goods the consumer **can afford** with given money income and prices: **PxX + PyY = M**.
- **Intercepts:** X-axis at **M/Px**; Y-axis at **M/Py**. **Slope = −Px/Py** (the minus sign shows a trade-off).
- **Shifts:**
  1. **Income rises:** parallel shift **outward** (more of both). Income falls: parallel shift **inward**.
  2. **Price of X changes alone:** the line **pivots** on the Y-intercept; the X-intercept moves (out if Px falls, in if Px rises).
  3. **Price of Y changes alone:** the Y-intercept moves; the **X-intercept stays the same**.
- **Consumer equilibrium:** where the budget line is **tangent** to the **highest attainable IC**. A consumer who chooses this point is a **utility-maximising consumer**. The consumer maximises satisfaction **subject to the budget constraint**.
- **Condition:** **MRSxy = Px/Py**, which is the same as **MUx/Px = MUy/Py** (the cardinal condition reached by another route).
- If **MRSxy > Px/Py**, buy **more X and less Y**; if MRSxy < Px/Py, buy more Y and less X.

**Explain more**

**Worked U = XY problem:** U = XY, Px = ₦4, Py = ₦8, M = ₦240.

1. Marginal utilities: MUx = ∂U/∂X = **Y**; MUy = ∂U/∂Y = **X**.
2. Equilibrium: MUx/Px = MUy/Py → Y/4 = X/8 → **X = 2Y**.
3. Budget: 4X + 8Y = 240 → 4(2Y) + 8Y = 240 → 16Y = 240 → **Y = 15**.
4. Then **X = 2(15) = 30**. Check: 4(30) + 8(15) = 120 + 120 = ₦240.
5. **Lambda (λ)**, the marginal utility of money = MUx/Px = 15/4 = **3.75** (also MUy/Py = 30/8 = 3.75).
6. **MRS of X for Y** = MUx/MUy = Y/X = 15/30 = **1/2**, equal to Px/Py = 4/8.
7. Utility reached: U = 30 × 15 = 450.

- **Shortcut for U = XY:** the consumer spends **half of income on each good**: X = M/(2Px) = 240/8 = 30; Y = M/(2Py) = 240/16 = 15. Use it to check your Lagrange answer.
- **Budget line here:** X-intercept 240/4 = 60, Y-intercept 240/8 = 30, slope −4/8 = −1/2.
- **Off-equilibrium check:** at X = 20, Y = 20 (cost 80 + 160 = ₦240), MRS = Y/X = 1 > Px/Py = 1/2. The consumer values X more than the market charges, so he should buy **more X and less Y**, moving towards (30, 15).
- Points on the budget line below the tangency IC are affordable but give less satisfaction; a higher IC beyond the line is desirable but unaffordable.

### Lesson 7.5: PCC, ICC, Engel curve, income and substitution effects

**Must know**

- **Price-consumption curve (PCC):** joins the equilibrium points as the **price of one good changes**, with income and the other price **constant**. The **demand curve is derived from the PCC**.
- **Income-consumption curve (ICC):** joins the equilibrium points as **income changes**, prices constant.
- **Engel curve:** shows the **quantity of a good bought at different levels of income** (derived from the ICC). Upward sloping for a normal good; downward sloping (bending back) for an inferior good.
- **Price effect = substitution effect + income effect.** The total effect of a price change divides into these two.
- **Substitution effect:** change in quantity due **only to the change in relative prices**, with **real income held constant**. It is **always negative**: quantity moves **opposite** to price.
- **Income effect:** change in quantity due to the change in **real income** (purchasing power). When price falls, real income **rises**; for a normal good quantity then rises.

| Good | Substitution effect (price falls) | Income effect (price falls) | Total effect |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Normal** | Q rises | Q rises (**same direction**) | Q rises |
| **Inferior** | Q rises | Q falls, **smaller** | Q still rises |
| **Giffen** | Q rises | Q falls, **larger** | **Q falls** |

**Explain more**

- If the income effect works in the **same direction** as the substitution effect, the good is **normal**. If it works in the **opposite** direction, the good **cannot be normal**: it is inferior (and Giffen if the income effect dominates). A Giffen good has an **upward-sloping demand curve**, an exception to the law of demand.
- **Separating the effects (Hicks):** after a price fall, shift the new budget line inwards, parallel, until it just touches the **original IC**. The move along the original IC is the substitution effect; the move from there to the new equilibrium is the income effect.
- **Example:** garri falls from ₦1,500 to ₦1,000 per paint bucket. The substitution effect makes garri cheaper relative to rice, so you buy more. The income effect: you can now afford more rice, so a poor household may cut garri (inferior). If that cut outweighs the substitution effect, garri behaves as a Giffen good.
- **Trap:** the substitution effect is negative for **every** good, including inferior and Giffen goods; only the income effect changes sign.
- **Odd one out:** the budget line, PCC and ICC are all **lines drawn in indifference analysis**; "utility" is a concept, not a line, so it does not belong with them.

### Lesson 7.6: Consumer surplus and revealed preference

**Must know**

- **Consumer surplus (Marshall):** the difference between what a consumer is **willing to pay** for a commodity rather than go without it and the amount **actually paid**. It measures the difference between the value consumers place on their total consumption and what they pay.
- **Geometrically:** the area **below the demand curve and above the price line**.
- A utility-maximising household buys up to the point where the consumer surplus on the **last unit is zero** (the price just equals what that unit is worth to him).
- **Revealed preference theory (Paul Samuelson):** builds demand theory from consumers' **actual market behaviour**, not introspection. If a consumer buys bundle A when bundle B was also affordable, A is **revealed preferred** to B. It assumes **consistency** and uses **strong ordering** (the chosen bundle is strictly preferred; no indifference).

**Explain more**

- **Simple case:** Tunde would pay ₦600 for chicken and chips but pays ₦500. Consumer surplus = **₦100**.
- **Several units:** Amaka's willingness to pay for plates of jollof rice at ₦400 a plate:

| Plate | 1st | 2nd | 3rd | 4th |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Willing to pay (₦) | 1,000 | 800 | 600 | 400 |
| Surplus (₦) | 600 | 400 | 200 | 0 |

She buys 4 plates. Willing to pay ₦2,800, actually pays 4 × ₦400 = ₦1,600, so consumer surplus = **₦1,200**. The 4th plate gives zero surplus, so she stops there.

**Three approaches compared:**

| Approach | Key economist | Utility | Ordering | Based on |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Cardinal (marginal utility) | Alfred Marshall | Measurable (utils) | | Introspection |
| Ordinal (indifference curve) | J.R. Hicks (with Allen) | Ranked only | **Weak** | Introspection |
| Revealed preference | Paul Samuelson | Not needed | **Strong** | **Observed behaviour** |

- **Trap:** the approach **not** based on introspection is revealed preference. Marshall is never linked to indifference curves; Samuelson is never linked to utils.
- **Trap:** consumer surplus is not profit, discount or "benefit"; the keyword is willing to pay **minus** actually paid.

### One-minute recap

- Utility = want-satisfying power; subjective. MU = ΔTU/ΔQ; slope of TU = MU.
- Diminishing MU = Gossen's first law. Saturation: MU = 0, TU maximum; MU negative, TU falls.
- Cardinal (Marshall): measurable utils, additivity, constant MU of money, rationality. One good: MU = P; free good: MU = 0; two goods: MUx/Px = MUy/Py = λ plus budget.
- Ordinal (Hicks and Allen): weak ordering; IC = equal satisfaction; map = set of ICs.
- IC properties: negative slope, convex (diminishing MRS), no crossing, higher is better, dense.
- MRS = slope of IC = MUx/MUy. Perfect substitutes: straight line; perfect complements: L-shaped; horizontal IC: X neutral.
- Budget line PxX + PyY = M, slope −Px/Py. Equilibrium: tangency, MRS = Px/Py. U = XY, ₦4, ₦8, ₦240 gives X = 30, Y = 15, λ = 3.75, MRS = 1/2.
- PCC → demand curve; ICC → Engel curve.
- Substitution effect always negative; normal: effects reinforce; Giffen: income effect outweighs.
- Consumer surplus = willing to pay − paid; revealed preference (Samuelson) uses observed behaviour and strong ordering.

### Theory practice

**Question:** A consumer has utility U = XY, income ₦600, Px = ₦10 and Py = ₦20. Find the equilibrium quantities, λ and the MRS at equilibrium.

*Worked solution:* MUx = Y, MUy = X. Y/10 = X/20 gives X = 2Y. Budget: 10(2Y) + 20Y = 600, so 40Y = 600, **Y = 15** and **X = 30**. Check: 300 + 300 = ₦600 (half the income on each good). λ = MUx/Px = 15/10 = **1.5** (= 30/20). MRSxy = Y/X = 15/30 = **1/2** = Px/Py.
