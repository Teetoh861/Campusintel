## Topic 10: Market Structures, Efficiency and Market Failure

**What you must know:** the meaning of market structure and the four chief types; perfect competition (assumptions, price taker, P = AR = MR, short-run and long-run equilibrium, shutdown point); monopoly (barriers, MR below AR, MR = MC, price discrimination, arbitrage); monopolistic competition and excess capacity; oligopoly, kinked demand, cartels and duopoly; monopsony and bilateral monopoly; allocative and productive efficiency, deadweight loss; market failure (externalities, public goods, merit and demerit goods, missing markets, information problems) and why government intervenes.

### Lesson 10.1: Market structure and the four chief types

**Must know**

- **Market structure:** the **composition of buyers and sellers** in a market (their number and size), plus how alike the products are and how easy entry is. It decides how much power each firm has over price. Behaviour (conduct) is shaped by structure; it is not the structure itself.
- **The chief types of market situation (4):** perfect competition, monopoly, monopolistic competition, oligopoly. **Duopoly** (two firms) is a special case of oligopoly.

| Feature | Perfect competition | Monopolistic competition | Oligopoly | Monopoly |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Number of firms | Very many, small | Many | Few, large (duopoly = 2) | One |
| Product | Homogeneous (identical) | Differentiated (similar, not identical) | Identical or differentiated | Unique, no close substitutes |
| Entry and exit | Free | Free (easy) | Substantial barriers | Blocked |
| Control over price | None: **price taker** | Some | Considerable; **interdependent** | Great: **price maker** |
| Firm's demand curve | Horizontal, perfectly elastic | Downward sloping, highly elastic | Downward sloping (kinked) | Downward sloping = industry demand |
| Long-run profit | Normal | Normal | Abnormal possible | Abnormal |
| Nigerian example | Farm produce (tomatoes, garri) | Restaurants, hair salons, bakeries | Telecoms (MTN, Airtel, Glo, 9mobile), cement, banks | Electricity transmission (TCN) |

- **Profit maximisation rule (all structures):** produce where **MC = MR**. Expand while **MR > MC**; cut output if **MC > MR**.

**Explain more**

- **Slope of total revenue = MR; slope of total cost = MC.** If the TR curve is flatter than the TC curve, MR < MC: extra output adds more to cost than to revenue, so do **not** increase output.
- The main aim of a firm (maximum profit) is achieved where **MC = MR and AR ≥ AC**. MC = MR alone is not enough: at that output price must also cover average cost.
- **Loss:** a firm makes a loss in the short run if **price (AR) is less than average cost (AC)**, because TR = P × Q is then below TC = AC × Q.
- Trap: "MC = MR is true only in perfect competition" is false; it holds in **all types of market**.

### Lesson 10.2: Perfect competition

**Must know**

- **Perfect competition:** a large number of relatively small firms, free entry and exit, a homogeneous product, and full knowledge by all buyers and sellers.
- **Assumptions (features):**
  1. Large number of buyers and sellers, so none can influence price.
  2. Homogeneous product.
  3. Perfect mobility of resources (free entry and exit).
  4. Perfect knowledge of market conditions.
- Every firm is a **price taker**. It can sell **any amount at the market price and none at all above it** (true).
- So the **firm's demand curve is horizontal, perfectly elastic**, and **P = AR = MR** (D = AR = MR is one line). The **industry** demand curve still slopes downward.
- **Profit maximum:** **MC = MR = P**, with MC rising.
- **Short run:** abnormal (supernormal) profit if P > AC; normal profit if P = AC; loss if P < AC.
- **Long run:** abnormal profit attracts new firms; supply rises and price falls until **P = minimum LAC**. Equilibrium: **MR = MC = AR = AC**. Firms earn only **normal profit** (zero economic profit, TR = TC).
- **Normal profit:** the minimum profit needed to keep the entrepreneur in the industry; it is counted as part of cost.
- **Short-run shutdown point:** the output where **price just equals minimum average variable cost (AVC)**. Below it the firm closes. The firm's short-run supply curve is its **MC curve above minimum AVC**.
- **Long-run shutdown point:** minimum point of LAC. If P < LAC at the MC = MR output in the long run, the firm **leaves the industry**.

**Explain more**

*Worked example: profit maximisation.* Market price ₦40, total fixed cost ₦40.

| Q | TC (₦) | MC (₦) | TR (₦) | Profit (₦) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 70 | 30 | 40 | -30 |
| 2 | 90 | 20 | 80 | -10 |
| 3 | 105 | 15 | 120 | 15 |
| 4 | 125 | 20 | 160 | 35 |
| 5 | 155 | 30 | 200 | 45 |
| 6 | 195 | 40 | 240 | 45 |

MR = P = ₦40 for every unit. MC = MR at Q = 6 (MC rising), giving maximum profit ₦45 (the 6th unit just pays for itself, so profit equals that at Q = 5). MC = MR = P = ₦40.

*Worked example: shutdown.* At its best output of 100 units a firm has AC = ₦60 and AVC = ₦45 (its minimum AVC), so AFC = ₦15 and TFC = ₦1,500.
- Price ₦50: loss = (50 - 60) × 100 = ₦1,000. Closing would lose all of TFC, ₦1,500. Price covers AVC, so it **keeps producing** at a loss.
- Price ₦40: producing loses (40 - 60) × 100 = ₦2,000, more than ₦1,500. Price is below AVC, so it **shuts down**.

- **Break-even point** = minimum AC (normal profit). **Shutdown point** = minimum AVC. Exams swap them.
- A competitive firm in long-run equilibrium that **lowers its price** goes below minimum AC and **operates at a loss**; it gains no extra sales because it could already sell all it wanted.
- **Better technology** cuts costs, shifts industry supply right and **lowers price**.
- If LRAC keeps sloping **downward** over the whole relevant output, bigger firms are always cheaper, so a few firms or one firm take over. The assumption of **perfect competition** then breaks down (this is how a natural monopoly arises).
- **Advantages:** lowest price, efficient use of resources, little advertising, large consumer surplus. **Disadvantages:** little profit, weak incentive to invent, social costs ignored.

### Lesson 10.3: Monopoly, price discrimination and arbitrage

**Must know**

- **Monopoly:** a market with **one, and only one, seller** of a product with **no close substitutes**, protected from entry. The firm is the industry.
- **Features:** one firm (its downward-sloping demand curve **is the industry demand curve**); barriers to entry; no supply curve independent of demand.
- **Sources of monopoly power (barriers to entry):**
  1. Control of the entire supply of a basic input (raw material).
  2. Economies of scale (natural monopoly).
  3. Franchises and patents (legal protection).
  4. State monopoly (public utilities: electricity, water, railways).
  5. Merger and acquisition.
  6. Location factor (sole firm in a small community).
- **AR curve slopes downward.** To sell one more unit the monopolist must cut price on all units, so **MR < AR (price)** and the MR curve slopes downward below AR. This is true of oligopoly too.
- **Equilibrium:** **MR = MC < P**. Long run: **abnormal profit** persists because entry is blocked.
- The monopolist can fix **price or output, but not both**. This is what "the monopolist is subject to the authority of the consumer" means.
- It **never produces on the inelastic part** of the demand curve, because there MR is negative and cannot equal a positive MC.
- **Price discrimination:** charging **different prices to different customers** for the same product, when the difference is not due to differences in cost.
- **Conditions for price discrimination:**
  1. **Elasticity of demand must differ** between the markets (higher price where demand is less elastic).
  2. The firm must be able to **separate** the buyers, at a cost that does not wipe out the gain.
  3. **No resale** from the cheap market to the dear market.
- **Arbitrage:** buying at a low price in one place and selling at a higher price in another. Price discrimination fails if arbitrage is possible.

**Explain more**

*Worked example: monopoly schedule.* Demand P = 100 - 10Q (₦), TFC = ₦50, MC = ₦30.

| Q | P = AR (₦) | TR (₦) | MR (₦) | TC (₦) | Profit (₦) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 90 | 90 | 90 | 80 | 10 |
| 2 | 80 | 160 | 70 | 110 | 50 |
| 3 | 70 | 210 | 50 | 140 | 70 |
| 4 | 60 | 240 | 30 | 170 | 70 |
| 5 | 50 | 250 | 10 | 200 | 50 |
| 6 | 40 | 240 | -10 | 230 | 10 |

MR falls twice as fast as price and sits below it. MR = MC = ₦30 at Q = 4, so price ₦60 and profit ₦70. P (₦60) > MC (₦30). TR peaks at Q = 5 (unit elasticity); beyond that MR is negative and demand is inelastic, which the monopolist avoids.

*Worked example: price discrimination.* MC = ₦20. Market 1 (adults): Q1 = 100 - P1, so MR1 = 100 - 2Q1. Market 2 (students): Q2 = 120 - 2P2, so MR2 = 60 - Q2.
- Set MR = MC in each market: 100 - 2Q1 = 20 gives Q1 = 40, P1 = ₦60; 60 - Q2 = 20 gives Q2 = 40, P2 = ₦40.
- Elasticity at these points: market 1 = 1 × 60/40 = 1.5; market 2 = 2 × 40/40 = 2. The **less elastic** market pays the **higher** price.
- Profit = (60 - 20) × 40 + (40 - 20) × 40 = ₦2,400, against about ₦2,133 if one price (₦46.67) were charged in both.
- Examples: lower cinema or bus fares for students; doctors charging rich and poor patients differently.
- **Monopoly, for:** no over-production, large-scale low cost, funds for research. **Against:** higher prices, restricted choice, artificial scarcity, no pressure to be efficient.
- Traps: a monopsonist (only buyer) is not a monopolist (only seller). "Different prices" alone is incomplete; it is different prices **to different customers**.

### Lesson 10.4: Monopolistic competition, oligopoly, duopoly, monopsony and bilateral monopoly

**Must know**

- **Monopolistic competition:** many firms selling close but not perfect substitutes. Features: many small firms; **product differentiation** (brand, packaging, quality, location); free entry and exit; downward-sloping, highly elastic demand with MR below it.
- **Short run:** MR = MC; it **may** make profit, break even or make a loss, and **may close down** if price is below AVC. None of these is "always".
- **Long run:** entry removes abnormal profit, so P = LAC (**normal profit**), with the demand curve tangent to LAC on its falling part. Output is below the minimum-cost output: **excess capacity**.
- **Oligopoly:** a **few** firms, a **highly concentrated** market, products identical (pure oligopoly) or differentiated. Its **unique** feature is **mutual interdependence**: each firm must weigh rivals' reactions.
- **Other features:** barriers to entry; heavy advertising; firms of unequal size; no unique pricing pattern; abnormal profit possible in the long run.
- **Kinked demand curve:** rivals **match price cuts** but **ignore price rises**. Above the ruling price demand is elastic; below it, inelastic. The kink creates a **gap (discontinuity) in MR**, so MC can move within the gap without changing price: oligopoly **prices are sticky (stable)**.
- **Collusion:** firms agree, openly or secretly, to fix prices or share markets instead of competing. A **cartel** is a formal collusive group that acts like one monopolist (OPEC for oil).
- **Duopoly:** only **two firms** supply the whole market; each one's price-output policy provokes the other's reaction.
- **Monopsony:** a **single buyer** facing many sellers (in labour, one employer and many workers). It faces the **upward-sloping market supply curve** and is a **price maker** (wage setter).
- **Bilateral monopoly:** a **single seller facing a single buyer** (one trade union bargaining with one employer). Price is settled by bargaining.

**Explain more**

- **Excess capacity example:** a Yaba restaurant could serve 200 plates a day at lowest AC, but in the long run sells 150, where its demand curve just touches AC. The unused 50 is excess capacity; profit is normal, not excess.
- **Kinked demand, in words:** two straight pieces meeting at the current price, flatter above, steeper below, with MR dropping vertically at the kink.
- Price wars are oligopoly behaviour, so firms prefer non-price competition (advertising) or collusion. Cartels tend to break down because each member gains by secretly cheating.
- Nigerian telecoms are an **oligopoly**, not a duopoly: a few big networks, each watching the others' data and call tariffs.
- Traps: monopsony = one **buyer**; monopoly = one **seller**; bilateral monopoly = **one of each**. A monopsonist faces a positively sloped **supply** curve, not a demand curve.

### Lesson 10.5: Efficiency and welfare

**Must know**

- **Allocative efficiency:** the optimal distribution of goods and services, taking consumers' preferences into account: the mix society values most. Condition: **P = MC**.
- **Productive efficiency:** producing at the **lowest possible cost**, at **minimum AC**.
- **Pareto efficiency:** no one can be made better off without making someone else worse off.
- **Perfect competition** in the long run achieves **both** (P = MC = minimum AC). It is the most efficient structure.
- **Monopoly misallocates resources** because **price exceeds marginal cost**: output is restricted and too few resources go into the good.
- **Deadweight loss:** the loss of total surplus (consumer plus producer surplus) that nobody gains, caused by producing less than the efficient output.

**Explain more**

*Worked example: deadweight loss.* Market demand P = 100 - 2Q (₦), constant MC = ₦20.
- **Competition:** P = MC, so 100 - 2Q = 20, Q = 40, P = ₦20. Consumer surplus = ½ × (100 - 20) × 40 = ₦1,600.
- **Monopoly:** MR = 100 - 4Q = 20 gives Q = 20, P = ₦60. Profit = (60 - 20) × 20 = ₦800. Consumer surplus = ½ × (100 - 60) × 20 = ₦400.
- **Deadweight loss** = ½ × (60 - 20) × (40 - 20) = ₦400. Check: 1,600 = 400 + 800 + 400.
- On a graph the deadweight loss is the triangle between the demand curve and MC, from monopoly output to competitive output.
- Monopolistic competition and oligopoly also have P > MC and do not produce at minimum AC, so they are neither allocatively nor productively efficient.
- Trap: **full utilisation** (all resources employed) can still produce the wrong mix; it is not allocative efficiency.

### Lesson 10.6: Market failure and government intervention

**Must know**

- **Market failure:** when one or more distortions stop the market system from allocating resources efficiently.
- **Causes:**
  1. **Externalities:** an action of an individual imposes a direct cost or benefit on a third party not involved. **Negative** = cost (pollution, noise). **Positive** = benefit (vaccination, education, a neighbour's flower garden).
  2. **Public goods:** non-rival and non-excludable (national defence, street lighting). The **free-rider problem** means markets under-supply them.
  3. **Merit goods:** goods whose **benefits people underestimate**, so they are under-consumed (education, healthcare).
  4. **Demerit goods:** harmful goods whose costs are underestimated, so they are over-consumed and carry **negative externalities** (alcohol, cigarettes, hard drugs).
  5. **Missing markets:** some products have no market at all.
  6. **Information problems:** **asymmetric information**, one side knows more than the other.
  7. **Monopoly power** (P > MC).
  8. **Property right failure:** resources nobody owns are overused.
- **Private goods** (rival and excludable) are **not** a cause of market failure.
- **Reasons government intervenes:**
  1. To overcome market failure.
  2. To redistribute income and wealth for equality of opportunity and outcome.
  3. To overcome prolonged recessions and reduce unemployment.
  4. To provide public goods and regulate monopolies.

**Explain more**

- **Social cost = private cost + external cost.** Example: a cement plant's private cost is ₦500 a bag and its dust imposes ₦100 a bag on nearby residents, so social cost is ₦600. The market ignores the ₦100 and over-produces. A **Pigovian tax** of ₦100 a bag makes the firm pay the full social cost. A positive externality is under-produced and can be corrected by a **subsidy**.
- Other tools: providing public goods directly, subsidising merit goods (free primary education), taxing or banning demerit goods, regulating monopolies.
- **Missing market example:** there is no market where you can buy cleaner air in Lagos, so no price signals its value.
- **Asymmetric information example:** a used-car seller at Berger knows the car's faults; the buyer does not.
- **Property rights example:** a shared community fishing pond is overfished because no one owns it.
- Traps: when the stem says "cost **or** benefit", the answer is **externality** (general); "benefit to a third party" alone is **positive externality**.

### One-minute recap

- Market structure = composition of buyers and sellers; four chief types: perfect competition, monopoly, monopolistic competition, oligopoly (duopoly is two firms).
- Profit maximum everywhere: MC = MR (with AR ≥ AC). Expand if MR > MC; loss if P < AC.
- Perfect competition: price taker, perfectly elastic demand, P = AR = MR; short-run abnormal profit, long-run normal profit with MR = MC = AR = AC; shutdown at P = minimum AVC.
- Monopoly: one seller, barriers, MR < AR, MR = MC < P, long-run abnormal profit, never on inelastic demand, fixes price or output but not both.
- Price discrimination needs different elasticities, separable markets and no resale (no arbitrage).
- Monopolistic competition: product differentiation, long-run normal profit and excess capacity.
- Oligopoly: few interdependent firms, kinked demand, sticky prices, collusion and cartels.
- Monopsony = single buyer (price maker, upward supply curve); bilateral monopoly = single buyer and single seller.
- Allocative efficiency P = MC; productive efficiency at minimum AC; monopoly causes deadweight loss.
- Market failure: externalities, public goods, merit and demerit goods, missing markets, information problems, property right failure; government intervenes to correct these, redistribute income and fight recession.

### Theory practice

**Question:** A monopolist faces demand P = 120 - 2Q (₦) and constant MC = ₦40. Find its profit-maximising output and price, the competitive output and price, and the deadweight loss.

*Worked solution:* TR = 120Q - 2Q², so MR = 120 - 4Q. Set MR = MC: 120 - 4Q = 40, Q = 20, P = 120 - 40 = ₦80. Competition sets P = MC: 120 - 2Q = 40, Q = 40, P = ₦40. Deadweight loss = ½ × (80 - 40) × (40 - 20) = ₦400. Price exceeds MC (₦80 > ₦40), so the monopoly misallocates resources.
