## Topic 3: Areas of Specialisation in Economics

**What you must know:** the major areas of specialisation in economics; what development, international, energy and environmental, and institutional economics each study, their key areas of focus and their named theories; welfare economics as the branch on optimal allocation; behavioural economics and its biases.

### Lesson 3.1: What an area of specialisation is

**Must know**

- **Area of specialisation (branch):** a part of economics that applies the general tools (scarcity, choice, demand and supply, costs) to one set of problems.
- **The 4 major areas of specialisation:**

1. **Development economics:** growth and living standards in developing countries (e.g. poverty reduction).
2. **International economics:** trade, exchange rates, globalisation (e.g. tariffs).
3. **Energy and environmental economics:** resource management and sustainability (e.g. carbon taxes).
4. **Institutional economics:** the role of institutions (laws, norms) in economic behaviour.

- Also know **welfare economics** and **behavioural economics** (Lesson 3.6).

**Explain more**

- Trap: match the example to the branch. Poverty reduction → development. Tariffs and exchange rates → international. Carbon taxes and pollution → environmental. Property rights, corruption, transaction costs → institutional.

### Lesson 3.2: Development economics

**Must know**

- **Development economics:** the branch that focuses on improving the economic well-being and quality of life of people in **developing (low-income) countries**: economic growth, poverty reduction, income inequality, and the policies needed to raise living standards.
- **5 key areas of focus:**

1. **Economic growth:** how economies grow through capital accumulation, labour force expansion and technological progress (and institutional quality). Growth is **necessary but not sufficient**: it does not by itself remove inequality.
2. **Poverty and inequality:** reducing poverty and disparities within and between countries, through income redistribution, education and health.
3. **Human development:** beyond GDP; access to education, healthcare and basic living standards; quality of life over material wealth alone.
4. **Sustainable development:** balancing growth with conserving resources and reducing environmental degradation.
5. **Policy interventions:** government policy, foreign aid and international organisations; microfinance, infrastructure investment, social safety nets, trade policy, industrialisation strategies.

- **Key theories:**
  - **Modernisation theory:** countries develop in a **linear** process, from traditional to modern stages.
  - **Dependency theory:** developing countries are kept underdeveloped by their historical **dependence on (exploitation by) wealthy nations**.
  - **Neoliberalism:** free markets, deregulation and privatisation are the path to development.
  - **Endogenous growth theory:** **human capital and innovation** drive long-term growth.

**Explain more**

- If Nigeria's GDP rises but the gain goes to a few and schools and clinics do not improve, there is growth without development.
- Key challenges: poverty, inequality, corruption and weak governance.
- Trap: "linear path from traditional to modern" is modernisation, not dependency. "Rich countries keep poor ones dependent" is dependency. "Privatise and deregulate" is neoliberalism.

### Lesson 3.3: International economics

**Must know**

- **International economics:** the economic interactions between countries: trade, investment and monetary relations; how countries exchange goods, services, capital and labour, and the effect on their economic performance.
- **4 key areas of focus:**

1. **International trade:** goods and services across borders; trade theories (comparative advantage), trade policies (**tariffs** and **quotas**), trade agreements (**WTO**, World Trade Organization; **NAFTA**, North American Free Trade Agreement).
2. **International finance:** capital flows, **exchange rates**, foreign investment, global financial markets; currency stabilisation, capital controls, financial crises.
3. **Globalisation:** the growing interconnectedness of the world economy; its causes and effects on trade, labour markets and inequality.
4. **Balance of payments:** a record of **all** financial transactions between a country and the rest of the world (trade balances, capital flows, foreign aid).

- **Key theories:**
  - **Ricardian theory of comparative advantage (David Ricardo):** countries should specialise in goods where they have the **lower opportunity cost**; both gain from trade.
  - **Heckscher-Ohlin theory:** a country's relative **factor endowments** (land, labour, capital) determine its comparative advantage. A labour-abundant country exports labour-intensive goods.
  - **Monetary theories:** the role of exchange rates, interest rates and monetary policy in the global economy.

**Explain more**

**Worked example: comparative advantage.** With the same resources, Nigeria can produce 30 units of crude oil or 15 of cocoa; Ghana 5 of crude oil or 10 of cocoa.

| Country | Crude oil | Cocoa | Opportunity cost of 1 oil | Opportunity cost of 1 cocoa |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Nigeria | 30 | 15 | 15 ÷ 30 = **½ cocoa** | 30 ÷ 15 = 2 oil |
| Ghana | 5 | 10 | 10 ÷ 5 = 2 cocoa | 5 ÷ 10 = **½ oil** |

- Nigeria has the **absolute** advantage in both goods (30 > 5 and 15 > 10).
- But Nigeria gives up less cocoa per unit of oil (½ < 2), so Nigeria has the **comparative** advantage in **crude oil**. Ghana gives up less oil per unit of cocoa (½ < 2), so Ghana specialises in **cocoa**.
- Method: opportunity cost of good X = units of Y given up ÷ units of X gained. The country with the **lower** opportunity cost specialises in that good.

**Why light electronics moved from the West to China:** the cost of labour is cheaper and the technical competencies are comparable with the West. Firms produce where cost is lowest for a given quality; China is labour-abundant (Heckscher-Ohlin). Traps: "China is the greatest economy" (size alone does not lower a firm's costs), "the Chinese are good people" and "the West is no longer interested in production" are not economic reasons.


### Lesson 3.4: Energy and environmental economics

**Must know**

- **Energy and environmental economics:** the relationship between economic activity and the environment, especially energy use, climate change and sustainable resource management. It seeks economic solutions to environmental problems while promoting energy security and sustainability.
- **4 key areas of focus:**

1. **Energy economics:** production, consumption and distribution of energy: fossil fuels (oil, coal, natural gas) and renewables (solar, wind, hydro); pricing, regulation, policy.
2. **Climate change and environmental policy:** **carbon pricing**, **cap-and-trade**, subsidies for clean energy.
3. **Resource management:** allocating scarce natural resources; **renewable** (fisheries, forests) and **non-renewable** (minerals, fossil fuels).
4. **Externalities:** unintended side effects such as pollution, corrected by market-based tools (taxes, subsidies, emissions trading) that **internalise** the cost.

- **Key theories:**
  - **Environmental Kuznets curve:** as an economy grows, environmental degradation **rises up to a point, then falls** as the economy matures and can afford cleaner technology. The curve is an **inverted U**.
  - **Coase theorem (Ronald Coase):** private negotiation can reach an efficient outcome when **property rights are well defined** and **transaction costs are low**.

**Explain more**

- Externality example: a cement factory near a Lagos community pays for limestone and labour but not for the dust its neighbours breathe. If the dust costs residents ₦2 million a year in health bills, a ₦2 million tax on the factory makes it bear the full cost. That is internalising the externality.
- Kuznets curve in words: on a graph with income per head on the horizontal axis and pollution on the vertical axis, pollution first slopes upward, peaks, then slopes downward.

### Lesson 3.5: Institutional economics

**Must know**

- **Institutions:** **formal rules** (laws, regulations) and **informal rules** (norms, culture).
- **Institutional economics:** the role of institutions in shaping economic behaviour and outcomes, how institutions evolve, and their effect on economic performance.
- **4 key areas of focus:**

1. **Property rights:** clear, protected property rights lead to better resource allocation, investment and growth.
2. **Market functioning:** how institutions shape markets, government regulation, and **market failures** (monopolies, information asymmetries).
3. **Governance and corruption:** political systems and legal frameworks; corruption, inefficient courts and weak institutions hinder development and efficiency.
4. **Transaction costs:** the costs of making an exchange: **searching for information, negotiating contracts, enforcing agreements**. High transaction costs block efficient markets and growth.

- **Key theories:**
  - **New Institutional Economics (NIE):** how institutions shape outcomes through transaction costs, information, and legal and political frameworks.
  - **Theories of institutional change:** how institutions evolve through **historical, path-dependent** processes, helping or obstructing development.

**Explain more**

- Example: a farmer without a registered title to her land cannot use it as collateral for a bank loan, so she invests less. Clear property rights fix this.
- Transaction cost example: a trader pays ₦5,000 in transport to compare prices across markets and ₦20,000 to a lawyer to draft a supply contract. Both are transaction costs, separate from the price of the goods.

### Lesson 3.6: Welfare economics and behavioural economics

**Must know**

- **Welfare economics:** the branch of economics devoted to determining the **optimal allocation of resources in society**; how resources can be allocated to make society as well off as possible, combining efficiency with fairness.
  - **Pareto efficiency (Vilfredo Pareto):** no one can be made better off without making someone else worse off.
  - **Pigou (Arthur Pigou):** markets fail through externalities and public goods; **Pigovian taxes** on harmful goods correct them.
  - **Social welfare function:** adds individual utilities into one measure of society's well-being.
- **Behavioural economics:** integrates **psychology** with economic decision-making, challenging the assumption that people are always **rational**.
- **4 biases:**

1. **Loss aversion:** people fear losses more than they value equal gains.
2. **Endowment effect:** people overvalue what they own.
3. **Present bias:** preferring immediate rewards over long-term benefits.
4. **Herd behaviour:** following the crowd, leading to asset bubbles.

- **Nudging:** designing choices to guide people toward better decisions (e.g. automatic enrolment in retirement savings).

**Explain more**

- Trap: "optimal allocation of resources in society" is **welfare economics**, not microeconomics (the broader study of households and firms), not positive economics (describes what is, not what is best) and not behavioural economics.
- Loss aversion example: losing ₦10,000 hurts more than finding ₦10,000 pleases.

### One-minute recap

- 4 major areas: development, international, energy and environmental, institutional.
- Development: growth, poverty and inequality, human development, sustainable development, policy. Growth is necessary, not sufficient.
- Modernisation (linear), dependency (kept dependent by rich nations), neoliberalism (free markets), endogenous growth (human capital, innovation).
- International: trade, finance, globalisation, balance of payments; Ricardo's comparative advantage (lower opportunity cost), Heckscher-Ohlin (factor endowments).
- Electronics moved to China: cheaper labour with comparable technical skills.
- Environmental: externalities internalised by taxes, subsidies, emissions trading; Kuznets curve is an inverted U; Coase theorem needs clear property rights and low transaction costs.
- Institutional: formal and informal rules; property rights, market functioning, governance, transaction costs; NIE.
- Welfare economics = optimal allocation of resources in society (Pareto, Pigou).
- Behavioural: loss aversion, endowment effect, present bias, herd behaviour, nudging.

### Theory practice

**Question:** Country A can produce 40 bags of rice or 20 cartons of shoes; Country B can produce 10 bags of rice or 10 cartons of shoes. Which good should each country specialise in?

*Worked solution:* Opportunity cost of 1 rice: A = 20 ÷ 40 = 0.5 shoes; B = 10 ÷ 10 = 1 shoe. Opportunity cost of 1 shoe: A = 40 ÷ 20 = 2 rice; B = 10 ÷ 10 = 1 rice. A has the lower cost in rice (0.5 < 1), so **A specialises in rice**. B has the lower cost in shoes (1 < 2), so **B specialises in shoes**, even though A has the absolute advantage in both.
