## Topic 5: Demand, Supply and Market Equilibrium

**What you must know:** the meaning and law of demand; schedules, curves and functions; determinants and types of demand; normal, inferior, substitute and complementary goods; exceptions (Giffen, Veblen); supply, its law, determinants and types; change in demand vs change in quantity demanded; equilibrium from a table and from equations (Qd = Qs); shortage and surplus; effects of shifts; price ceiling, price floor, minimum wage and rent control.

### Lesson 5.1: Demand, the law of demand, schedules and functions

**Must know**

- **Price system (price mechanism):** the way the forces of demand and supply interact to fix the price and quantity of a commodity. The theory of demand studies the behaviour of the **consumer**.
- **Wants:** the unlimited desires of the consumer, with no plan to buy. **Demand** reflects a decision about which wants to satisfy and a plan to buy.
- **Demand:** the quantity of a good or service consumers are **willing and able** to buy at a given price within a given period of time, other things remaining unchanged.
- **Effective demand:** willingness to buy backed by the **ability to pay**. **Ineffective demand:** willingness or ability is lacking.
- **Quantity demanded:** the amount consumers plan to buy at **one particular price**.
- **Law of demand:** the higher the price, the lower the quantity demanded; the lower the price, the higher the quantity demanded, *ceteris paribus* (all other things being equal). Price and quantity demanded are **inversely (negatively)** related.
- **Assumptions of the law:** (1) consumer's income stays the same; (2) consumer's habits stay the same; (3) tastes and preferences stay the same; (4) no change in substitutes (prices of related goods). Only **own price** changes.
- **Demand schedule:** a **table** of prices and the quantities demanded per unit of time. **Demand curve:** the **graph** of the schedule. It is **negatively sloped**: downward from left to right.
- **Demand function:** Qd = f(Pc, Pr, I, T, N), where Pc = own price, Pr = prices of related goods, I = income, T = tastes, N = population (advertising, A, is often added). A linear form is **Qd = a − bP** (a = autonomous demand, the minus sign shows the law of demand).

**Explain more**

| Price of Amala (₦ per bag) | 1,000 | 2,000 | 3,000 | 4,000 | 5,000 | 6,000 | 7,000 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Quantity demanded (bags) | 120 | 100 | 80 | 60 | 40 | 20 | 0 |

As price rises, quantity demanded falls: the curve runs from top left to bottom right.

- **Using a demand function:** if Qd = 140 − 0.02P, at P = ₦1,000, Qd = 140 − 20 = **120**; at P = ₦1,500, Qd = 140 − 30 = **110**. Substitute the price and work it out.
- **Market demand** is the **horizontal sum** of individual demands. If at ₦3,000 John buys 60 bags and Rose buys 20, market quantity demanded = **80**.
- **Why the curve slopes downward:** **diminishing marginal utility** (each extra unit gives less satisfaction, so you buy more only at a lower price). A lower price also makes the good cheaper relative to substitutes (substitution effect) and raises real income (income effect).
- Each point on a demand curve shows the **highest price consumers are willing and able to pay** for that unit.
- **Trap:** the law of demand speaks of **quantity demanded**, not "demand". "The higher the price, the smaller the quantity demanded" is right; "the smaller the demand" is wrong. The law holds other determinants constant and lets price alone influence quantity.
- **Trap:** demand curve, aggregate demand curve and production possibility curve all slope **negatively**; the supply curve does **not**.

### Lesson 5.2: Determinants of demand, related goods and types of demand

**Must know**

**Determinants of demand:**

1. **Price of the commodity** (the most important; it causes movement along the curve).
2. **Prices of related goods** (substitutes and complements).
3. **Income** of the consumer.
4. **Tastes and preferences** (shaped by advertising, fashion, new improved products). **Preferences** means an individual's **attitudes and tastes** towards goods and services.
5. **Population** (size and structure; demographic and sociological factors).
6. **Expectations** of future price or income changes.
7. Others: psychological factors, marketing strategies, weather/season, availability of credit, interest rates, competition among service providers, government policies and laws.

- **Normal good:** demand **rises when income rises** and falls when income falls. **Most goods are normal.**
- **Inferior good:** demand **falls when income rises** and **rises when income falls** (e.g. second-hand clothing, cheap public transport).
- **Substitutes:** goods that can be used **in place of** each other (tea and coffee, butter and margarine, cola and lemon-lime drinks). Price of one **rises** → demand for the other **rises**.
- **Complements:** goods **used together** (tea and sugar, car and petrol, pen and ink). Price of one **falls** → demand for the other **rises**.
- The effect of a related good's price on demand is called **cross demand**.

**Types of demand:**

1. **Joint (complementary) demand:** goods demanded together; demand for one brings demand for the other (car and petrol, bread and butter).
2. **Competitive demand:** goods that are substitutes and compete to satisfy the same want (Coca-Cola and Pepsi, yam and potato).
3. **Derived demand:** a good wanted not for itself but for what it helps produce (labour for production, flour for bread, wood for furniture, money for purchases). **Demand for any factor of production, especially labour, is derived.**
4. **Composite demand:** one good demanded for **several uses** (palm oil for cooking and soap; flour for bread, cake and snacks).

**Exceptions to the law of demand** (upward-sloping, "exceptional" demand curve):

1. **Giffen goods:** very cheap staples of the poor; quantity demanded **falls when price falls** (and rises when price rises). The demand curve **slopes upward**.
2. **Veblen (ostentatious) goods:** goods of conspicuous consumption, symbols of wealth (expensive cars, jewellery); more is bought at higher prices.
3. **Fear of a future price rise or shortage:** people buy more even as price rises.
4. **Consumer ignorance:** buyers judge quality by price or are misled by branding.
**Bandwagon effect:** buying because others are buying. (The **snob effect** is the reverse: buying less because many others now have it.)

**Explain more**

- **Signs to memorise:** substitute's price **rises** → demand rises; complement's price **falls** → demand rises.
- **Spotting the relationship:** you buy more X when Y's price **rises** → substitutes. You buy more X when Y's price **falls** → complements. If a fall in the price of Bluetooth earbuds shifts demand for wired earphones **left**, they are **substitutes**. If cheaper petrol raises demand for large cars, they are **complements**. Roller skates and elbow pads are complements; cheaper pitch rental raises demand for football boots.
- **Opportunity cost link:** when a good's opportunity cost (price) falls, people buy **more of it and more of its complements**.
- **Mixed effects:** if a substitute's price and a complement's price both rise, demand for X may rise, fall or stay the same, depending on which effect is stronger.
- **Expectations:** if motorists expect petrol prices to rise next week, **today's demand rises**. An expected fall in future price reduces demand today.
- **Income examples:** in a recession, demand for a normal good (rechargeable fans) **decreases**. If incomes fall, demand for an inferior good (second-hand clothing) **increases** (shifts right).
- **Trap:** an ordinary inferior good still obeys the law of demand (price and quantity are **inversely** related). Only a **Giffen** good has an upward-sloping demand curve. All Giffen goods are inferior; not all inferior goods are Giffen.
- **Trap:** copper prices can stay low for years because copper now has close **substitutes** (aluminium, fibre optics).

### Lesson 5.3: Supply, its law, determinants and types

**Must know**

- **Supply:** the quantity of a good producers are **willing, able and ready** to offer for sale at each possible price per unit of time. **Quantity supplied:** the amount producers are willing to sell at **one particular price** in a given period.
- **Law of supply:** the higher the price, the higher the quantity supplied; the lower the price, the lower the quantity supplied, *ceteris paribus*. Price and quantity supplied are **directly (positively)** related.
- **Supply curve:** slopes **upward** from left to right. Reasons: (1) **cost coverage**: producing more raises costs (marginal cost rises), so producers need a higher price; (2) **profit incentive**: higher prices encourage more output.
- **Supply schedule** = a table; **supply curve** = a graph of it.
- **Supply function:** Qs = f(P, W, C, T, G, E, …). Linear form **Qs = c + dP** (positive sign shows the law of supply).

**Determinants of supply:**

1. Price of the commodity.
2. **Cost of production** (prices of inputs: wages, raw materials, machinery).
3. **Technology** (improvement raises supply).
4. **Weather, season, climate** and natural events (drought, floods, pests).
5. **Prices of other goods** the firm could produce instead.
6. **Taxes** (reduce supply) and **subsidies** (raise supply).
7. **Number of producers** and capacity of existing firms.
8. **Producers' expectations** and prevailing circumstances.

**Types of supply:**

1. **Joint (complementary) supply:** goods produced together from one source (beef and hide/leather, palm oil and palm kernel). More of one automatically gives more of the other. These are **complements in production**.
2. **Competitive supply:** goods that compete for the same resources (bread and meat pies from one oven). These are **substitutes in production**. It is also described as the supply of two or more goods that serve as substitutes for one another and satisfy the same want (butter and margarine).
3. **Composite supply:** one product supplied to serve several purposes (electricity for lighting, heating and industry); more for one use leaves less for others.

**Explain more**

| Price of Amala (₦ per bag) | 1,000 | 2,000 | 3,000 | 4,000 | 5,000 | 6,000 | 7,000 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Quantity supplied (bags) | 0 | 10 | 20 | 30 | 40 | 50 | 60 |

- **Using a supply function:** if Qs = −10 + 0.01P, at ₦1,000, Qs = −10 + 10 = **0**; at ₦1,500, Qs = −10 + 15 = **5**.
- Each point on a supply curve shows the **lowest price** at which a supplier is willing to sell one more unit.
- **Substitutes in production:** a Lagos bakery can bake bread or meat pies. If the price of meat pies **rises**, the bakery shifts ovens to pies, so the **supply of bread decreases** (shifts left). If the price of X **falls**, the supply of Y **increases**.
- **Complements in production:** if health fears cut demand for beef, fewer cattle are slaughtered, the **supply of leather falls**, and leather belts get **dearer**.
- **Exception to the law of supply:** the **backward-bending supply curve of labour**. Above a certain wage, workers choose more leisure, so hours supplied fall as the wage rises.
- **Trap:** the law of supply says firms offer **more at higher prices**. It does not say supply and demand rise together.

### Lesson 5.4: Change in demand or supply vs change in quantity

**Must know**

| | Change in **quantity** demanded/supplied | Change in demand/supply |
|---|---|---|
| Cause | **Own price** of the good only | Any **other** determinant |
| Graph | **Movement along** the same curve | **Shift** of the whole curve ("bodily shift") |
| Words | Extension / contraction | Increase / decrease |

- **Demand:** price falls → movement **down** the curve = increase in quantity demanded (**extension**). Price rises → movement **up and to the left** = decrease in quantity demanded (**contraction**).
- **Increase in demand** = **rightward (outward)** shift (more demanded at every price); **decrease in demand** = **leftward** shift (less at every price).
- **Supply:** price rises → movement **up** the supply curve (more quantity supplied); price falls → movement **down** (less quantity supplied).
- **Increase in supply** = **rightward (outward)** (or downward) shift (more supplied at every price); **decrease in supply** = **leftward** shift (less at every price).
- **Which curve moves?** An event that changes the **cost or ability to produce** (weather, drought, technology, input prices, taxes on producers) shifts **supply only**; demand stays where it is. An event that changes **buyers' willingness or ability to buy** (income, tastes, health news, prices of related goods, population) shifts **demand only**; supply stays where it is. For example, cheaper car-making technology increases the supply of cars but **not** the demand.

**Explain more**

**Shifts demand right (normal good):** income rises; tastes improve; a substitute's price rises; a complement's price falls; population rises; a future price rise is expected. The opposite of each shifts demand left.

**Shifts supply right:** input prices fall (lower wages); technology improves; subsidies; more producers; good weather; the price of a substitute-in-production falls. **Shifts supply left:** higher input costs (dearer machinery, rising wages); taxes; drought or an expected drought; a substitute-in-production becomes more profitable.

- Gas cookers: 200,000 sold at ₦150,000 one year and more the next **at the same price**. That is an **increase in demand** (population, income, cheaper cooking gas). An increase in **supply** cannot explain more sold at the **same** price.
- A 20% rise in the price of cars changes buying plans but **does not shift** the demand curve for cars. A change in the price of chicken does not shift chicken's demand curve.
- Streaming subscriptions are a **substitute** for DVD players and DVDs are a **complement**: dearer streaming and cheaper DVDs both shift the **demand for DVD players right**.
- Cheaper aviation fuel shifts the **supply** of air travel right. A short, light rainy season shifts the **demand** for umbrellas left.
- If wages fall but raw material prices rise, supply may shift left or right.
- **Trap:** a change in expectations, income or technology always gives a **change in demand/supply** (a shift), never a movement. A change in the good's **own price** never shifts its own curve.

### Lesson 5.5: Market equilibrium, shortage, surplus and shifts

**Must know**

- **Equilibrium:** a state with **no tendency to change**. **Equilibrium (market-clearing) price** is where **quantity demanded = quantity supplied**, found where the demand and supply curves **intersect**. It is set by the **interaction** of demand and supply, which fixes **both** price and quantity.
- **Excess demand (shortage):** Qd > Qs, at a price **below** equilibrium. Below equilibrium, quantity demanded is **greater** than the equilibrium quantity and quantity supplied is **less** than it. Price is pushed **up**; as it rises, Qd falls and Qs rises until the shortage disappears.
- **Excess supply (surplus):** Qs > Qd, at a price **above** equilibrium. Price is pushed **down**.
- Price moves with **excess demand (Qd − Qs)**: positive, price rises; negative, price falls; zero, equilibrium.
- **Mathematical method:** set **Qd = Qs**, solve for P, then put P into either equation to get Q.

**One curve shifts (the other unchanged):**

| Change | Equilibrium price | Equilibrium quantity |
|---|---|---|
| Demand increases (right) | Rises | Rises |
| Demand decreases (left) | Falls | Falls |
| Supply increases (right) | Falls | Rises |
| Supply decreases (left) | Rises | Falls |

**Both curves shift:**

| Change | Price | Quantity |
|---|---|---|
| Demand up, supply up | Uncertain | **Rises** |
| Demand down, supply down | Uncertain | **Falls** |
| Demand up, supply down | **Rises** | Uncertain |
| Demand down, supply up | **Falls** | Uncertain |

**Explain more**

**Worked example 1 (schedule):**

| Price (₦) | Qd | Qs | Qd − Qs | Situation |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 3,000 | 80 | 20 | +60 | Shortage |
| 4,000 | 60 | 30 | +30 | Shortage |
| 5,000 | 40 | 40 | 0 | **Equilibrium** |
| 6,000 | 20 | 50 | −30 | Surplus |

Equilibrium price = **₦5,000**, quantity = **40 bags**. At ₦6,000, supply exceeds demand by 30. Method: scan for the row where Qd = Qs; for any other price, subtract to get the size of the shortage or surplus.

**Worked example 2 (equations):** Qd = 20 − 2P and Qs = 5 + 3P.
20 − 2P = 5 + 3P → 15 = 5P → **P = ₦3**. Q = 20 − 2(3) = **14** (check: 5 + 3(3) = 14).

**Worked example 3 (a shift):** Qd = 1,000 − 2P, Qs = −200 + 4P.
1,000 − 2P = −200 + 4P → 1,200 = 6P → **P = ₦200**, Q = 1,000 − 400 = **600**.
- Demand increases to Qd = 1,300 − 2P: 1,500 = 6P → P = **₦250**, Q = **800** (both rise).
- Instead, supply increases to Qs = 100 + 4P: 900 = 6P → P = **₦150**, Q = **700** (price falls, quantity rises).

- **Reading shifts backwards:** price **up**, quantity **down** → supply shifted left. Price and quantity both **down** → demand shifted left. A fall in the price of milk could come from news that milk causes illness (demand left).
- **Related markets:** if a drought cuts beef supply, beef gets dearer and demand for **pork** (a substitute) shifts **right**. If shawarma gets cheaper, demand for burgers shifts left, so burger price and quantity both **fall**. If input costs for A fall and B is A's complement, A's price falls and B's price rises.
- **Both shifts:** when technology improves (supply right) and preference rises (demand right), quantity rises but price depends on which shift is larger. Solar inverter prices fell because supply shifted right **by more** than demand.
- **Trap:** with both curves shifting, one variable is always "uncertain". The **only** pair that always raises price is demand up + supply down; the only one that always lowers price is demand down + supply up.

### Lesson 5.6: Price controls: ceiling, floor, minimum wage, rent control

**Must know**

- **Price control:** government fixes prices by law when market prices seem unfairly high or low. It stops the market reaching equilibrium.
- **Price ceiling (maximum price):** a legal price set **below** equilibrium, above which sellers may not charge. It protects **consumers** (drugs, rents, essentials). Quantity demanded **increases**, quantity supplied falls → **persistent shortage (excess demand)**.
- **Problems of a ceiling:** (1) rationing (coupons); (2) queues and waiting lists; (3) **black (parallel) markets** at prices above equilibrium; (4) selling by sellers' preference; (5) random allocation.
- **Price floor (minimum price):** a legal price set **above** equilibrium, below which no one may buy. It protects **producers** (farm prices). Quantity supplied rises, quantity demanded falls → **persistent surplus (excess supply)**.
- **Problems of a floor:** frustrated sellers, undercutting the legal price, quotas, buyers choosing whom to buy from, conditional sales. Remedies include buffer stocks (government buys and stores the surplus) and exports.
- **Minimum wage:** a **price floor** for labour set above the equilibrium wage → **surplus (excess supply) of labour = unemployment**, especially among young and unskilled workers.
- **Rent control:** a **price ceiling** on rents → shortage of rental units and possible **black-market** transactions.

**Explain more**

Using Qd = 1,000 − 2P and Qs = −200 + 4P (equilibrium ₦200, 600 units):

| Control | Price | Qd | Qs | Result |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Ceiling | ₦150 | 700 | 400 | Shortage of **300** |
| Floor | ₦250 | 500 | 800 | Surplus of **300** |

- A ceiling on cement below equilibrium **increases quantity demanded** (a movement along the demand curve) and cuts quantity supplied.
- To support a ceiling, government can cut consumption (find cheaper substitutes) or raise supply (subsidies, imports, new firms).
- **Trap:** a ceiling **above** equilibrium, or a floor **below** it, has no effect (it does not bind). Ceiling → shortage; floor → surplus. Never swap them.
- **Trap:** rent control "may give rise to black-market transactions" is the safe answer; it creates excess **demand**, not excess supply.

### One-minute recap

- Demand = willingness **and** ability to buy; effective demand needs ability to pay.
- Law of demand: price and quantity demanded are inverse; the demand curve slopes down (diminishing marginal utility).
- Law of supply: price and quantity supplied are direct; the supply curve slopes up (rising cost, profit motive).
- Normal goods: demand rises with income; inferior goods: demand falls with income.
- Substitute's price up → demand up; complement's price down → demand up.
- Types of demand: joint, competitive, derived (labour), composite. Types of supply: joint, competitive, composite.
- Exceptions: Giffen (upward-sloping demand), Veblen/ostentatious, fear of future price rise, ignorance; backward-bending labour supply.
- Own price → movement along the curve; any other factor → shift.
- Equilibrium: Qd = Qs; below it, shortage; above it, surplus.
- Ceiling below equilibrium → shortage, black market (rent control); floor above → surplus (minimum wage → unemployment).

### Theory practice

**Question:** The demand and supply functions for garri are Qd = 600 − 3P and Qs = 100 + 2P (P in naira per cup). (a) Find the equilibrium price and quantity. (b) If the government fixes a maximum price of ₦80, what happens?

*Worked solution:* (a) 600 − 3P = 100 + 2P → 500 = 5P → **P = ₦100**; Q = 600 − 300 = **300 cups**. (b) ₦80 is below equilibrium, so it is a binding ceiling: Qd = 600 − 240 = 360, Qs = 100 + 160 = 260, giving a **shortage of 100 cups**, with queues, rationing and a likely black market.
