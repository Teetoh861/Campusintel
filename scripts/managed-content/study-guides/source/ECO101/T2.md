## Topic 2: Methodology of Economics

**What you must know:** positive vs normative statements; microeconomics vs macroeconomics; economics as a science and an art; inductive vs deductive methods; hypotheses, theories, models, assumptions and ceteris paribus; graphical, mathematical and empirical tools; and how to read graphs: axes, graph types, relationships, maximum and minimum points, and slope calculations.

### Lesson 2.1: Positive and normative economics

**Must know**

- **Methodology of economics:** the principles and approaches economists use to analyse economic problems, construct theories and develop policy recommendations.
- **Positive economics:** deals with facts, data and objective analysis; describes and explains economic phenomena **as they are** ("what is" or "what will be"), without value judgements. It predicts **how people will behave**.
- **Normative economics:** deals with value judgements and opinions; what the economy **"ought to be"** or **"should do"**. It concerns **how people should behave**.

| | Positive | Normative |
|---|---|---|
| Deals with | What is / will be | What ought to be |
| Nature | Objective, fact-based, descriptive | Subjective, value-based, prescriptive |
| Testable? | **Yes**, can be verified or falsified by evidence | **No**, cannot be proven true or false |
| Use | Explains and **predicts** | **Policy-oriented**, recommends |
| Example | "Higher interest rates lead to reduced borrowing." | "Government should provide free healthcare." |

- **4 characteristics of positive economics:** fact-based, descriptive, objective and testable, predictive. **Limitation:** it does not say whether outcomes are desirable or ethical.
- **4 characteristics of normative economics:** value-based, prescriptive, subjective and non-testable, policy-oriented. **Limitation:** subjectivity leads to disagreement.

**Explain more**

- **The test:** look for "should", "ought", or a judging word (fair, unfair, better, enough, disgrace, legitimate, more important). If none, ask: could data check this? If yes, it is positive.
- A positive statement need not be **true**: "If the central bank buys shares in commercial banks, inflation will rise" is positive because evidence can test it. An untested hypothesis is still positive.
- Normative without "should": "Nigerians do not save **enough**", "Strawberry ice cream is **better** than vanilla", "Welfare cuts impose an **unfair** hardship on the poor", "Redistributing income is a **legitimate** role for government".
- Positive: "A bar of chocolate costs ₦500", "Households are the main source of saving", "A rise in the price of petrol will lead to a fall in the quantity bought", "When national unemployment is high, youth unemployment is usually higher", "Rent controls reduce the supply of housing".
- On a tax: **who bears the burden** and **how it affects incentive to work** are positive; **whether it is fair** is normative.
- **Trap:** "positive" does not mean optimistic or strongly worded; positive and normative statements can be made in **both** micro and macro.
- The **German historical school** emphasised the normative (ethical) side of economics. **Robbins's** view was the opposite: economics is neutral between ends, a positive science.

### Lesson 2.2: Microeconomics and macroeconomics

**Must know**

- The terms "micro" and "macro" economics were coined by **Ragnar Frisch** (Norwegian). J.M. Keynes is linked with the growth of macroeconomics.
- **Microeconomics:** the study of **individual choice under scarcity**: the behaviour of individual units (consumers/households, firms, industries, individual markets) and how their decisions interact, and how prices allocate scarce resources among different uses. Called **price theory** because it focuses on how relative prices of small units are determined and various equilibria reached.
- **Macroeconomics:** the study of the **economy as a whole** (national and global), using **aggregate variables**: national income/output, unemployment, inflation (general price level), economic growth, fiscal and monetary policy. Called **income theory**.

**Micro covers (all of these):**

1. Theory of **product pricing** (prices of goods and services).
2. Theory of **factor pricing** (wages, rent, interest, profit).
3. Theory of **economic welfare** (efficiency of allocation).

Also: theory of the firm, consumer theory, market structures, cost of production, distribution of resources. Micro answers **what, how and for whom** to produce.

**Explain more**

- Micro deals mainly with **comparative statics** (comparing one equilibrium with another), **partial equilibrium** (one market at a time, others held constant) and **positive economics**. The **time path** from one equilibrium to another is **dynamics**, which is not micro's main concern. Looking at all markets at once is **general equilibrium**.

| Question | Branch |
|---|---|
| Why the price of rice rose; a lower smartphone price and sales | Micro |
| Why electricians earn more than cleaners (factor pricing) | Micro |
| A petrol price rise and one person; cigarette tax and cigarette sales | Micro |
| How many workers a small business should hire | Micro |
| A bank chief on lending standards in the banking industry | Micro |
| Rise in the **average** level of prices; money supply and inflation | Macro |
| Total output and jobs in a recession; national unemployment rate | Macro |
| Growth prospects of an economy; government spending and national output | Macro |
| Company tax and the **natural rate of unemployment** | Macro |
| CBN Governor on Nigeria's role in the world economy | Macro |

- **Traps:** macro is **not** "government" vs micro "private firms"; both use the same basic tools (demand, supply). The **price of one good** is micro; the **general price level** is macro.

### Lesson 2.3: Science, art and methods of reasoning

**Must know**

- **Science:** a systematic body of knowledge using observation, experimentation and reasoning to establish principles and laws. **Art:** practical application of knowledge to achieve objectives.
- Economics is **both a science and an art**, depending on how and for what purpose it is used. It is a **social science** that uses mathematics more heavily than sociology, political science or anthropology.
- **Science features (5):** systematic study, empirical basis, generalisations, cause-and-effect relationships, testing and verification.
- **Limits as a science (3):** inexact (human behaviour is unpredictable), lack of universal validity, non-experimental (controlled experiments are rarely possible).
- **Art features (5):** practical application, policy formulation, problem-solving, creative solutions, dynamic nature.
- **Scientific method:** observation → hypothesis formulation → testing → theory development.

| | Inductive | Deductive |
|---|---|---|
| Direction | **Particular → general** (specific observations to general laws) | **General → particular** (assumptions to specific conclusions) |
| Steps | Observation, data analysis, generalisation, verification | Assumptions, logical reasoning, application, verification |
| Nature | **Empirical**, historical, realistic | **Abstract**, analytical, hypothetical, a priori |
| Used by | **German historical school** | **English classical economists** (e.g. Ricardo, J.S. Mill) |
| Examples | Law of demand; business cycle theories | Law of diminishing marginal utility; Ricardian theory of rent |
| Advantages | Real-world basis, empirical evidence, flexibility | Logical clarity, time-efficient, wide applicability |
| Limitations | Time-consuming, possibility of error, limited scope | Unrealistic assumptions, limited empirical basis, ignores context |

**Explain more**

- Induction collects facts first, then generalises; deduction needs no experimentation and is faster. Traps: "the classical economists mainly used induction" is false; "deduction is highly realistic" is false; "the historical school supported deduction" is false.
- **Empirical research:** research done by observing real-life data or analysing patterns of actual events to identify trends. It links with induction.
- Economists use both methods together: deduce a prediction, then verify it inductively.

### Lesson 2.4: Hypotheses, theories, models and tools

**Must know**

- **Hypothesis:** a proposition whose validity is yet to be tested. It is best tested by **whether it accurately predicts outcomes** that follow from its assumptions (not by how realistic its assumptions are, nor by whether economists agree).
- **Theory:** a hypothesis that has been **tested and supported** by evidence. Also called an economic law, principle or model; built on assumptions.
- **Model:** a **simplified** representation of reality. It turns verbal statements into precise, scientific forms. A **good model includes only the features needed for the purpose at hand**. It does not include all facts and need not use graphs.
- **Steps in building a model:** **abstraction → deduction → testing**.
- **Variable:** a quantity that can change. **Dependent** variable: the one explained. **Independent** variable: the one doing the explaining. **Constant (parameter):** a quantity fixed in value, e.g. 50 in Qd = 50 − 2P.
- **Function:** the relationship between a dependent variable and one or more independent variables, e.g. Qd = f(P, Y).
- **Ceteris paribus:** "all other things remaining equal"; holds all other factors constant to isolate the effect of one variable.

**4 reasons for assumptions:** simplify reality; make models testable; facilitate predictions; ensure logical consistency.

**4 types of assumptions:**

1. **Behavioural:** rationality, self-interest, perfect information.
2. **Structural:** market structure, production technology, degree of government intervention.
3. **Equilibrium:** market clearing, steady-state growth.
4. **Mathematical:** linear relationships, differentiability, ceteris paribus.

**3 languages of economics:** verbal (words; widest audience), graphs (visual; relationship between two variables), equations (complex relations of three or more variables).

**3 tool groups:**

1. **Graphical:** supply and demand curves, PPF, indifference curves and budget lines, IS-LM model, Phillips curve, Lorenz curve, growth models (Solow).
2. **Mathematical:** optimisation (Lagrange multipliers, calculus for marginal analysis), differential and difference equations, linear algebra and matrices (Leontief input-output, general equilibrium), game theory (Nash equilibrium), control theory and dynamic programming, econometric models.
3. **Empirical:** descriptive statistics (mean, median, variance, skewness, kurtosis), econometrics (OLS, GMM, time series: ARIMA, VAR, cointegration; panel data; instrumental variables; difference-in-differences and regression discontinuity), computational methods (Monte Carlo, machine learning, Bayesian econometrics).

**Explain more**

| Tool | Strengths | Weaknesses |
|---|---|---|
| Graphical | Easy to interpret; shows trends; spots outliers; supports exploration | Cannot establish causality; limited for complex relationships; sensitive to scaling |
| Mathematical | Formal theory; generalises; predicts; aids policy | Strong assumptions; misses behavioural factors; needs empirical validation |
| Empirical | Validates theory with real data; can establish causality; controls for bias; handles nonlinear links | Depends on data quality; sensitive to specification; multicollinearity, endogeneity, omitted variables; computationally heavy |

- Graphs give quick insight; maths gives theoretical foundation; empirics gives evidence. Integrate all three.
- Other micro tools: statistical methods, case studies, comparative statics, behavioural and experimental economics.
- **Realism vs simplicity:** assumptions let you focus but may oversimplify. Models improve by **relaxing** assumptions: asymmetric information (Akerlof's "Market for Lemons"), bounded rationality (Kahneman and Tversky's prospect theory), sticky prices (New Keynesian).
- **Bounded rationality (Herbert Simon, 1955):** people decide within limits of information, cognitive ability and time, so they **satisfice** (pick "good enough"), e.g. comparing only a few phones.
- Classical models assume flexible wages and prices; Keynesian models assume rigidity.

### Lesson 2.5: Reading graphs

**Must know**

- **x-axis:** the **horizontal** axis. **y-axis:** the **vertical** axis. **Origin:** where they meet, (0, 0).
- Values increase **left to right** on the x-axis and **upward** on the y-axis. **Both** axes can show negative values (left of or below the origin), e.g. negative profit.
- **x-coordinate:** the distance from the point **to the y-axis**. **y-coordinate:** the distance from the point **to the x-axis**.

| Graph | What it shows | Use it for |
|---|---|---|
| **Time-series** | One or more variables (y-axis) against **time** (x-axis) | Level at any date, direction of change, speed of change, **trend** |
| **Cross-section** | Values of a variable for **different groups at one point in time** | Inflation in West African countries in one year; June rainfall in 30 cities |
| **Scatter diagram** | One variable plotted **against another** | Whether two variables are **correlated**, and how strongly |

- **Trend:** a general tendency for a variable to rise or fall. Only a **time-series** graph shows trends.

**Explain more**

- Time-series of shoe sales: you can read the level, the direction **and** how fast sales changed (steepness). The line slopes **up** when the variable rises, **down** when it falls, and is **flat** when it is unchanged.
- **Positive trend** 1990-2020 means output was **higher in 2020 than 1990**, not that it rose every year. **Negative trend** in unemployment means it was **lower** at the end.
- Rice price climbing steadily: positive (upward) trend.
- Scatter examples: household income against food spending; Kano temperature against bottled water sales; saving against income. If interest rates rise whenever Lagos house prices fall, a scatter diagram shows a **negative relationship**.
- **Correlation is not causation:** a high correlation does **not** mean x causes y. A third factor may drive both, or causation may run the other way.
- **Any** graph type can mislead: stretched or squeezed scales, an axis not starting at zero, a chosen time period.

### Lesson 2.6: Relationships and slope

**Must know**

- **Positive (direct) relationship:** variables move in the **same direction** (both rise or both fall). Graph slopes **upward**; slope positive.
- **Negative (inverse) relationship:** variables move in **opposite directions**. Graph slopes **downward** (falls left to right = rises right to left); slope negative.
- **Unrelated (independent):** a **horizontal** line (y stays the same whatever x is; slope zero) or a **vertical** line (x stays the same while y changes; slope undefined).
- **Maximum:** curve rises then falls (∩). **Minimum:** curve falls then rises (U). Slope **at** the maximum or minimum is **zero**.
- **Slope = change in y ÷ change in x = Δy/Δx** ("rise over run"; vertical-axis variable over horizontal-axis variable). Not Δx/Δy, not Δy divided by the starting x, and not a ratio of percentage changes.
- Straight (linear) line: **constant slope**. Curve: slope changes; slope **at a point** = slope of the **tangent** at that point; slope **across an arc** = slope of the straight line joining two points (approximates the curve's slope).
- On a graph, **two** variables change; all others are held constant (**ceteris paribus**).

**Explain more**

**Worked slope 1 (negative):** x rises 6 → 8, y falls 4 → 3. Slope = (3 − 4)/(8 − 6) = −1/2. Traps: −2 (ratio upside down), 1/2 (sign lost).

**Worked slope 2 (positive):** (90, 30) and (120, 40). Slope = (40 − 30)/(120 − 90) = 10/30 = **1/3**.

**Finding a new y:** new y = old y + slope × Δx.
- Slope 3/4; x = 20 gives y = 14. At x = 32: 14 + (3/4)(12) = 14 + 9 = **23** (9 alone is only the change).
- Slope 3; x = 10 gives y = 33. At x = 11: 33 + 3 = **36**.
- y always equals x: slope = **1**.

**Sign rules:** Δy = −4, Δx = 2 → slope −2, negative relationship. Δy = −20, Δx = −4 → slope +5, **positive** (both fell together). A slope of 0.5 is positive.

**Size of slope:** slope > 1: y changes **more** than x. Slope < 1 (positive): y changes **less** than x. A large Δx with a small Δy gives a **small** slope, upward or downward.

**Demand example:** price on y-axis, quantity on x-axis. Price ₦100 → ₦80, quantity 20 → 30. Slope = ΔP/ΔQ = −20/10 = −2.

**Curves from tables:**

| Workers | Chin-chin packs/day | Slope (extra packs) |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | 0 | |
| 1 | 10 | 10 |
| 2 | 18 | 8 |
| 3 | 24 | 6 |

Positive relationship, **decreasing slope**: upward-sloping, getting **flatter**.

- **Statements to classify:** study hours and CGPA: positive (= direct). Interest rates rise, saving rises: positive. Interest rates fall, spending rises: inverse, negative slope. Car speed and km per litre: inverse. Grades and party time: downward, **could be straight or curved** (direction alone does not tell you the shape). Lagos rainfall and a cable TV fee: unrelated. Tomato price unchanged as car sales rise: **horizontal** line.
- **Ceteris paribus on a graph:** if suya bought depends on price, income and taste, a price-quantity graph lets **only price and quantity** change; income and taste are held constant. To graph many variables, hold all but **two** constant.

### One-minute recap

- Positive = what is, testable; normative = what ought to be, value judgement.
- Micro = individual units, price theory (Frisch's terms); macro = aggregates, income theory.
- Economics is both a science and an art; inexact because of human behaviour.
- Induction: particular → general, empirical (historical school). Deduction: general → particular, abstract (classicals).
- Hypothesis (untested) → theory (tested); models simplify; abstraction, deduction, testing.
- Tools: graphical, mathematical, empirical.
- Time-series = trend; cross-section = groups at one time; scatter = correlation (not causation).
- Slope = Δy/Δx; constant on a straight line; tangent at a point, arc between points; zero at a maximum or minimum.

### Theory practice

**Question:** A straight line passes through (4, 10) and (12, 6). Find its slope, state the relationship, and find y when x = 20.

*Worked solution:* Slope = (6 − 10)/(12 − 4) = −4/8 = −1/2. Negative (inverse) relationship. From x = 12 to 20, Δx = 8, so Δy = −1/2 × 8 = −4; y = 6 − 4 = **2**.
