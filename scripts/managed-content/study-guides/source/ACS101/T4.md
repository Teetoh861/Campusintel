## Topic 4: Actuarial Science, Insurance and the Role of Actuaries in Society

**What you must know:** what actuaries do for insurers and for society; the key insurance ideas (pooling, underwriting, hazards, reinsurance, risk management); pricing, reserving and solvency with their simple calculations; the branches, products and pension arrangements actuaries work on; where actuaries work and their career choices.

### Lesson 4.1: What actuaries do for insurers and for society

**Must know**

- **Actuarial science:** deals with evaluating risks and maintaining the economic stability of insurance or financial organisations, using **mathematics, statistics and probability** to anticipate future events and take preventive measures.
- **An actuary** analyses, manages and measures the financial implications of future risk; develops and validates financial models to guide decisions; and is the analytical backbone of society's financial security programmes (insurance, social security, pensions).
- **Method:** analyse **past data**, identify a **trend**, predict the future, then decide how much money to set aside for future losses. Using the past is like "driving a car by looking in the rear-view mirror", so the actuary must also **quantify how the future will be different from the past** (e.g. the effect of a big rise in petrol prices on motor insurance costs).
- **Main challenge:** when there is **no past data**, or the data is no longer relevant because of policy or other changes (e.g. a brand-new product).

**What actuaries do (list):**

1. **Evaluate the likelihood of future events:** forecast the impact of catastrophes; calculate the expected lifetime of a retiree on a pension.
2. **Design creative ways to reduce the likelihood of undesirable events:** life insurance policies, annuities, promoting safety (seat belts).
3. **Decrease the impact of undesirable events that do occur:** death benefits, retirement benefits, indemnity benefits.
4. **Participate in corporate planning:** mergers and acquisitions; evaluating investment programmes.
5. **Help insurance companies:** calculate rates for new insurance products; forecast and interpret underwriting results; maintain databases suited to the analysis of pricing issues.

- **Typical areas of actuarial work:** insurance (general, health, life); finance (banking, corporate, investment, pensions); data science and analytics; technology; enterprise risk; resource and environment (**climate change**).
- **Where else actuaries add value:** supporting **marketing teams by modelling demand** for a new product; supporting accounting teams with models; budgeting and forecasting; product development; managing risk.
- **In digital ecosystems:** expertise in **predictive modelling and optimisation** for quicker, smarter business risk decisions.

| | Actuary | Accountant | CFA charterholder |
|---|---|---|---|
| Focus | Future events that may or may not occur | Events that have already happened | Financial reports and investments |
| Assets/liabilities | Both; **uniquely trained to understand liabilities** (they are complicated) | Reports on both | Geared towards **assets** |
| Products | How products make money | Cost, revenue, profit | Which products or companies to **invest in** |

**Explain more**

- **Lagos example:** to predict crashes next November, the actuary studies the percentage of people in accidents in previous years, finds a trend, then **establishes how much each person should pay** so premiums cover the damages.
- A death benefit paid to a family *decreases the impact* of an event that has happened; a seat-belt campaign *reduces the likelihood or effect* of the event. Exams swap these two.
- Valuing an insurer's long-term liabilities before a bank buys it is **corporate planning (mergers and acquisitions)**.
- Actuaries do **not** decide court cases, sell door to door or repair vehicles.

### Lesson 4.2: Core insurance ideas

**Must know**

- **Insurance company:** an institution that, by contract, binds itself to **indemnify** another party against specified loss in return for premiums. **Indemnity:** compensation for damages or loss.
- **Financial security system:** an arrangement in which one person takes on the obligation to provide benefits to offset undesirable economic consequences suffered by a second person, in return for payments called **considerations** (e.g. insurance products, retirement plans). It is **mandatory** if everyone in the group must take part; otherwise **voluntary**.
- **Insurable risk** criteria: (1) a potential loss; (2) that is significant; (3) loss and its economic value well defined and out of the policyholder's complete control; (4) covered losses reasonably independent. The risk must not be so large that no insurer could pay.
- **Peril:** the cause of loss (fire, flood, theft) against which cover is bought. **Hazard:** a condition that increases the probability or size of a loss.
- **Moral hazard:** an insured takes more risk knowing he will be compensated. **Adverse selection (antiselection):** people with above-average likelihood of loss are more likely to apply for or keep insurance.
- **Pooling:** forming a group of policies with similar characteristics (e.g. group size, benefit structure) to protect the insurer, based on the **law of large numbers**: the larger and more homogeneous the group, the more certain the predictions. **Risk pool:** such a group of policies. **Risk subject:** the person or object involved in the insured event.
- **Underwriting:** the process by which an insurer decides **which people to accept and on what terms**. **Underwriting factors:** what the insurer considers before issuing a policy and setting the premium (age, health history, occupation).
- **Risk classification:** grouping insureds with similar underwriting factors so each group pays a fair premium.
- **Reinsurance:** another licensed (re)insurer agrees to cover all or part of a primary insurer's losses for a premium. **Catastrophe reinsurance:** pays when losses from a **single event** exceed the buyer's retention.
- **Mortality:** the incidence of **death** in a population. **Morbidity:** the rate of **disease**.
- **Frequency distribution:** how **many** claims occur. **Severity distribution:** how **large** each claim is.
- **Risk management:** identifying and assessing risk, then developing strategies to minimise, monitor and control it. **Ideal prioritisation:** greatest loss **and** greatest probability first; lower loss and lower probability later.
- **Risk management system** activities: risk identification, risk assessment, risk control, risk reporting and monitoring, risk financing. ("Risk concealment" is not one.)

**Explain more**

- **Fire vs petrol:** an Onitsha shop insured against fire stores petrol beside wiring. Fire = **peril**; the stored petrol = **hazard**.
- **Leaving an insured car unlocked** = moral hazard (behaviour changes *after* cover). **More sick people applying for a flat-premium health plan** = adverse selection (who joins).
- **Law of large numbers:** 5,000 similar traders give far more reliable predictions than 20; size and similarity, not honesty, matter.
- A state that requires every employee to join its health scheme runs a **mandatory** system; a club where joining is optional runs a **voluntary** one.
- Risks ranked (I) high loss, high probability; (II) low, low; (III) high loss, low probability; (IV) low loss, high probability: handle **I first**, II last.

### Lesson 4.3: Pricing, reserving and solvency

**Must know**

- **Actuarial pricing:** pricing risk on probabilities of loss occurrence, to develop premiums that cover losses from underwritten risks and future benefits.
- Expected claim cost per policy = probability of claim × average claim
- Expected total claims = number of policies × probability × average claim
- Premium with loading = expected claim cost × (1 + loading %)
- **Gross (office) premium** components: (1) risk premium; (2) commission; (3) allowance for other expenses; (4) allowance for premium tax; (5) profit loading. Surrender value is **not** a part.
- **Reserves** (life insurance): money held to cover **future policyholder benefits and claims** on policies already sold.
- **IBNR reserve:** estimate owed for covered losses that have occurred but **not yet been reported**.
- **Earned premium:** the part for cover already given. **Unearned premium:** premium paid for cover **not yet provided**; held in the **unearned premium reserve (UPR)**.
- Unearned premium = annual premium × months of cover remaining / 12
- Loss ratio = claims incurred / premiums earned
- **Solvency:** assets greater than the sum of debt obligations. Solvency margin = assets − liabilities
- **Risk-based capital (RBC):** theoretical capital needed to absorb a business's risks so that it has an acceptably low chance of insolvency. **Capital requirement:** capital a bank or financial institution must hold as set by its regulator.
- **Experience rating:** adjusting a client's premium using the part of its own claims experience judged credible. **Credibility:** the relative weight given to one of two or more estimates (e.g. the group's own data versus manual rates).

| Actuary | Main job |
|---|---|
| **Pricing actuary** | Sets the premium: decides the appropriate "just a little more" (loading) and validates the expected number and amount of claims |
| **Valuation actuary** | Calculates reserves (liabilities) for policies already sold |
| **Pension actuary** | Projects members' benefits from ages and salaries, allowing for leavers and pay rises |
| **Consulting actuary** | Advises client organisations from outside, often on pensions |

- **Simulation:** used to **model potential outcomes under uncertainty**. A **single projection** uses one set of assumptions, so it **may not capture the full range of scenarios**. Extra scenarios show the **range of outcomes under different conditions**. The **mean** measures central tendency of the output; range, variance and standard deviation measure spread.

**Explain more**

*Worked example 1 (pricing).* An insurer covers 1,000 keke drivers. Probability of a claim = 0.05, average claim = ₦300,000.
- Expected cost per driver = 0.05 × ₦300,000 = **₦15,000**.
- Expected total claims = 1,000 × ₦15,000 = **₦15,000,000**.
- With a 25% loading: ₦15,000 × 1.25 = **₦18,750**.
- Trap: the total (₦15,000,000) or the loading alone (₦3,750) appear as wrong options.

*Worked example 2 (unearned premium).* Premium ₦240,000 paid on 1 April 2025 for 12 months; year-end 31 December 2025.
- Months elapsed = 9 (April to December). Earned = ₦240,000 × 9/12 = ₦180,000.
- Months remaining = 3. Unearned = ₦240,000 × 3/12 = **₦60,000**.
- Count the months *left*, not the months gone.

*Worked example 3 (loss ratio and solvency).* Claims incurred ₦30m, premiums earned ₦48m: loss ratio = 30/48 = **62.5%**. Assets ₦5.0bn, liabilities ₦4.2bn: solvency margin = **₦0.8bn** (solvent, since assets exceed liabilities). Never divide premiums by claims.

- **IBNR case:** floods on 29 December, year-end 31 December, losses not yet reported: the provision is IBNR, not UPR.
- Ten years of reliable bank data earns high **credibility**; a tiny start-up relies on manual rates.

### Lesson 4.4: Branches, products and where actuaries work

**Must know**

**8 specialisations:**

1. Health and Care
2. Life Insurance
3. Pensions
4. Investment and Finance
5. Financial Derivatives
6. General Insurance: Reserving
7. General Insurance: Pricing
8. Enterprise Risk Management

**Most common industries:**

1. **Insurance:** property (home) and casualty (auto); life and annuities.
2. **Employee benefits:** retirement benefits, health benefits, social security.
3. **Financial services:** banks, investing, risk management, mergers and acquisitions.

- Actuaries are largely employed by **insurance companies and consulting firms**, in: life, annuities and mutual funds; health; pensions; property and casualty.
- **Life insurance:** term and permanent products giving **death benefit protection**.
- **Annuity:** fixed or variable products; customer pays a lump sum or series of payments now for **periodic payments beginning immediately or in the future**.
- **Mutual fund:** **pools the assets of multiple investors** and buys securities towards a common, pre-defined goal.
- **Pension:** **steady income**, typically a guaranteed annuity, to a **retired or disabled employee** under legal or contractual terms.
- **Term life:** pays only if death occurs within a set period. **Whole life:** set death benefit, level premium, guaranteed cash values. **Key person insurance:** life cover on a key employee or owner; the **business is the beneficiary**.
- **Casualty insurance:** broad non-life, non-health cover against loss of property, damage or liabilities (motor, liability, theft).
- **Lapse:** policy ends because the renewal premium is not paid within the grace period. **Surrender value:** what the policyholder receives on choosing to stop the policy.
- **Health segments (employers):** small group 2 to 50 employees; middle market 51 to 3,000; national accounts 3,001+.

**Typical projects:**

| Area | Project |
|---|---|
| Life insurance | Designing and pricing products; impact of a disease (e.g. AIDS) on mortality tables |
| Property and casualty | Cost of personal auto or homeowners cover in an area; impact of global warming |
| Health benefits | Setting HMO premium rates |
| Retirement systems | Pricing the cost of increasing retirement benefits |

**Explain more**

- **Branch by task:** 10-year term assurance priced on mortality = **life actuary**; HMO premiums for employers = **health actuary**; employer's yearly payments into a DB scheme = **pension actuary**; matching assets to annuity payment timing = **investment actuary**; reserves for motor and fire claims not yet settled = **general insurance reserving**.
- Motor insurance on a commercial bus is **general (non-life)**; whole life, term life and annuities are life business.
- A company with 120 staff is **middle market**.
- A retiree paying ₦10,000,000 once for monthly income for life has bought an **annuity**.

### Lesson 4.5: Pensions, social security and government

**Must know**

- **Defined benefit (DB):** benefit set by scheme rules from **pay, years of service, age at retirement**, independently of contributions; the **employer** bears the investment risk.
- **Defined contribution (DC):** benefit depends on contributions paid plus investment return; the **employee** bears the investment risk.
- DB pension = accrual rate × years of service × final salary
- DC yearly payment in = (employee + employer monthly contribution) × 12
- **Pay-as-you-go:** benefits paid out of current revenue, with **no funding** for future liabilities.
- **Funding objective:** assesses the level of funding a pension plan needs to provide its members' benefits.
- **Active member:** still contributing and building benefits. **Deferred member:** has left before pension age with preserved benefits paid at retirement.
- **Nigeria:** the **Contributory Pension Scheme** (Pension Reform Act 2014) is **DC**: each worker has a **Retirement Savings Account (RSA)**; minimum contributions are 8% (employee) and 10% (employer) of monthly emoluments. It is regulated by **PenCom**. Insurance is regulated by **NAICOM**.

**Roles in government, social security, banking and regulation:**

1. Costing social security and public-service pensions (e.g. a 10% rise in civil servants' benefits = **retirement systems** work).
2. Supporting the regulator (**NAICOM**) in setting and reviewing **capital and reserving requirements**.
3. In banking, measuring the risks behind regulatory **capital requirements**.
4. Measuring insurance liabilities under **IFRS 17**, which **increases demand** for actuaries.

**Explain more**

*Worked example 4 (DB).* Accrual 1.5% per year, 30 years' service, final salary ₦3,600,000.
- Pension = 0.015 × 30 × ₦3,600,000 = **₦1,620,000 a year**.
- Trap: forgetting years of service gives ₦54,000.

*Worked example 5 (DC).* Monthly pay ₦300,000; employee 8% = ₦24,000; employer 10% = ₦30,000.
- Monthly total = ₦54,000; yearly = ₦54,000 × 12 = **₦648,000** (ignoring returns and charges).
- Trap: giving only one month, or only one side's contribution.

- **Nigeria (2022):** few qualified actuaries (about 19 Fellows) against 56 insurers, no local actuarial tables, and plans for risk-based capital and IFRS 17: demand exceeds supply.

### Lesson 4.6: Career choices for an actuary

**Must know**

- **3 main bodies:** **Institute and Faculty of Actuaries (IFoA)**, **Society of Actuaries (SOA)**, **Casualty Actuarial Society (CAS)**. ASSA (South Africa) is another.
- **CAS covers property and casualty (general) only**; SOA covers life, pensions and health; IFoA and ASSA cover all four.
- **Two levels** (SOA, CAS): **Associate** (ASA, ACAS) first, then **Fellow** (FSA, FCAS); both by examinations taken while working.
- **IFoA specialist principles:** SP1 Health and Care; SP2 Life Insurance; SP4 Pensions and Other Benefits; SP5 Investment and Finance; SP6 Financial Derivatives; **SP7 General Insurance Reserving and Capital Modelling**; SP8 General Insurance Pricing; SP9 Enterprise Risk Management.

**Skills needed:**

1. Mathematical and analytical skills
2. Project management and problem-solving skills
3. Excellent communication skills (oral and written)
4. Strong business sense and commercial awareness
5. Self-motivation (exams while working)
6. Ambition and ability to work in a team

**Reasons to become an actuary:** job security; good pay; recognised qualifications; **highly transferable skills**; **international opportunities**; "**earn while you learn**"; early advancement is largely **exam-based**; dynamic work.

**Explain more**

- Working alone and avoiding communication is **not** a required skill. Advancement does **not** depend only on length of service.

### One-minute recap

- Actuarial science evaluates risk with mathematics, statistics and probability; actuaries use past data but must quantify how the future will differ.
- Actuaries evaluate likelihood, reduce likelihood, decrease impact, join corporate planning, and help insurers price and forecast.
- Peril causes loss, hazard increases it; moral hazard is behaviour after cover, adverse selection is who applies.
- Pooling relies on the law of large numbers; underwriting decides who to accept and on what terms.
- Expected claim cost = probability × average claim; premium = cost × (1 + loading).
- Unearned premium = premium × months left / 12; loss ratio = claims incurred / premiums earned; solvency margin = assets − liabilities.
- DB: employer bears risk; DC (Nigeria's CPS, PenCom): employee bears risk.
- Bodies: IFoA, SOA, CAS (P&C only); Associate then Fellow.

### Theory practice

**Question:** An insurer covers 400 Lagos shop owners against burglary for one year. The probability of a claim is 0.02 per shop and the average claim is ₦500,000 (at most one claim each). It adds a 30% loading. Find (a) the risk premium per shop, (b) the gross premium per shop, (c) the expected loss ratio if claims come in as expected.

*Solution:*
- (a) 0.02 × ₦500,000 = **₦10,000**.
- (b) ₦10,000 × 1.30 = **₦13,000**.
- (c) Expected claims = 400 × ₦10,000 = ₦4,000,000; premiums = 400 × ₦13,000 = ₦5,200,000; loss ratio = 4,000,000 / 5,200,000 = **76.92%** (to 2 decimal places).
