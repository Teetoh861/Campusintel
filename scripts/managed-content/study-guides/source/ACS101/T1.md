## Topic 1: Nature of Actuarial Science and the Actuary

**What you must know:** the definitions of actuarial science and an actuary; risk, uncertainty and contingent events; probability, expected value, the law of large numbers and pooling; what actuaries do and how they model; where actuaries work; the skills needed; how actuaries differ from accountants, statisticians and CFAs.

### Lesson 1.1: What actuarial science is and who an actuary is

**Must know**

- **Actuarial science:** deals with evaluating risks and maintaining the **economic stability** of insurance or financial organisations. Graduates learn to use **mathematics, statistics and probability** principles to **anticipate future events and take preventive measures**. In short, it is concerned with the **financial consequences of uncertain future events**.
- **Main goal of actuarial studies:** to build the knowledge needed to do an actuary's job and to prepare students to pass their actuarial exams faster.
- **Actuary (standard definition):** a person who applies **mathematical approaches to anticipate, measure and manage risk**.
- Other definitions you must recognise:
  - A business professional who manages **financial risk** related to insurance product design, pensions and other financial corporate planning.
  - By applying probability, statistics, risk theory and financial principles, an actuary is able to **quantify** future risk in insurance, annuity and pension programmes.
- **An actuary:**
  1. Analyses, manages and measures the financial implications of future risk.
  2. Develops and validates financial models to guide decision making.
  3. Serves as the **analytical backbone** of society's financial security programmes (**insurance, social security, pensions**).
- **Disciplines drawn on:** probability, statistics, economics, finance and computer science.
- **Origin of the word:** Latin **actuarius**, a Roman clerk who kept records and accounts.

**Explain more**

- **"Part super-hero, part fortune-teller, part trusted advisor."** The fortune-teller part is evaluating the likelihood of future events **using numbers, not crystal balls**. Actuaries are experts in three things:
  1. Evaluating the likelihood of future events.
  2. Designing creative ways to reduce the likelihood of undesirable events.
  3. Decreasing the impact of undesirable events that do occur.
  
  "Guaranteeing that undesirable events never occur" is **not** one of them: no one can promise that.
- As **problem solvers and strategic thinkers**, actuaries measure the probability and risk of future events and predict their financial impact on a business and its clients.
- **Rear-view mirror:** an actuary typically uses **data from the past to predict the future**, like driving by looking in the rear-view mirror, so must also **quantify how the future will be different from the past**. A sharp rise in petrol prices changes motor insurance costs, so last year's claims are not simply copied forward.
- **Main challenge:** there is **no past data**, or the data is **no longer relevant** because of policy or other changes.
- Match the discipline to the task: estimating death rates from past records is **statistics**; processing two million records and running simulations is **computer science**; asking how Nigerian inflation, interest rates and unemployment affect claims and returns is **economics**.

### Lesson 1.2: Risk, uncertainty and contingent events

**Must know**

- **Risk:** (1) the **possibility of failing to meet objectives**; (2) it **can only be defined in context**: a risk to one person or entity may not be a risk to another.
- **Uncertainty:** a situation that involves **imperfect or unknown information**. It applies to predictions of future events, to measurements already made, or to the unknown. It is also called **unmeasurable risk**.
- **Actuarial risk:** a phenomenon subject to uncertainty in one or more of **3 variables: occurrence, timing and severity**.
- **Contingency:** an **event that may or may not occur**. (It can also mean cash held for emergencies, or a condition that must be met for a contract to be binding.)
- **Contingent:** an economic benefit that depends on an event whose occurrence or timing is not certain.
- **Hazard:** a condition that **increases the probability or expected size** of a loss.
- **Peril:** the **probable cause** (fire, flood, earthquake, theft) that exposes a person or property to loss, and against which insurance is bought.
- **Risk factor:** any attribute, characteristic or exposure that increases the likelihood of a future loss.
- **Mortality:** the incidence of **death** in a population, often measured by the probability that a randomly selected person **dies** within a period.
- **Morbidity:** the rate of **disease** in a population, often measured by the probability that a randomly selected person **becomes seriously ill** within a period.

**Explain more**

- **Risk versus uncertainty:** under risk, the chances of outcomes **can be measured**; under uncertainty they **cannot**.
- **Risk in context:** heavy rain threatens a Benue farmer's crops but is no concern to a Lagos software company.
- **Which variable is uncertain?**
  - Whole life policy paying ₦2,000,000 whenever Mr Okonkwo dies: death is certain and the amount is fixed, so only the **timing** is uncertain.
  - Motor cover in Abuja: the insurer does not know whether the car will crash (**occurrence**) or how much repairs would cost (**severity**).
  - "Ownership" is **not** an actuarial risk variable.
- A policy paying ₦1,000,000 only if Mrs Adebayo dies within 10 years is **contingent**: it depends on an uncertain event. A fixed amount does not make it certain.
- **Trap:** fire is the **peril**; the faulty wiring in a Kano warehouse is the **hazard**. Do not swap them.
- **Trap:** mortality is death; morbidity is illness.

### Lesson 1.3: Probability, expected value, pooling and insurance

**Must know**

- **Probability** measures the likelihood that an event occurs. It always lies **from 0 to 1** inclusive (0 = impossible, 1 = certain). A value such as 1.2 or a negative value can **never** be a probability.
- **Expected value:** the **probability-weighted average** of the values taken by a random variable.
  - E(X) = x\_1 × p\_1 + x\_2 × p\_2 + ... + x\_k × p\_k
  - Expected claim cost per policy = probability of a claim × average claim amount
  - Total expected claims = number of policies × expected claim cost per policy
- **Law of large numbers:** as the number of independent, similar trials increases, the observed proportion of an event becomes **more stable**. Larger groups give more certain predictions.
- **Statistical regularity:** random events show regular patterns when repeated enough times, or when there are enough similar events. It is an umbrella term covering the law of large numbers.
- **Pooling:** forming a group of policies with **similar characteristics**, so that an insurer can manage individual risks by **reducing the variability (variance)** of total outcomes. A good pool is (1) **large**, (2) **similar (homogeneous)**, (3) with losses **reasonably independent**.
- **Risk aversion:** preferring a **small certain loss** (the premium) to a **large uncertain one**. Because risk-averse people will pay **above their expected loss**, the insurer can **cover its expenses and make a profit**.
- **Insurable risk** has:
  1. A potential loss
  2. that is significant and important;
  3. a loss and economic value that are well defined and **out of the policyholder's complete control**;
  4. covered losses that are reasonably independent.
  
  It must also not be so large that no insurer could pay for it.
- **Financial security system:** an arrangement in which one party assumes the obligation to provide benefits that offset undesirable economic consequences suffered by a second party, in return for payments called **considerations**. Examples: insurance products and retirement plans.
- **Experience:** the data obtained from the actual operation of an insurance or pension scheme.

**Explain more**

- **Worked example 1 (expected loss).** An Onitsha shop has a 0.4% chance of a fire in a year, with a loss of ₦5,000,000 if it happens, and nil otherwise.
  - Step 1: 0.4% = 0.004.
  - Step 2: E(loss) = 0.004 × ₦5,000,000 + 0.996 × ₦0 = **₦20,000**.
- **Worked example 2 (expected claim per policy).** An insurer covers 10,000 buses; 3% have an accident each year; the average claim is ₦400,000.
  - Per bus: 0.03 × ₦400,000 = **₦12,000**.
  - Whole portfolio: 10,000 × ₦12,000 = ₦120,000,000. "Per bus" wants ₦12,000.
- **Risk aversion in figures.** An Aba trader faces a 0.5% chance of losing ₦3,000,000. Her expected loss is 0.005 × ₦3,000,000 = ₦15,000, yet she gladly pays a ₦30,000 premium. The extra ₦15,000 is what lets the insurer cover expenses and earn a profit.
- **Law of large numbers in practice.** Insurer B with 50,000 similar okada riders predicts total claims more reliably than Insurer A with 50, because larger similar groups give more stable proportions.
- **Trap:** a pool whose members are "as different as possible" is **not** desirable. **Trap:** a loss fully under the policyholder's control is **not** insurable.

### Lesson 1.4: What actuaries do and how they work

**Must know**

- **The actuary** identifies, analyses and documents risks; monitors risk areas; defines problems and generates ideas; develops recommendations that lead to implemented solutions; and **continuously monitors results**.
- An actuary's work is based on **theory, data and experience** and is performed with **professional judgement**, which is needed because tasks affect **several stakeholders with different, sometimes conflicting, needs**.
- **What actuaries do:**
  1. **Evaluate the likelihood of future events:** forecast the impact of catastrophes; calculate the expected lifetime of a retiree receiving a pension.
  2. **Design ways to reduce the likelihood of undesirable events:** life insurance policies, annuities; promote safety (seat belts).
  3. **Decrease the impact of undesirable events that occur:** death benefits, retirement benefits, indemnity benefits.
  4. **Participate in corporate planning:** mergers and acquisitions; evaluating investment programmes.
  5. **Help insurance companies:** calculate rates for new products; forecast and interpret underwriting results; maintain databases for analysing pricing issues.
- **Further value:** products meeting customers' needs **now and in the future**; expertise in **data quality and analytics**; **predictive modelling and optimisation** for smarter risk decisions in digital businesses; supporting **marketing** (modelling demand for a new product), **accounting** (models and assumptions for **budgeting and forecasting**), **product development**, and **identifying and managing risk**.
- **Key terms:**
  - **Actuarial model:** a **simplified representation** of real-world relationships, used to explain a system, derive estimates and guide decisions.
  - **3 components:** **input** (delivers assumptions, parameters and data), **processing** (transforms inputs into estimates), **output** (translates estimates into useful business information).
  - **Life cycle, 3 phases:** **specification**, **implementation**, **production** (one or more model runs).
  - **Actuarial assumption:** an estimate of an uncertain variable fed into a model, normally to calculate premiums or benefits.
  - **Deterministic model:** one fixed set of assumptions, events assumed to happen with certainty, giving a single result.
  - **Stochastic model:** estimates **probability distributions** of outcomes by letting one or more inputs vary randomly.
  - **Best estimate:** neither optimistic nor conservative.
  - **Scenario:** a set of assumptions about future conditions, based on professional judgement or prescribed by law.
  - **Actuarial control cycle:** **define the problem → design the solution → monitor the results**.
  - **Pricing:** developing premiums to cover losses from underwritten risks and future benefits, using probabilities of loss.
  - **Reserve:** a liability set aside to pay **future claims**.
  - In short, actuaries **analyse past data** to work out how much money should be **set aside to cover financial losses that could occur in the future**.
  - **Valuation:** placing a value on an insurer's assets and/or liabilities.
  - **Risk management:** identifying and measuring risk, then developing strategies to minimise, monitor and control it. Handle first the risks with the **greatest loss and greatest probability**.
  - **Risk transfer:** shifting risk to another party (for example, paying an insurer a premium). **Risk retention:** keeping the risk yourself (self-insurance, deductibles).

**Explain more**

- **Matching tasks to categories:**
  - Seat-belt campaign by a Lagos motor insurer's actuary: **reducing the likelihood** of undesirable events.
  - Monthly pension to a retired Ibadan civil servant, death benefit to a family: **decreasing the impact** of events that occur.
  - Estimating how long a pensioner will draw a pension, forecasting flood costs: **evaluating the likelihood** of future events.
  - Assessing a bank merger and investment programmes: **corporate planning**.
  - Acting as the insurer's lawyer is **not** an actuarial task; nor is ignoring results after a solution is in place.
- **The Lagos car-accident method.** To predict accidents in Lagos this November, the actuary first looks at the **percentage of people involved in accidents in previous years**, identifies a **trend**, predicts this year's percentage, then sets how much each person should pay.
  - Worked example: 2.0%, 2.2%, 2.4% in the last three years. The rise is 0.2 percentage points a year, so this year = 2.4% + 0.2% = **2.6%**.
  - If the average claim is ₦500,000, the expected claim cost per driver = 0.026 × ₦500,000 = **₦13,000** (before expenses and profit).
- **Pricing versus reserving:** deciding what to charge new motor customers next year is **pricing**; estimating what to hold now for claims on policies already sold is **reserving**.
- **Deterministic versus stochastic:** one fixed 10% interest assumption giving one answer is deterministic; 10,000 random simulations giving a distribution of profits is stochastic.

### Lesson 1.5: Where actuaries work, skills, and how they differ from others

**Must know**

- **Typical areas of actuarial work:** insurance (general, health, life); finance (banking, corporate, investment, pensions); data science and analytics; technology; enterprise risk; resource and environment (climate change).
- **8 specialisations:**
  1. Health and Care
  2. Life Insurance
  3. Pensions
  4. Investment and Finance
  5. Financial Derivatives
  6. General Insurance: Reserving
  7. General Insurance: Pricing
  8. Enterprise Risk Management
- **Most common industries:**
  1. **Insurance:** property (home) and casualty (auto); life and annuities.
  2. **Employee benefits:** retirement benefits, health benefits, social security.
  3. **Financial services:** banks, investing, risk management, mergers and acquisitions.
- Actuaries are largely employed by **insurance companies and consulting firms**, in **4 areas**: life, annuities and mutual funds; health; pensions; property and casualty.
- **Skills needed:** mathematical and analytical skills; project management and problem-solving skills; excellent **communication** skills (oral and written); strong business sense and commercial awareness; **self-motivation**; ambition and ability to work in a team. Also: business acumen, computer skills, work ethic, creativity, independence, collaboration.
- Besides mathematics, **self-motivation and good communication skills** are equally important.

| | Actuary | Accountant | CFA |
|---|---|---|---|
| Business focus | Financial impact of events that **may or may not occur in the future** | Financial impact of events that **have already happened** | Analyse financial reports and investments |
| How | Uses large amounts of data to calculate the probability and impact of future events and what to do | Works with **known** numbers; creates or audits balance sheets and income statements | Uses analysis to recommend investments |
| Assets and liabilities | **Both**, specially trained in complicated **liabilities** | Reports on both | Geared towards **assets** |
| Product focus | Understands products and how they make money | Reports cost, revenue and profit of a product | Which products or companies to invest in |

- **Actuary vs statistician:** a statistician collects, analyses and interprets data in any field. An actuary uses statistics and probability **together with financial mathematics and business knowledge** to put a **money value** on future risk, mainly in insurance, pensions and finance.

**Explain more**

- **Life:** term and permanent death-benefit products. **Annuities:** a lump sum or payments now for periodic payments starting immediately or later. **Mutual fund:** pools investors' assets to buy securities for a common goal.
- **Health:** products for individuals, families and sole proprietors, and plans for employers by size: small group 2 to 50 employees, middle market 51 to 3,000, national accounts 3,001 or more.
- **Pensions** give steady income, usually a guaranteed annuity, to retired or disabled employees. **Defined benefit:** based on pay, years of service, retirement age and other factors. **Defined contribution:** the employee defers part of salary into the plan and **bears the investment risk**. Nigeria's Contributory Pension Scheme, regulated by **PenCom**, is a defined contribution scheme.
- **Property and casualty:** business, personal (auto, homeowners), and financial, professional and international insurance.
- **Typical projects:** pricing life products (impact of AIDS on mortality tables); costing motor or home cover in an area (impact of global warming); setting HMO premium rates; pricing increased retirement benefits. In Nigeria, insurers are supervised by **NAICOM**.
- **Why self-motivation:** trainees must **pass professional exams while working full time**.
- **Communication in action:** explaining to a non-mathematical board why premiums must rise.
- **Applying the table:** setting aside money today for 30 years of pension promises that depend on how long workers live is an **actuary's** job. All three professions are prestigious and should work together.

### One-minute recap

- Actuarial science evaluates risks and maintains the economic stability of insurance and financial organisations.
- An actuary applies mathematical approaches to anticipate, measure and manage risk; Latin actuarius.
- Risk = possibility of failing to meet objectives, defined in context; uncertainty = unmeasurable risk.
- Actuarial risk variables: occurrence, timing, severity. Contingency = an event that may or may not occur.
- Probability is from 0 to 1; expected claim = probability × average claim; large, similar, independent pools reduce variability.
- Model: input, processing, output; phases specification, implementation, production; control cycle: define, design, monitor.
- Pricing sets future premiums; reserving holds money for claims on policies already sold.
- Actuaries look forward and specialise in liabilities; accountants look back; CFAs focus on assets.

### Theory practice

**Question:** An insurer covers 20,000 similar okada riders in Lagos. Past experience shows 1.5% of riders make a claim each year, with an average claim of ₦250,000. Ignoring expenses and profit, find (a) the expected claim cost per rider and (b) the total expected claims. (c) Why is this prediction reliable?

*Solution:*
- (a) 1.5% = 0.015. Expected cost per rider = 0.015 × ₦250,000 = **₦3,750**.
- (b) Total = 20,000 × ₦3,750 = **₦75,000,000**.
- (c) By the law of large numbers, a large pool of similar, independent risks gives a stable claim proportion, so total claims are predictable.
