## Topic 14: Liability and Asset Portfolio Management 'Problem' of Banks

**What you must know:** the three goals (liquidity, profitability, solvency) and why they conflict; reasons for liquidity and for profitability; priorities in allocating funds; the four liquidity theories; asset, liability and asset-liability management and ALCO; liquidity ratio, cash reserve ratio, excess reserves and loans-to-deposits computations; maturity mismatch and gap analysis; capital adequacy, the Basel accords and CAMEL.

### Lesson 14.1: The three goals and the 'problem'

**Must know**

- **3 major goals of a deposit money bank (DMB):** **liquidity, profitability and solvency** (not longevity or endurability).
- **Liquidity:** the conversion of current assets into cash within a short period of time without appreciable loss of value. In banking: the ability of a bank to meet its customers' demands for cash and its other obligations as they fall due (to pay depositors on demand).
- **Profitability:** earning a reasonable margin of income (interest on loans and investments) over costs (interest on deposits and other expenses).
- **Solvency:** the ability to meet all obligations in the long run, with assets exceeding liabilities. To be solvent a bank must be both liquid and profitable.
- **The 'problem':** balancing the competing goals of liquidity, profitability and solvency. Loans earn interest but tie up cash; idle cash keeps the bank liquid but earns no income. **The more liquid a bank is, the less profitable it tends to be.**
- **Cash** (notes and coins) is the **most liquid asset** but earns **no income**.

**4 reasons a bank keeps adequate liquidity:**

1. To meet the cash demands (withdrawals) of customers; current account holders need give no notice.
2. To maintain the unshaken confidence of the public.
3. To meet the statutory liquidity requirements of the CBN (liquidity ratio, cash ratio).
4. To meet cash expenses, such as petty cash expenses.

**6 reasons a bank pursues profitability:**

1. To satisfy the interest of shareholders (dividends).
2. Evidence of strength and progress (radiates public confidence).
3. To meet the obligations of lenders and creditors.
4. Index of performance and stability.
5. Source of capital: retained (ploughed-back) profit strengthens the bank.
6. Buffer for unexpected losses.

**Explain more**

- Paying dividends is a reason for **profitability**, not liquidity; maximising interest by lending out as much as possible is the opposite of a liquidity reason. Exams swap these.
- The main cost of holding more cash than needed is the **opportunity cost**: the interest or investment income the bank could have earned (from other banks or government securities). There is no CBN penalty for excess cash.
- **Solvent but illiquid:** assets comfortably exceed liabilities, but they are mostly long-term loans, so the bank cannot pay all the depositors who come today. Illiquidity can trigger a run that grounds even a solvent bank. **Insolvent:** liabilities exceed assets.
- Nigerians have a high propensity to hold cash, so a Nigerian bank cannot neglect liquidity whatever the cost.

### Lesson 14.2: The balance sheet and the priorities for funds

**Must know**

- **Assets** (from most to least liquid): cash; balances with the CBN and other banks; money at call and short notice; Treasury bills and other government securities; loans and advances; long-term investments; premises and equipment.
- **Liabilities:** capital (shareholders' funds: share capital and reserves); deposits (current, savings, fixed); borrowings from the CBN and other banks; balances held for other banks.
- **Loans and advances:** the bank's **risk assets**; they generate most of its revenue and earn more than government securities.

**Traditional priorities in allocating a commercial bank's funds** (in order):

1. **Primary reserves** (first claim): cash in vault, balances with the CBN (including the cash reserve requirement), balances with other banks, cheques in course of collection. For liquidity, not income.
2. **Secondary reserves:** short-term, marketable, interest-earning assets, mainly **Treasury bills**, Treasury certificates and call money. Some income, quickly turned into cash.
3. **Loans and advances** to customers: the main source of profit.
4. **Investments for income:** longer-term securities bought with what is left.

**Explain more**

- Moving ₦20 billion from Treasury bills into three-year corporate loans makes a bank **more profitable but less liquid and more exposed to credit risk**. Moving the other way makes it more liquid and less profitable. The rule: higher return comes with lower liquidity and higher risk.
- Land and buildings and long-term mortgages are **not** secondary reserves; vault cash is a **primary** reserve.

### Lesson 14.3: Liquidity theories and management approaches

**Must know**

| Theory | Key idea | Weakness |
|---|---|---|
| **Commercial loan (real bills) theory** | Grant only short-term, self-liquidating loans that finance goods in production or trade and are repaid when the goods are sold. Linked to Adam Smith (1776). | Ignores that a stable core of deposits can safely support some longer-term lending; goods may not sell in a slump. |
| **Shiftability theory** (H. G. Moulton, 1918) | Stay liquid by holding assets that can be sold or shifted to other investors or to the central bank before maturity without significant loss (e.g. Treasury bills). | In a general crisis all banks sell at once, so assets may not be shiftable without heavy loss. |
| **Anticipated income theory** (H. V. Prochnow) | A loan is liquid if it is repaid from the borrower's expected future income, usually in regular instalments. | Depends on the borrower's income actually arriving. |
| **Liability management theory** (1960s) | Liquidity comes from the **liability side**: borrow (interbank, certificates of deposit, central bank) when cash is needed. | Borrowing costs rise and funds may dry up in a crisis. |

- **Asset management:** treats deposits and other liabilities as largely given and concentrates on choosing the mix of cash, securities and loans to meet liquidity and profit goals.
- **Liability management:** actively raising funds (interbank borrowing, certificates of deposit) to meet loan demand or withdrawals instead of selling assets.
- **Asset-liability management (ALM):** coordinated management of both sides of the balance sheet so that the mix, maturity and pricing of assets and liabilities together achieve liquidity, profitability and risk targets.
- **ALCO (Asset and Liability Committee):** a committee of senior management (executive directors) that sets and monitors balance sheet strategy: liquidity, maturity profile, interest rate gaps and the pricing of loans and deposits. Not a CBN or NDIC body, not a shareholders' committee.

**Sources of liquidity at short notice:**

- **Asset side:** sell or rediscount Treasury bills; let maturing Treasury bills run off; collect maturing loans.
- **Liability side:** borrow in the interbank market; issue certificates of deposit; borrow from the CBN through its Standing Lending Facility; attract new deposits.
- Granting a new five-year loan **uses** liquidity; it is never a source.

**Explain more**

- A Balogun Market textile trader borrowing ₦3 million for 90 days to buy stock, repaid when the fabric is sold, is the classic **commercial loan** case.
- A civil servant's three-year car loan repaid by monthly salary deductions is the **anticipated income** case.
- A bank that borrows ₦15 billion overnight in the interbank market and issues certificates of deposit, instead of selling its investments, is practising **liability management**.
- A monthly meeting of executive directors that reviews the deposit maturity profile, sets fixed deposit rates and decides to cut the negative gap is the **ALCO**, not the audit, credit or remuneration committee.

### Lesson 14.4: Regulatory liquidity ratios and computations

**Must know**

- **Liquidity ratio:** the minimum percentage of deposit liabilities a DMB must hold in specified liquid assets (cash, balances with the CBN, Treasury bills, net interbank placements). Current minimum for DMBs: **30%**.
- **Cash reserve ratio (CRR):** the percentage of deposits a bank is legally required to keep as cash reserves with the CBN. Since September 2025 (retained at the September 2026 MPC): **45%** for DMBs, 16% for merchant banks, 75% on non-TSA public sector deposits.
- **Loans-to-deposits ratio (LDR):** CBN minimum **50%** since April 2024 (was 65%).
- **Formulas:**
  - Liquidity ratio = Liquid assets ÷ Deposit liabilities × 100
  - Required liquid assets = Required ratio × Deposits; shortfall = Required − Actual
  - Liquid assets switchable to loans = Actual liquid assets − Required liquid assets
  - Required reserves = Reserve ratio × Deposits
  - Excess reserves = Total reserves − Required reserves
  - New loans one bank can safely grant from a fresh deposit = Deposit × (1 − Reserve ratio)
  - LDR = Loans and advances ÷ Total deposits × 100

**Explain more**

1. **Liquidity ratio.** Deposits ₦120bn; cash ₦8bn, Treasury bills ₦25bn, net interbank placements ₦5bn. Liquid assets = 8 + 25 + 5 = ₦38bn. Ratio = 38 ÷ 120 × 100 = 31.67% (2 d.p.).
2. **Switchable surplus.** Required = 30% × 120 = ₦36bn. Surplus = 38 − 36 = ₦2bn can go into new loans.
3. **Shortfall.** Same bank with liquid assets of only ₦30bn: shortfall = 36 − 30 = ₦6bn.
4. **CRR.** Assume a 45% CRR on eligible deposits of ₦60bn: reserves = 0.45 × 60 = ₦27bn.
5. **Excess reserves.** Deposits ₦800m, reserves ₦200m, ratio 20%. Required = 0.20 × 800 = ₦160m. Excess = 200 − 160 = ₦40m.
6. **Single-bank lending.** Fresh deposit ₦20m, ratio 20%, no prior excess: keep 0.20 × 20 = ₦4m; lend 20 − 4 = ₦16m. Do not multiply by 1 ÷ 0.20; that is the whole system's money multiplier, not one bank.
7. **Reserve deficiency after a withdrawal.** Deposits ₦500m, reserves exactly ₦50m (10%). A ₦30m cash withdrawal cuts both: deposits = ₦470m, reserves = ₦20m. Required = 0.10 × 470 = ₦47m. Deficiency = 47 − 20 = ₦27m. Required reserves fall only by 10% of the withdrawal, so a deficiency appears.
8. **LDR.** Loans ₦330bn, deposits ₦500bn: 330 ÷ 500 × 100 = 66%. Do not divide deposits by loans.

### Lesson 14.5: Maturity mismatch and gap analysis

**Must know**

- **Maturity mismatch:** financing long-term loans with deposits that can be withdrawn on demand or at short notice. Creates liquidity risk and interest rate risk.
- **Rate-sensitive assets (RSA) and liabilities (RSL):** those that reprice within a given period (e.g. 12 months).
- **Repricing (funding) gap** = RSA − RSL.
  - **Positive gap** (RSA > RSL): **asset-sensitive**; net interest income (NII) **rises when rates rise**, falls when rates fall.
  - **Negative gap** (RSL > RSA): **liability-sensitive**; NII falls when rates rise, rises when rates fall.
- **Change in NII = Gap × Change in interest rate.**
- Expect rates to fall: a **negative** gap benefits most. Expect rates to rise: a **positive** gap.

**Explain more**

- **Positive gap.** RSA ₦450bn, RSL ₦400bn: gap = 450 − 400 = +₦50bn (asset-sensitive). Rates fall 3 percentage points: ΔNII = 50bn × (−0.03) = −₦1.5bn.
- **Negative gap.** RSA ₦200bn, RSL ₦260bn: gap = 200 − 260 = −₦60bn (liability-sensitive). Rates rise 2 points: ΔNII = −60bn × 0.02 = −₦1.2bn.
- Convert percentage points to decimals (2 points = 0.02). ₦50bn × 0.02 = ₦1bn, not ₦100m or ₦10bn.
- **Mismatch trap:** 10-year fixed-rate mortgages funded by 90-day fixed deposits. When rates rise sharply, deposit costs reprice upward quickly while mortgage income stays fixed, so the bank suffers.

### Lesson 14.6: Capital adequacy, Basel and CAMEL

**Must know**

- **Capital adequacy ratio (CAR)** = Capital ÷ Risk-weighted assets × 100. RWA = sum of (each asset × its risk weight).
- **Basel Committee on Banking Supervision (BCBS):** set up by the G10 central banks in Basel, Switzerland, after banking crises of the 1970s; works under the **Bank for International Settlements (BIS)**.
- **Basel I (July 1988):** capital of at least **8% of risk-weighted assets**; credit risk only. Risk weights: **0%** claims on government; **20%** claims on banks; **50%** residential mortgages; **100%** corporate and consumer loans.
- **Basel II (2004)**, 3 pillars:
  1. Minimum capital requirements (credit, market and operational risk).
  2. Supervisory review process.
  3. Market discipline through effective public disclosure.
  Deposit insurance is **not** a pillar.
- **Basel III (December 2010, finalised 2017):** stronger common equity (4.5% of RWA), 2.5% capital conservation buffer, countercyclical buffer, leverage ratio, and two liquidity ratios: **Liquidity Coverage Ratio (LCR):** enough high-quality liquid assets to cover net cash outflows over a **30-day stress period**; **Net Stable Funding Ratio (NSFR):** stable long-term funding for the whole balance sheet.
- **CAMEL** (rating used by CBN and NDIC): **C**apital adequacy, **A**sset quality, **M**anagement, **E**arnings, **L**iquidity. (CAMELS adds **S**ensitivity to market risk.)
- Nigerian CAR minimums: **15%** for banks with international authorisation, **10%** for national and regional banks.

**Explain more**

- **CAR worked.** Capital ₦18bn. Government securities ₦50bn (0%), interbank claims ₦30bn (20%), residential mortgages ₦40bn (50%), corporate loans ₦100bn (100%).
  - RWA = 0 + (30 × 0.2) + (40 × 0.5) + (100 × 1) = 0 + 6 + 20 + 100 = ₦126bn.
  - CAR = 18 ÷ 126 × 100 = 14.29% (2 d.p.).
  - Against 15%: required capital = 0.15 × 126 = ₦18.9bn; shortfall = ₦0.9bn.
- A bank at exactly its minimum CAR that wants many more corporate loans must **raise more capital or retain more profit** first. More deposits do not help (they are not capital), and cutting zero-weight government securities does not reduce RWA.
- Basel I's weakness: one measure (credit risk capital) using book values, ignoring operational risk; Basel II followed, then the 2007-2008 crisis led to Basel III.
- Bank recapitalisation (₦500bn international, ₦200bn national, ₦50bn regional; deadline 31 March 2026) is about minimum capital, a separate rule from the CAR ratio.

### One-minute recap

- Three goals: liquidity, profitability, solvency; balancing them is the portfolio 'problem'.
- More liquid means less profitable; cash is the most liquid asset and earns nothing.
- 4 liquidity reasons (withdrawals, confidence, CBN rules, cash expenses); 6 profitability reasons (dividends, strength, creditors, performance, capital, buffer).
- Fund priorities: primary reserves, secondary reserves (Treasury bills), loans and advances, investments for income.
- Theories: commercial loan (self-liquidating), shiftability (sell before maturity), anticipated income (instalments), liability management (borrow).
- ALCO sets balance sheet strategy; ALM manages both sides together.
- Liquidity ratio 30%; CRR 45% for DMBs; LDR 50%; excess reserves = actual − required.
- Gap = RSA − RSL; ΔNII = gap × Δrate; positive gap gains when rates rise.
- CAR = capital ÷ RWA; Basel I 8% (1988); Basel II three pillars; Basel III LCR (30 days) and NSFR; under BIS.
- CAMEL: capital, asset quality, management, earnings, liquidity.

### Theory practice

**Question:** Agege Bank has deposit liabilities of ₦300bn and qualifying liquid assets of ₦100bn. The minimum liquidity ratio is 30%. (a) What is its liquidity ratio? (b) What is the largest amount of liquid assets it can switch into loans and still comply? (c) What happens to its liquidity and profitability after the switch?

*Answer:*
- (a) 100 ÷ 300 × 100 = 33.33% (2 d.p.).
- (b) Required = 0.30 × 300 = ₦90bn. Switchable = 100 − 90 = ₦10bn.
- (c) Its liquidity ratio falls to exactly 30% (less liquid), but the ₦10bn now earns loan interest, so it becomes more profitable, with more credit risk.
