## Topic 2: The Development of Money

**What you must know:** barter, its features, advantages and limitations; definitions of money and the schools of thought; the functions and characteristics of money; the types of money and how money evolved from barter to the eNaira; the money stock (M1, M2, M3), near money and the value of money; how banks create credit and what limits it.

### Lesson 2.1: Barter and why money came

**Must know**

- **Barter (trade by barter):** the direct exchange of goods and services for other goods and services without the use of money. In short, exchange of commodity for commodity (goods for goods).
- **Barter economy:** a simple economic system where production is limited, so surplus output is limited, and trade takes place only by exchanging commodities for commodities. It is an economy where money is not in use. Barter existed before the money economy.
- **3 basic features of a barter economy:** (1) a producer produces almost all his needs himself; (2) the level of surplus is limited; (3) a producer directly exchanges his products for other people's products.
- **Barter vs money economy:** barter = goods exchanged directly for goods; money economy = goods and services exchanged for money.

**Limitations of barter:**

1. **Double coincidence of wants** (the main limitation): each party must have what the other wants **and** want what the other has, at the same time.
2. **Lack of a common measure of value** (no unit of account): too many relative prices to know; the problem of fixing exchange rates between goods.
3. **Indivisibility** of goods (a cow cannot be split for a few bags of salt).
4. **Difficulty of storing value** (no means of storing wealth; goods perish).
5. **Difficulty of deferred payment** (no standard for debts and credit).
6. **Transport difficulties** (bulky goods carried long distances in search of a partner).
7. **Lack of specialisation** (everyone a "jack of all trades, master of none").
8. **Waste of time and cost of information** (failure to expedite transactions).
9. **Waiting cost and storage cost:** stockpiling output while waiting for a partner creates two fresh costs: the **waiting (opportunity) cost** of satisfaction forgone, and the **storage cost**.

- **Relative prices under barter:** with n goods, number of relative prices = n(n − 1) / 2. A money economy needs only one money price per good.
- **Two major advantages of a money economy over barter:** money is a good **store of value** (barter goods are often perishable), and money is **divisible** into small units, so small purchases are possible.

**Advantages of barter (5):** simplicity and directness; no need for money; flexibility in valuing goods; **freedom from inflation, currency devaluation and over-issue of money**; promotion of self-sufficiency.

**Explain more**

- Barter is traced to about 6000 BC in Mesopotamia (modern Iraq).
- Trap: "direct exchange of goods for goods" is what barter **is**, not a drawback; "inflation from over-issue of currency" is a problem of money, not of barter.
- Double coincidence example: a yam farmer wants cloth, but the only weaver wants fish, not yams. No trade. Indivisibility example: a cattle owner wants salt worth far less than one cow.
- Worked example (relative prices): with 1,000 goods, 1,000 × 999 / 2 = 499,500, about **500,000** relative prices. With 50 goods: 50 × 49 / 2 = 1,225.
- **Modern barter** survives as **countertrade** (two countries swap, say, crude oil for machinery with no money changing hands), barter exchanges, and in crises such as Zimbabwe's hyperinflation.
- Money is a **social invention that evolved gradually** because barter's shortcomings made a **generally acceptable** medium of exchange necessary. It was not created by one decree, and it came long before commercial banks.

### Lesson 2.2: Meaning and definitions of money

**Must know**

- **Money (general definition):** anything that is generally acceptable as a means of payment for goods and services and in settlement of debts. Put simply, money is a **medium of exchange**.
- **Coulborn:** "a means of valuation and of payment, as both the unit of account and the generally acceptable medium of exchange".
- **Pierce and Shaw (1974):**
    - **Abstract definition:** money is a **unit of account**, the common denominator in which the exchange value of all goods and services is expressed.
    - **Tangible (concrete) definition:** money is a medium of exchange whose ownership is capable of changing hands.
- **3 modern classifications:** (1) general, (2) legal, (3) functional.

**3 schools of thought under the general definition:**

| School | View |
|---|---|
| Currency school | Money = legal tender currency (naira and kobo, pounds, dollars) |
| Banking school | Agrees, but says cheques must count: "cheque is money and money is cheque", since most bank transactions use cheques |
| Monetarist (Chicago) view, **Milton Friedman** | Money is **anything that serves as a temporary abode of purchasing power** (includes cheques, drafts, promissory notes, bills of exchange) |

- **Legal definition:** money is what the law says it is.
- **Functional definition:** money is measured by aggregates **M1, M2, M3** set by each central bank (Lesson 2.5).
- **Hicks (1967):** "money is defined by its functions; anything is money which is used as money; **money is what money does**."
- **Numismatics:** the study of money, especially coins and paper currency.

**Explain more**

- A law declaring something legal tender does not on its own make it money; people must actually accept it.
- Without money, the **price mechanism** and the **resource allocation system** would both be inoperative: in a market economy, money prices signal where resources should move and who gets what.
- Money promotes **specialisation and division of labour**, removes the need for a double coincidence of wants, and encourages **savings and investment**. It does the opposite of forcing each household to produce all it needs.

### Lesson 2.3: Functions and characteristics of money

**Must know**

**Functions of money** (Jevons, 1875, gave four; a fifth is often added):

1. **Medium of exchange:** the intermediary in trade; removes the double coincidence of wants. The most fundamental function.
2. **Unit of account (measure of value, standard of value, common denominator of value):** all prices expressed in one unit (the naira), so for each good you know how many units of money it costs, as the metre measures distance.
3. **Store of value (store of wealth):** holding purchasing power for future use, e.g. saving for old age or a "rainy day".
4. **Standard of deferred payment:** debts contracted now and settled in money later (loans, bonds, credit sales).
5. **Transfer of value:** moving value from person to person and place to place.

- **Primary functions:** medium of exchange and measure of value (unit of account). **Secondary:** store of value and standard of deferred payment.
- **Not functions:** settling disputes, creating employment, earning interest, keeping prices constant at all times.

**Characteristics (qualities) of good money (8):**

| Quality | Meaning |
|---|---|
| General acceptability | Accepted by all, by custom or by law. **The most important**; the one quality near money lacks |
| Scarcity | Relatively scarce, not as abundant as sand, but not too rare |
| Durability | Lasts through repeated use; needed for store of value |
| Homogeneity (uniformity) | Every unit identical: ₦1 in Abuja = ₦1 in Ibadan |
| Divisibility | Divisible into small units so transactions of any size can be made (₦1 = 100 kobo) |
| Portability | Easy to carry about |
| Recognisability (cognisability) | Recognised as genuine at once, without technical aid |
| Stability | Stable in value over time |

**Explain more**

- Match the example to the function. Prices of goods quoted in naira = **unit of account**. Repaying a loan in fixed naira over five years, borrowing ₦500,000 now to repay ₦550,000 in two years, buying now and paying in three months, or a bond paying naira interest and principal at set dates = **standard of deferred payment**. Saving for old age = **store of value**. Emeka in Lagos sending ₦30,000 monthly to his parents in Kano = **transfer of value**.
- To serve as a **store of value and standard of deferred payment**, money must be **stable in value**. Money is a poor store of value during **inflation**. **Inflation** = a persistent rise in the general price level over a period; **deflation** = a persistent fall; **stagflation** = inflation with stagnant output and high unemployment.
- "Measure of value" is a function, not a quality. "Durable money" is not a type of money. "Abundance" is not a quality (money must be scarce).
- Money with legal tender status is **legal money**; money accepted only by custom is **customary money**.

### Lesson 2.4: Types of money and how money evolved

**Must know**

**Order of evolution:** barter → commodity money → metallic money (coins) → paper money → bank deposit money → electronic and digital money.

- **Commodity money:** has intrinsic value of its own apart from its use as money (cowries, salt, cattle, grain, gold, tobacco). It may be used **without a distinguishing mark** or **with a design or symbol** (minted coins). Commodity money has largely gone out of use.
- **Standard (full-bodied) money:** money whose intrinsic (metal) value **equals** its face value.
- **Token money:** has no commodity value; its value comes entirely from being generally acceptable (paper money, bank deposits). Includes:
    - **Representative (convertible) token money:** paper money convertible into a commodity such as gold **on demand**.
    - **Inconvertible token money (fiduciary issue):** not backed by gold or any commodity; its supply is limited only by **law**.
- **Fiduciary issue:** bank notes not backed by gold but by government securities. **Fiduciary money** is accepted because of **trust** in the issuer.
- **Fiat money:** money declared **legal tender** by government decree; no intrinsic value, not backed by a commodity. "Fiat" is Latin for **"let it be done"**. Today's naira is fiat money.
- **Legal tender:** money that the law says must be accepted in settlement of debts. In Nigeria: **naira notes and coins issued by the CBN**. Cheques, postal orders and treasury bills are not legal tender.
- **Bank deposit money:** deposits withdrawable by cheque or transfer. A cheque is not money itself; it transfers the deposit, which is money.

**Limitations of commodity money (5):** perishability; **variability in quality** (two cows of one breed, one fat, one thin); transport difficulty; **supply fluctuations** (a bumper grain harvest crashes grain's value); difficulty in making small transactions.

**Why gold and silver were preferred (7):** durability (no rust or decay), uniformity, divisibility, portability (high value for weight), scarcity, intrinsic value, universal acceptability.

**Explain more**

- **Lydia** (Asia Minor, modern Turkey) minted the first standardised coins about 600 BC from **electrum**, a natural alloy of gold and silver. Before coins, Rome used *aes rude* (rough bronze), then *aes signatum* (marked bars).
- **Debasement:** a ruler cuts the gold or silver content of coins (e.g. mixing in copper) but keeps the face value, earning a profit called **seigniorage**. Results: inflation, loss of public trust, rejection of debased coins for full-value or foreign coins. It **lowers**, never raises, each coin's purchasing power.
- Worked example: a ruler holds 60 kg of silver. Full coins hold 25 g. 60 kg = 60,000 g. Full coins: 60,000 / 25 = 2,400. Debased to 20 g: 60,000 / 20 = 3,000. Extra coins = 3,000 − 2,400 = **600**. (Same method: 120 kg at 20 g gives 6,000 coins; at 15 g gives 8,000; so 2,000 more.)
- **Paper money:** began in China (Tang dynasty, about 807 AD; Song dynasty *jiaozi*). Europe's first banknotes: Stockholms Banco, 1661; Bank of England founded 1694. Early problems: **counterfeiting** (answered with watermarks, intricate designs, special paper, several signatures), trust, overissue, wear.
- **Goldsmiths (17th-century England):** their receipts for gold deposited circulated as **goldsmiths' notes**, forerunners of bank notes. Since depositors did not all collect at once, they issued notes worth more than the gold held: the start of **fractional reserve banking**. Early notes had to be **convertible** because people accepted them only believing they could get gold or silver on demand. The **Bank Charter Act 1844** let the Bank of England make a fiduciary issue of £14 million.
- **Gold standard:** currencies defined as a fixed weight of gold; notes exchangeable for gold; fixed exchange rates; money supply limited by gold reserves (no free expansion). The link ended in **1971** when the USA suspended convertibility of the dollar into gold.
- **Electronic money:** cards, online banking, mobile money such as **M-Pesa** in Kenya (promotes **financial inclusion** of the unbanked).
- **Cryptocurrency (e.g. Bitcoin, 2009):** decentralised digital currency on **blockchain**, not issued or controlled by any government or central bank; very volatile; **not legal tender**.
- **CBDC:** electronic central bank money for payments and storing value; legal tender. Nigeria's **eNaira** was launched on **25 October 2021**, the first CBDC in Africa (the world's first nationwide CBDC was the Bahamas' Sand Dollar, 2020). It has the **same value as the cash naira** (1:1), **complements, not replaces**, cash, is open to people without bank accounts, and pays **no interest**. Expected benefits: lower cost of printing and handling cash, financial inclusion, better monetary policy data, safer payments, cheaper cross-border trade. Uptake has been low; the CBN now positions it more as payments infrastructure.

| Point | CBDC (eNaira) | Cryptocurrency |
|---|---|---|
| Issuer | Central bank | Users / private network |
| Legal tender | Yes | No |
| Volatility | Low | High |
| Backing | State-backed by law | Not backed by law |

### Lesson 2.5: The money stock, near money and the value of money

**Must know**

- **Money supply (money stock, stock of money):** the total stock of money held by the public in an economy at a point in time: currency (notes and coins) held by the public **plus** bank deposits.
- **Demand (sight) deposits:** current account, withdrawable on demand by cheque. **Time deposits:** fixed period; **savings deposits:** withdrawal conditions; both earn interest.
- **Quasi-money (near money) in Nigeria's aggregates:** time deposits + savings deposits held by the public with banks.

**Formulas (Nigeria):**

- M1 (narrow money) = currency outside banks + demand deposits
- QM (quasi-money) = savings deposits + time deposits
- M2 (broad money) = M1 + QM = currency + demand + savings + time deposits
- M3 = M2 + deposits with non-bank financial institutions (NBFIs)

- **Near money (quasi-money):** financial assets that can be converted into cash quickly with little or no loss, but are **not generally accepted as a medium of exchange**. They store value but must first be converted before spending. Examples: **treasury bills**, treasury certificates, time and savings (fixed) deposits, bills of exchange, bonds, debentures, insurance policies, postal orders, stocks and shares.
- **Demand deposits on current account are money (part of M1), not near money.**

**Money vs near money (6 differences):**

| Basis | Money | Near money |
|---|---|---|
| Legality | Legal tender | No legal tender status |
| Liquidity | Spent directly | Must be converted first |
| Acceptability | Generally accepted | Not generally accepted |
| Yield | Cash earns no yield | Earns a yield |
| Conversion | None needed | Short delay and some cost |
| Safety | Cash can be lost or stolen | Safer, so often preferred |

- **Value of money:** its **purchasing power**, the quantity of goods and services a given amount of money can buy. It varies **inversely** with the general price level and is measured with a **price index** (index number method).
- Value of money = 1 / P. New value as % of old = (old price index / new price index) × 100.

**Explain more**

- **Worked example (M aggregates):** currency ₦600bn, demand deposits ₦1,400bn, savings and time ₦1,100bn. M1 = 600 + 1,400 = **₦2,000bn** (savings and time are left out of M1).
- **Worked example (table, ₦ billion):**

| Year | Currency | Demand | Savings | Time | M1 | QM | M2 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2007 | 500 | 1,000 | 780 | 680 | 1,500 | 1,460 | 2,960 |
| 2008 | 750 | 1,550 | 950 | 800 | 2,300 | 1,750 | 4,050 |
| 2009 | 1,000 | 2,000 | 1,450 | 1,250 | 3,000 | 2,700 | 5,700 |
| 2010 | 1,125 | 2,500 | 1,800 | 1,600 | 3,625 | 3,400 | 7,025 |
| 2011 | 1,125 | 2,500 | 1,800 | 1,600 | 3,625 | 3,400 | 7,025 |

  Check 2010 M2: 1,125 + 2,500 + 1,800 + 1,600 = ₦7,025bn.
- **Worked example (M1, M2, M3):** currency 80, demand 320, savings 300, time 50, NBFI deposits 400 (₦bn). M1 = 80 + 320 = 400. M2 = 400 + 300 + 50 = 750. M3 = 750 + 400 = **₦1,150bn**. Another year: currency 150, demand 400 gives M1 = **₦550bn**; currency 100, demand 300, savings 280, time 80 gives M2 = **₦760bn**.
- Friedman's M2 = currency + demand + time deposits; the broadest M3 is linked with **Gurley and Shaw**. M1 shows the medium of exchange role; M2 and M3 add store of value.
- Each component of the money stock is money **to the extent that it performs some of the functions of money**. Currency and demand deposits (M1) are the most liquid and most generally accepted, so they are "most qualified" to be called money (M1 is sometimes called high-powered money; strictly, high-powered money is currency plus banks' reserves with the central bank).
- A **falling share of cash** in the money stock shows more public confidence in banks **and** a more sophisticated financial system (in Nigeria's early-1990s bank distress the cash share rose).
- **Worked example (value of money, one index):** price index rises from 100 to 250. New value = 100 / 250 × 100 = **40%** of the old value (a 60% fall).
- **Worked example (weighted basket):** weights gari 0.3, yam 0.3, rice 0.4 (weights sum to 1). First-year prices ₦500, ₦800, ₦1,000: P1 = 0.3×500 + 0.3×800 + 0.4×1,000 = 150 + 240 + 400 = 790. Second-year prices ₦700, ₦1,000, ₦1,300: P2 = 210 + 300 + 520 = 1,030. Value = 790 / 1,030 × 100 = **76.70%** (to 2 d.p.) of its first-year value. This assumes the income shares stay fixed.
- **Velocity of money** = times a unit of money changes hands in a period (Fisher: **MV = PQ**). Demand for money: Cambridge **Md = kPY**; Keynes's three motives (transactions, precautionary, speculative); Friedman's modern quantity theory.

### Lesson 2.6: Credit creation and the supply of money

**Must know**

- **Credit creation:** banks create money (deposits) by granting loans and overdrafts from deposits, keeping only a fraction as reserves (fractional reserve banking).
- **Assumptions:** a primary cash deposit starts it; a legal fraction is kept as reserve; the rest (excess reserve) is lent; loans are redeposited in the same or another bank; no cash drain.
- **Formulas:**
    - Credit (money) multiplier k = 1 / r, where r = cash reserve ratio
    - Total deposits created = initial deposit / r = initial deposit × k
    - Credit creation multiplier = total deposits created / original deposit
    - Total loans = total deposits − initial deposit
- The **higher** the reserve ratio, the **lower** the multiplier and the credit created.

**Limitations to credit creation:**

1. Size of the legal **cash reserve ratio**.
2. Amount of the initial cash deposit (level of cash reserves).
3. **Leakages**: cash kept outside banks ("under the pillow").
4. Fear of **cash drain** to other banks.
5. Lack of acceptable **collateral security**.
6. Actions of the monetary authorities.

**Determinants of money supply:**

- **Exogenous** (set by the central bank): the **minimum cash reserve ratio**, the level of bank reserves (open market operations).
- **Endogenous** (from the economy): the public's desire to hold cash rather than deposits, business activity, demand for loans, interest rates.

**Explain more**

- **Worked example (10% ratio):** ₦1,000 deposited.

| Round | Deposit (₦) | Reserve 10% (₦) | Loan 90% (₦) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Original | 1,000 | 100 | 900 |
| 1st redeposit | 900 | 90 | 810 |
| 2nd redeposit | 810 | 81 | 729 |
| All banks | 10,000 | 1,000 | 9,000 |

  k = 1 / 0.10 = 10; total deposits = 1,000 × 10 = ₦10,000; loans = 10,000 − 1,000 = ₦9,000.
- At 20%: ₦2,000 / 0.20 = **₦10,000**. At 12.5%: k = 1 / 0.125 = **8**. At 25%: ₦1,000m × 4 = ₦4,000m. At 40%: k = 2.5, so ₦1,000m gives ₦2,500m.
- Leakage: if the public also keeps cash equal to 10% of deposits with r = 10%, the deposit multiplier falls to 1 / (0.10 + 0.10) = 5.
- More cash deposited by the public **expands**, never limits, credit creation.
- **Raises money supply:** a cut in the cash reserve ratio; CBN buying securities. **Reduces it:** a rise in the reserve ratio; CBN **selling** treasury bills to banks in open market operations; a greater public preference for cash.

### One-minute recap

- Barter = goods for goods; main problem: double coincidence of wants; extra costs: waiting and storage; n goods need n(n − 1)/2 relative prices.
- Money = anything generally acceptable in payment. Friedman: temporary abode of purchasing power. Hicks: money is what money does.
- Functions: medium of exchange and unit of account (primary); store of value, deferred payment (secondary); transfer of value.
- Qualities: acceptability (most important), scarcity, durability, homogeneity, divisibility, portability, recognisability, stability.
- Evolution: barter → commodity → coins → paper → deposits → electronic. Fiat = "let it be done"; gold link ended 1971; eNaira launched 25 October 2021.
- M1 = currency + demand deposits; M2 = M1 + savings + time; M3 = M2 + NBFI deposits.
- Value of money = purchasing power, inverse to the price level, measured by a price index.
- Credit multiplier k = 1/r; total deposits = initial deposit / r.

### Theory practice

**Question:** An economy's banks create total deposits of ₦500 million from an initial deposit of ₦50 million. (a) Find the money multiplier and the reserve ratio. (b) The reserve requirement is then raised by 100% and the initial deposit by 40%. Find the new total deposits.

*Solution:*

- (a) k = 500 / 50 = **10**. Since k = 1 / r, r = 1 / 10 = **10%**.
- (b) New r = 10% × 2 = 20%. New initial deposit = ₦50m × 1.4 = ₦70m. New k = 1 / 0.20 = 5. Total deposits = ₦70m × 5 = **₦350 million**. The doubled reserve ratio outweighs the larger deposit.
