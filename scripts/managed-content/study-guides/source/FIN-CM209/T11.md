## Topic 11: Bank Account Services for Exporters and Importers

**What you must know:** how banks help foreign traders; the methods of receiving payment for exports and who bears the risk; documentary letters of credit (parties, UCP 600, types); collections (clean, documentary, D/P, D/A, URC 522); shipping documents; nostro, vostro and domiciliary accounts; Form M, Form NXP and Form A; spot and forward exchange; export finance (bill negotiation, pre- and post-shipment finance, forfaiting, factoring, export credit insurance); NEXIM; the trade roles of merchant and commercial banks.

### Lesson 11.1: Banks in foreign trade and the methods of payment

**Must know**

- **Role of banks in international trade:** banks lend to traders for their overseas deals (overdraft and other lending facilities) and act as the **link between customers and their overseas trade partners**, especially in collecting payment for exports.
- **Methods banks use to obtain payment for exports:**

1. **Open account:** the exporter sends the **documents of title direct to the importer** abroad, has **no control** of the goods and **relies completely on the importer's honesty** to remit the proceeds after receiving (and selling) the goods. Most risky; discouraged.
2. **Payment by collection:** the exporter ships the goods and sends the bill of exchange (with or without documents) to **his banker for collection**.
    - **Clean collection:** **only the bill** of exchange is sent, no shipping documents.
    - **Documentary collection:** the bill is **accompanied by commercial documents** (bill of lading, insurance certificate or policy, and others).
    - Not fully safe: the exporter **cannot be 100% sure** the importer will pay.
3. **Documentary letter of credit:** the **surest** method and the one most advised to customers; it gives a **guarantee of payment to the exporter provided he complies with the terms of the credit**.
4. **Bill negotiation:** an exporter who needs funds and cannot wait **negotiates the bill to his bank by endorsement and delivery**; the bank pays **face value less a discount for commission and interest**, then collects the full amount at maturity.

- **Payment in advance (cash in advance):** the importer pays before shipment. Safest for the exporter; **greatest risk for the importer**.
- **Exporter's risk, lowest to highest:** payment in advance, letter of credit, documentary collection, clean collection, **open account**. The importer's risk runs the other way.
- **How the money actually moves:** by **mail transfer**, **telegraphic (wire) transfer** or **bank draft**. Banks actually remit abroad by **telegraphic (wire) transfer**, sent over **SWIFT**; standing orders and direct debits are domestic.

**Explain more**

- A Kano sesame exporter who hands his bank a bill drawn on a German buyer **with no shipping documents** is asking for a **clean collection**. Add the bill of lading and insurance certificate and it becomes a **documentary collection**.
- Open account = documents go **directly** to the buyer. Collection = documents go **through the banks**. Do not confuse "the importer's bank guarantees payment" (a credit) with a collection, where no bank guarantees anything.

### Lesson 11.2: Documentary letters of credit

**Must know**

- **Documentary letter of credit (LC):** a written undertaking by a bank (the issuing bank), given at the importer's request, to pay the exporter a stated sum **against documents that comply exactly** with the terms of the credit.

| Party | Who it is |
|---|---|
| **Applicant** | the **importer**, who asks his bank to open the credit |
| **Beneficiary** | the **exporter**, in whose favour the credit is opened |
| **Issuing (opening) bank** | the **importer's bank**, which opens the credit in favour of the exporter |
| **Advising bank** | a bank in the **exporter's country** (usually his bank) that **tells the exporter** the credit has been opened, without necessarily adding its own promise to pay |
| **Confirming bank** | a bank (usually in the exporter's country) that **adds its own definite undertaking to pay** |

- **Process:** (1) exporter and importer sign a sales agreement with specific terms; (2) the **importer's bank (issuing bank) opens the credit** in favour of the exporter; (3) the **exporter's bank (advising bank)** is advised of it; (4) goods are shipped; (5) the exporter presents the documents; (6) **the documents of title are released to the importer only against payment (D/P) or acceptance (D/A) of the bill of exchange**, so the exporter keeps control of the goods until then.
- **Rules:** **UCP 600** (Uniform Customs and Practice for Documentary Credits), published by the **International Chamber of Commerce (ICC)**, in force since 1 July 2007.
- **3 key UCP 600 principles:**

1. A credit is **irrevocable even if it does not say so**.
2. **Irrevocable** = it **cannot be amended or cancelled without the agreement of the issuing bank, any confirming bank and the beneficiary**.
3. **Banks deal with documents, not with goods.** Complying documents must be paid; **non-complying documents may be refused**.

- **Types of credit:**

| Type | Feature |
|---|---|
| **Confirmed** | a second bank adds its own undertaking to pay |
| **Sight** | paid **when compliant documents are presented** |
| **Usance (term, deferred)** | paid at a **stated future date** |
| **Transferable** | the beneficiary (a middleman) **transfers part or all of the credit to a second beneficiary**, his supplier |
| **Back-to-back** | a middleman uses the credit from his buyer **as security for a second, new credit** opened in favour of his supplier |
| **Red clause** | lets the exporter **draw an advance before shipment** to buy and pack the goods |
| **Revolving** | the amount is **restored after each drawing** for an agreed period |
| **Standby** | works as a **guarantee**, paid only if the applicant **fails to perform or pay** |

**Explain more**

- An Aba shoe exporter who doubts the overseas issuing bank asks a reputable Nigerian bank to add its promise: the credit is now **confirmed**. Confirmation is optional; an irrevocable credit does **not** have to carry a second bank's guarantee.
- A Port Harcourt importer whose documents comply cannot stop payment by complaining about goods still at sea. The bank **pays**, because banks deal in documents. His remedy is against the seller under the sales contract.
- **Transferable vs back-to-back:** transferable = the **same** credit passed on (Mrs Ezenwa passing part of her Ghanaian buyer's credit to the Kano factory). Back-to-back = a **second, separate** credit backed by the first.
- A Lagos manufacturer importing the same raw material from India every month uses a **revolving** credit instead of opening a new one each time.
- Standby credits follow **ISP98** (or UCP 600); collections follow URC 522 (Lesson 11.3). Incoterms (CIF, FOB and so on) are delivery terms, not LC rules.

### Lesson 11.3: Documentary collections and shipping documents

**Must know**

- **Documentary collection:** the exporter's bank sends the bill and shipping documents to a bank in the importer's country, which releases them to the importer on his paying or accepting. **Banks act only as agents and give no undertaking to pay.**
- **Rules:** **URC 522** (Uniform Rules for Collections, ICC).
- **Parties:** **principal** (exporter, drawer); **remitting bank** (the **exporter's bank**, which receives the documents and sends them abroad); **collecting bank** (any bank in the process other than the remitting bank); **presenting bank** (the collecting bank that presents the documents to the importer); **drawee** (importer).

| | D/P (documents against payment) | D/A (documents against acceptance) |
|---|---|---|
| Importer gets documents | only **on paying** | on **accepting a bill payable later** (e.g. 90 days) |
| Bill | at sight | usance (term) |
| Exporter's risk | lower: no payment, no documents | higher: importer has the goods before he pays |

| Collection | Letter of credit |
|---|---|
| URC 522 | UCP 600 |
| Banks are agents only; **no bank undertaking** | **Issuing bank undertakes to pay** against compliant documents |
| Less safe, cheaper | Surest, dearer |

- **Shipping documents:**

1. **Bill of lading (B/L):** issued by the shipping company. **3 functions:** (i) **receipt** for the goods, (ii) **evidence of the contract of carriage**, (iii) **document of title**, transferable by **endorsement**; the holder can take delivery.
2. **Commercial invoice:** prepared by the **seller**; shows description, quantity, unit price and total value; used for **payment and customs valuation**.
3. **Certificate of origin:** states the **country where the goods were produced or manufactured**.
4. **Insurance policy or certificate:** proves the goods are covered against loss in transit; under **CIF** terms the buyer claims on it if goods are damaged at sea.
5. **Air waybill:** the carrier's receipt for goods sent by air; **not a document of title**, so goods are released to the **named consignee** without it.

**Explain more**

- Why prefer a collection to open account even though neither has a bank guarantee? Because in a collection the **shipping documents stay under bank control until the buyer pays or accepts**.
- The Onitsha importer who accepted a 90-day bill, took the documents, cleared the goods and then refused to pay: the exporter lost because the collection was on **D/A** terms.
- Trap: a bill of lading does **not** certify the country of origin; that is the certificate of origin's job.

### Lesson 11.4: Foreign currency accounts, Nigerian trade forms and foreign exchange

**Must know**

- International trade needs international settlements; two accounts are principally kept: **nostro** and **vostro**.
- **Nostro** (Latin "our"): accounts **maintained by Nigerian banks abroad**, usually in foreign currency with a **correspondent bank**, to **receive export proceeds** and **settle payments requiring foreign currency** overseas.
- **Vostro** (Latin "your"): accounts **kept by foreign commercial banks with Nigerian banks**, into which payments in local currency are made and from which those banks **settle their obligations in Nigeria in naira**.
- **Loro** ("their"): an account of a third bank, referred to by two other banks.
- One account, two names: a Nigerian bank's dollar account in New York is its **nostro**; the New York bank calls it a **vostro**.
- **Domiciliary account:** an account held in a **foreign currency** (USD, GBP, EUR and so on) in a **bank in Nigeria**, by residents or non-residents, for foreign remittances, trade payments and holding forex. Since deregulation, exporters may keep their forex earnings in it. **2 types:** **Export Domiciliary Account** and **Ordinary Domiciliary Account**.
- **Nigerian trade forms** (all processed through an **authorised dealer bank**):

| Form | Use |
|---|---|
| **Form M** | statutory **import** document, completed and **registered electronically** through the authorised dealer bank, supported by a **proforma invoice**, before importing |
| **Form NXP** (Nigerian Export Proceeds Form) | statutory **export** form, so the export and the **repatriation of its proceeds** are documented and tracked |
| **Form A** | foreign exchange for **invisible** (service) payments, e.g. school fees, medical bills, travel |

- **Spot transaction:** settled almost immediately (normally within **2 business days**) at **today's rate**.
- **Forward exchange contract:** fixes a rate **today** for exchange at a **future date**; removes exchange rate risk.
- **Formula:** Saving from forward = Foreign amount x (Spot rate on settlement day - Forward rate). A negative answer is an extra cost.

**Explain more**

- **Worked example (forward saving):** an importer books a forward contract to buy USD 25,000 in 3 months at ₦1,520 per US dollar. On settlement day the spot rate is ₦1,560.
    - Cost at spot: 25,000 x 1,560 = ₦39,000,000.
    - Cost under forward: 25,000 x 1,520 = ₦38,000,000.
    - Saving: ₦39,000,000 - ₦38,000,000 = **₦1,000,000** (check: 25,000 x 40 = ₦1,000,000).
- If the naira had instead strengthened to ₦1,500, he would still pay ₦1,520: 25,000 x (1,500 - 1,520) = -₦500,000, an extra cost of ₦500,000 compared with spot. A forward removes uncertainty both ways; it is cover, not a bet.
- A Lagos importer who owes USD 40,000 in three months and fears naira weakness should take a **forward exchange contract** now, not wait for a spot purchase in three months.
- Traps: the account every bank keeps with the **CBN** for clearing is not a nostro; a customer's dollar account at a local branch is **domiciliary**, not nostro. Form M is for **imports**, NXP for **exports**. Nostro and vostro work is done in a bank's **Reconciliation Unit**; LCs and collections in **Trade Services**.

### Lesson 11.5: Export finance, NEXIM and the trade roles of banks

**Must know**

- **Pre-shipment finance:** money to **buy, process and pack** goods **before shipment** (e.g. packing credit, red clause credit).
- **Post-shipment finance:** money **after shipment**, e.g. **negotiating or discounting the export bill**.
- **Bill negotiation formula:** Amount credited = Face value - Interest - Commission, where Interest = Face value x Rate x (Months / 12) and Commission = Face value x Commission rate.
- **Factoring:** **sale of short-term book debts** (receivables) to a factor, who pays an agreed portion (often about 60% of book value) at once.
- **Forfaiting:** **purchase, without recourse, of medium-term export receivables** such as **avalised** (bank-guaranteed) bills or promissory notes.
- **Export credit insurance:** protects the exporter against **commercial risk** (buyer's default) **and political risk** (war, transfer bans) in the buyer's country.
- **NEXIM (Nigerian Export-Import Bank):** established by the **NEXIM Act No. 38 of 1991**, **jointly owned by the Federal Government and the CBN**, as Nigeria's **Export Credit Agency**, with a share capital of ₦50 billion under the 1991 Act. **5 functions:**

1. Export **credit guarantee and credit insurance** facilities to Nigerian exporters.
2. **Domestic credit insurance** that assists exports.
3. A **foreign exchange revolving fund** lent to exporters to **import foreign inputs** for producing exportable goods.
4. **Investment guarantee and investment insurance** facilities to exporters.
5. An adequate **information system** to support export business.

- NEXIM also gives **short- and medium-term loans** to exporters, short-term **guarantees for bank loans** to exporters, and cover against **political and commercial risks** of non-payment by foreign buyers. It does **not** grant mortgage loans.
- **Merchant banks' foreign exchange services** (wholesale, to corporates): **correspondent banking**, where they **arrange the confirmation of letters of credit** for clients; and **operations**: opening LCs, remitting export proceeds to clients' beneficiaries abroad, getting forex for clients' payments abroad. Their **traditional first function is acceptance**: adding their name to bills of exchange to finance others' trade (hence "accepting houses").
- **Commercial banks in international trade:** accepting and collecting foreign bills; discounting bills and documentary credits so sellers are paid at once; **confirming letters of credit**; buying and selling foreign currency; acting as **referees** (status opinions) on traders' creditworthiness. They do **not fix the official exchange rate**; that is the CBN's role.

**Explain more**

- **Worked example (bill negotiation):** Chief Adebayo, a Lagos cocoa exporter, negotiates a ₦4,000,000 bill 3 months before maturity. Interest 12% p.a., commission 0.5% of face value.
    - Interest: 4,000,000 x 0.12 x 3/12 = ₦120,000.
    - Commission: 4,000,000 x 0.005 = ₦20,000.
    - Credited: 4,000,000 - 120,000 - 20,000 = **₦3,860,000**.
    - Traps: ₦3,880,000 forgets the commission; ₦3,980,000 forgets the interest.
- A Sapele timber exporter financed to process the timber and later having his bill discounted has received **pre-shipment then post-shipment** finance, in that order.
- A cashew exporter fearing war or a currency transfer ban in the buyer's country needs **export credit insurance** such as NEXIM offers. Paying school fees abroad is a Form A matter, not a NEXIM fund.
- **Status opinion:** when a Ghanaian importer asks about a Nigerian exporter, the exporter's bank gives a confidential reply on his creditworthiness, acting as a **referee**. It is given in confidence and without responsibility, but must not be misleading.

### One-minute recap

- Exporter's risk rises: advance payment, LC, documentary collection, clean collection, **open account** (riskiest). Advance payment is riskiest for the importer.
- Clean collection = bill only; documentary = bill plus documents; banks are agents only, under **URC 522**.
- LC: applicant = importer; beneficiary = exporter; issuing = importer's bank; advising = exporter's bank; confirming adds a promise. **UCP 600**: irrevocable even if silent; documents, not goods.
- D/P = documents on payment; D/A = documents on acceptance (riskier).
- Transferable, back-to-back, red clause (advance), revolving (reinstated), standby (guarantee).
- B/L = receipt, contract of carriage, document of title; air waybill is not a title document.
- Nostro = ours abroad; vostro = theirs here in naira; domiciliary = forex account in Nigeria (export and ordinary).
- Form M imports, Form NXP exports, Form A invisibles. Forward fixes today's rate for a future date.
- Bill negotiation: face value - interest - commission. NEXIM: Act 38 of 1991, FGN and CBN, export credit agency.
- Merchant banks: acceptance, LC confirmation. Commercial banks act as referees; the CBN, not they, sets the official rate.

### Theory practice

**Question:** An exporter negotiates a ₦6,000,000 export bill with his bank 2 months before maturity. The bank charges interest at 15% per annum and a commission of 0.25% of face value. How much does he receive?

*Solution:* Interest = 6,000,000 x 0.15 x 2/12 = ₦150,000. Commission = 6,000,000 x 0.0025 = ₦15,000. Amount credited = 6,000,000 - 150,000 - 15,000 = **₦5,835,000**.
