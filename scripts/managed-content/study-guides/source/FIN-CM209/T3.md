## Topic 3: Historical Development of Banking in Nigeria

**What you must know:** the colonial (expatriate) banks and the West African Currency Board; the indigenous banks, the boom and doom and why they failed; Paton, the 1952 Ordinance, the 1958 law, the CBN and the 1969 Decree; indigenisation, development banks and merchant banks; SAP, distress and its cures; the 2004-2005 consolidation, the 2009 crisis, AMCON, universal banking and the current (2024-2026) recapitalisation.

### Lesson 3.1: The colonial banks and the West African Currency Board

**Must know**

- **First bank:** the **African Banking Corporation (ABC), 1892**, Lagos. It distributed British (Bank of England) currency. It marked the start of commercial banking in Nigeria.
- **Bank of British West Africa (BBWA), 1894:** took over ABC's Lagos business in 1894; first surviving bank; near-monopoly for over 30 years; banker to the colonial government. Today **First Bank of Nigeria** (name from 1979).
- **Colonial Bank** (1916/1917) merged with Barclays, the Anglo-Egyptian Bank and the National Bank of South Africa to form **Barclays Bank DCO** (1925). Today **Union Bank of Nigeria**.
- **British and French Bank (for Commerce and Industry), 1949.** Today **United Bank for Africa (UBA)**.
- These **three expatriate banks** (BBWA, Barclays DCO, British and French) dominated banking before independence. They are called the **first-generation banks**.

| Old name | Present name |
| --- | --- |
| Bank of British West Africa (later Standard Bank) | First Bank |
| Colonial Bank / Barclays DCO | Union Bank |
| British and French Bank | UBA |
| Agbonmagbe Bank | Wema Bank |
| International Bank for West Africa | Afribank (later Mainstreet, now part of Polaris) |

**Characteristics of the colonial banks:**

1. Foreign-owned and managed; **registered, headquartered and controlled from London**, so they fell under **London's** regulatory jurisdiction.
2. Served colonial government and **British/foreign trade** (palm oil, cocoa, groundnuts).
3. **Discriminatory lending:** they took African deposits but refused Africans credit.
4. Conservative lending with heavy collateral.
5. Branches only in **cities and coastal towns** where British trade was, not rural credit for peasant farmers.

**West African Currency Board (WACB), 1912:** issued and distributed currency in the British West African colonies before central banks. The **West African pound** was fixed at par with sterling and **100% backed by sterling** reserves. **BBWA was its agent** for custody and distribution of the currency.

- **Advantages:** unified currency accepted by the people; relieved government of currency management; no inflation or foreign exchange crisis; eased acceptance of new currencies at independence.
- **Criticisms:** (1) money supply was **tied to the balance of trade** (export earnings); (2) **no independence or discretion** for Nigeria's own monetary policy; (3) **hindered the growth of Nigeria's own financial system** (reserves invested abroad, no local investment); (4) failed to **train Africans** in monetary management; (5) one currency for colonies gaining independence at different dates.

**Explain more**

- Convertibility into sterling was a **feature** of the WACB system, not one of the criticisms listed against it.
- Trap: in some older questions ABC is dated 1882. The correct year is **1892**; choose the "1892, African Banking Corporation" option.
- The 1940 Lagos trader whose loan is refused while a British firm gets credit illustrates **discriminatory lending by the expatriate banks**, not any later CBN rule.

### Lesson 3.2: Indigenous banks, the boom and the doom (free banking era)

**Must know**

- **Free (laissez-faire) banking era (about 1891-1951):** anyone could open a bank with **no licence, no minimum capital and no regulation**. It ended in 1952.
- **Why indigenous banks emerged (2 reasons):** (1) the **expansion of African businessmen**; (2) the **discriminatory lending of the expatriate banks**. Nationalism added fuel.
- **Early indigenous banks:**
  - **Industrial and Commercial Bank, 1929**: the first indigenous bank; failed in 1930.
  - **Nigerian Mercantile Bank, 1931**: failed.
  - **National Bank of Nigeria, 1933**: first successful indigenous bank.
  - **Agbonmagbe Bank, 1945** (founded by Okupe): today **Wema Bank**.
  - **African Continental Bank (ACB), 1947**: associated with Dr Nnamdi Azikiwe.
- **Banking boom and banking doom (1945-1955):** many banks registered (about 145 banking companies in 1947 by one count), then most collapsed. By end-1954 **only three** indigenous banks survived: **National Bank, ACB and Agbonmagbe**, all rescued by their **regional governments**. Afribank was **not** one of them.

**Causes of the collapse (learn the list):**

1. Inadequate capital (undercapitalisation, weak capital base).
2. Untrained, inexperienced staff and bad management.
3. Fraud and stealing; insider abuse.
4. Indiscriminate branch expansion (over-expansion).
5. Crude accounting and poor record keeping.
6. **No regulatory framework** or supervisor.
7. Competition from the stronger foreign banks and the discriminating attitude of the colonial authorities.
8. Lack of government patronage; political and ethnic ties.
9. The 1952 Ordinance's capital rules, which many could not meet.

**Explain more**

- "NOT a cause" traps: **excessive CBN regulation** or **strict CBN cash reserve rules** (the CBN did not exist yet); **holding too much capital**; **foreign merchants' patronage and encouragement**; margin loans to stockbrokers or failing ₦25 billion (those are 2000s events). In "EXCEPT" items where every option (poor management, aggressive competition, no regulator) is a real cause, choose **none of the above**.
- "All of the above" is right when the options are weak capital, untrained managers and fraud.

### Lesson 3.3: Regulation begins: Paton, 1952, the CBN and the 1969 Decree

**Must know**

- **Paton Commission (1948):** led by **G. D. Paton**; enquired into banking business in Nigeria and **recommended the form and extent of control** to introduce.
- **Banking Ordinance 1952:** Nigeria's **first banking law**, based on the Paton Report, passed because of the **widespread failure of indigenous banks** in the free banking era. Provisions:
  1. A **licence** (from the Financial Secretary) before banking business.
  2. **Minimum paid-up capital**: £12,500 (₦25,000) for banks incorporated in Nigeria; £100,000 (₦200,000) for banks incorporated abroad, so **lower for Nigerian banks**.
  3. **Reserve fund** from profits.
  4. **Examination** of banks and periodic returns.
  5. Assistance to indigenous banks; **3-year grace period** to comply.
- It did **not** create deposit insurance (NDIC came in 1988). Its lasting significance: it **ended free banking and set up the principle of government regulation**.
- **After 1952 many more banks failed** because (all of these): they could not meet the minimum capital; there was **no supervisory agency** to give direction; they lacked government patronage.
- **1958 law** (Banking Ordinance/Act 1958, with the CBN Act): expatriate capital raised to ₦400,000; indigenous unchanged at ₦25,000; licensing power passed to the Minister of Finance after consulting the CBN.
- **Road to the CBN:** Mbadiwe's motion (1952); **Fisher Report (1953)** said it was too early; IBRD mission (1953); **J. B. Loynes report (1957)** favoured a central bank. **CBN Act 1958**; operations began **1 July 1959**.
- **First Governor:** **Roy Pentelow Fenton** (British, 1958-1963). First Nigerian Governor: **Aliyu Mai-Bornu** (1963-1967). Current Governor (2026): **Olayemi Cardoso** (since September 2023).
- **Early CBN steps:** own currency (1959); **treasury bills (April 1960)**, which began the money market; **Lagos Bankers' Clearing House (1961)** to settle cheques drawn on one bank and paid into another; support for the Lagos Stock Exchange (1961).
- **Significance:** Nigeria gained **control of its own currency and monetary policy**.
- **Banking Decree 1969:** **more comprehensive**; added to the CBN's control tools; extended monetary control to **most banking institutions other than commercial banks**. Minimum paid-up capital **₦600,000 indigenous, ₦1.5 million expatriate**. Repealed by **BOFID No. 25 of 1991** (with CBN Decree No. 24 of 1991).

**Regulatory milestones in order:** free banking (to 1951) · Banking Ordinance 1952 · CBN Act 1958 · Banking Decree 1969 · NDIC Decree 1988 · BOFID 1991 · Failed Banks Decree 1994 · CBN Act 2007 · BOFIA 2020 (current).

**Explain more**

- 1952 versus 1969: the 1952 Ordinance ended free banking under colonial rule; the 1969 Decree gave the CBN a much wider range of controls.
- You may also see the 1952 requirement quoted as ₦50,000 authorised capital. Either way, the point is that many small banks could not meet it.
- A tiny Lagos bank closing in 1953 within the grace period is a victim of the **1952 licensing and minimum capital rules**.

### Lesson 3.4: After independence: indigenisation, development and merchant banks

**Must know**

- **Indigenisation:** transferring ownership and control of enterprises, banks included, **from foreigners to Nigerians**.
- **Nigerian Enterprises Promotion (Indigenisation) Decree 1972:** the Federal Government took **40%** in the **three largest foreign banks** (First, Union, UBA). The 1976-1977 amendments raised it: banking had to be **at least 60% Nigerian-owned**.
- **Outcomes:** Nigerians moved into **top management and directorships**; banking skills grew; new indigenous and state-owned banks were set up.
- **Development finance institutions:**

| Institution | Purpose |
| --- | --- |
| Nigerian Industrial Development Bank (NIDB) | long-term finance for industrial projects |
| Nigerian Bank for Commerce and Industry (NBCI) | lending to Nigerian businessmen, including to buy shares in foreign firms |
| Nigerian Agricultural and Cooperative Bank (NACB) | agriculture and rural cooperatives |
| Federal Mortgage Bank of Nigeria | long-term housing (mortgage) finance |

- **Merchant banking** began in **1960** with **Nigerian Acceptances Limited (NAL)** and **Philip Hill (Nigeria) Limited**; they merged in **1969** as **NAL Merchant Bank**, Nigeria's first merchant bank.
- **Merchant banks vs commercial banks:** merchant banks are **wholesale** (large firms, institutions, medium- to long-term corporate finance, issuing house, leasing, acceptances, few branches); commercial banks are **retail** (current and savings accounts, short-term loans, wide branches).

**Explain more**

- A 10-year loan for a textile factory in the 1970s points to **NIDB**. NIDB and NBCI were merged into the **Bank of Industry** (2001); NACB became today's **Bank of Agriculture**.
- The rural banking scheme (1977) and community banks (1990, now microfinance banks) took banking beyond the cities.

### Lesson 3.5: SAP deregulation, distress and the response

**Must know**

- **Deregulation era (1986 to the mid-1990s):** the **Structural Adjustment Programme (SAP)** from 1986. Reforms:
  1. Deregulation of **interest rates**.
  2. Removal of **credit ceilings**.
  3. **Liberalised bank licensing**, so banks proliferated (about 40 commercial and 15 merchant banks before 1986 to about 125 commercial and merchant banks by 1994).
  4. Foreign exchange liberalisation: **Second-tier Foreign Exchange Market (SFEM), 1986**, rate set by market forces.
  5. Privatisation of government stakes.
- SAP did **not** increase Federal Government deposits in banks: in 1989 public sector deposits were **withdrawn** from banks to the CBN. Raising capital to ₦25 billion is 2004, not SAP.
- **Bank distress:** a bank suffers severe **financial, operational and managerial weakness** that makes it **unable to meet its obligations to customers, owners and the economy as and when due**.
- **CAMEL (5):** **C**apital adequacy, **A**sset quality, **M**anagement, **E**arnings, **L**iquidity. Market share is not one.
- **Symptoms (5):** boardroom squabbles; customer complaints of unpaid maturing obligations; **desperate borrowing** above market rates; window dressing; **persistent adverse clearing position and overdraft with the CBN**.
- **Causes:** internal: weak management, inadequate capital, fraud and insider abuse, poor asset and liability management. External: **macroeconomic instability** (inflation, recession), weak legal framework, delayed supervisory action, external shocks and balance of payments problems, political interference.
- **Responses:** **NDIC (1988)**, from a 1983 CBN committee proposal for a **depositors protection fund**; **Failed Banks (Recovery of Debts) and Financial Malpractices in Banks Decree 1994**: tribunals that recovered debts owed to failed banks **and** tried their directors; liquidations, the largest being **26 banks on 16 January 1998**.

**Explain more**

- A bank that is a regular **net placer** of surplus funds in the interbank market is healthy, not distressed.
- Cures for distress: a stable economy, sound regulation, good management, liquidity and funds management, fraud control.

### Lesson 3.6: Consolidation, the 2009 crisis and banking today

**Must know**

- **Consolidation:** reducing the number of banks and other deposit-taking institutions while **increasing the size and concentration** of those that remain.
- **2004 programme:** announced by **CBN Governor Charles Soludo** at a special Bankers' Committee meeting on **6 July 2004**. Minimum capital base rose from **₦2 billion to ₦25 billion** by **31 December 2005** (18 months).
- **Definitions:** capital base = **paid-up capital and reserves unimpaired by losses**; paid-up capital = ordinary shares plus non-redeemable preference shares.
- **Allowed modes:** **only mergers and outright acquisitions**. A mere **group arrangement** (a holding company keeping separate banks) was **not** accepted.
- **Incentives:** authority to deal in **foreign exchange**; **public sector deposits**; recommendation to collect public revenue; prospect of managing part of the **external reserves**; tax incentives; lower transaction costs. **Not** an incentive: exemption from CBN supervision.
- **Result:** **89 banks became 25** (75 banks merged into groups); 14 failed and were liquidated. Stanbic and IBTC then merged, leaving 24. **Wema Bank merged with National Bank of Nigeria.**
- **2009 crisis:** governor **Sanusi Lamido Sanusi**; a **joint CBN and NDIC** special examination. In **August 2009** the CEOs of **five** banks were removed: **Afribank, Finbank, Intercontinental, Oceanic, Union**. Then three more: **Bank PHB, Equatorial Trust Bank, Spring Bank**. Causes: **margin loans and oil and gas exposure, weak risk management and corporate governance**, insider abuse. Constant borrowing at the **Expanded Discount Window** showed the banks were **illiquid**. The CBN injected about ₦620 billion.
- **AMCON (2010):** buys **non-performing loans** from banks, takes over the collateral and helps recapitalise them.
- **Universal banking (2001):** one licence for commercial, merchant (investment) and other financial services. **Reviewed in 2010:** banks had to **divest non-core businesses or use a holding company**, under **commercial, merchant or specialised** licences.
- **2010 commercial bank capital:** **regional ₦10 billion, national ₦25 billion, international ₦50 billion** (merchant ₦15 billion).
- **Current position:** **BOFIA 2020** replaced BOFIA 1991; the **CBN Act 2007** governs the CBN. In **March 2024** the CBN set new minimum capital: **international ₦500 billion, national ₦200 billion, regional ₦50 billion, merchant ₦50 billion, non-interest national ₦20 billion and regional ₦10 billion**, deadline **31 March 2026**. In April 2026 the CBN confirmed that **33 banks** had met it.

**Explain more**

- The March 2004 review rated 62 banks sound, 14 marginal, 11 unsound, and 2 had not sent returns (62 + 14 + 11 + 2 = 89).
- Why consolidate? The 89 banks were small and fragile; one South African bank (ABSA) had more assets than all of them together.
- The 2004 governor was **Soludo**, not Sanusi (2009) or Cardoso (2024).

**Development of the financial system:** it grew through rising savings and investment, the establishment of the CBN, and technology such as electronic banking. A return to barter, closing banks or removing regulation would hold it back.

**Periods to name:**

1. Pre-independence free banking (before 1959; colonial era 1892-1960).
2. Regulated banking (1960-1985).
3. SAP deregulation (1986 to 1993/95).
4. Consolidation (July 2004 to December 2005), followed by post-2009 reform and today's digital era.

### One-minute recap

- ABC 1892 first; BBWA 1894 (First Bank); Colonial Bank/Barclays DCO (Union); British and French 1949 (UBA).
- WACB 1912: sterling-backed West African pound, BBWA as agent; no monetary independence.
- Indigenous: ICB 1929 (first), National Bank 1933, Agbonmagbe 1945 (Wema), ACB 1947; only National Bank, ACB and Agbonmagbe survived the 1945-1955 boom and doom.
- Paton Report led to the Banking Ordinance 1952: licence, minimum capital, reserves, examination; ended free banking.
- CBN Act 1958, operations 1 July 1959, Fenton first governor; Loynes 1957.
- Banking Decree 1969: ₦600,000 indigenous, ₦1.5 million expatriate; replaced by BOFID 1991, now BOFIA 2020.
- Indigenisation 1972 (40%), 1976-1977 (60%); NAL plus Philip Hill gave NAL Merchant Bank (1969).
- SAP 1986: deregulation, SFEM; distress measured by CAMEL; NDIC 1988; 26 banks liquidated in 1998.
- Soludo, 6 July 2004: ₦2 billion to ₦25 billion; 89 to 25 banks; mergers and acquisitions only.
- 2009: five plus three CEOs removed; AMCON 2010; 2024 recapitalisation (₦500 billion international) with a 31 March 2026 deadline.

### Theory practice

**Question:** In the 2004 consolidation, a bank had a capital base of ₦2 billion. (a) How much extra capital did it need, how many times did the requirement rise, and by what percentage? (b) By what percentage did the number of banks fall when 89 became 25?

*Solution:*

(a) Extra capital = ₦25 billion - ₦2 billion = **₦23 billion**. Times = 25 ÷ 2 = **12.5 times**. Percentage rise = (23 ÷ 2) × 100 = **1,150%**.

(b) Fall = 89 - 25 = 64 banks. Percentage = (64 ÷ 89) × 100 = 71.91, so **about 71.9%** (rounded to one decimal place).
