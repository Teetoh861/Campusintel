## Topic 7: Organisational Structure of Clearing Banks

**What you must know:** what a clearing bank is and its six characteristics; the three levels of structure (governance, management, operational); board committees and executive officers; front, middle and back office and support functions; the branch network; line and staff, centralisation and decentralisation; unit, branch, group, chain and correspondent banking; the clearing process and the history of cheque clearing in Nigeria (manual, MICR, NACS, the 2004 settlement framework, electronic systems).

### Lesson 7.1: Clearing banks and their characteristics

**Must know**

- **Clearing banks:** financial institutions that are **members of a clearing house**, **maintain accounts with the Central Bank** and can **participate directly in the clearing and settlement** of cheques, payments and other payment instruments. In Nigeria they are **commercial banks licensed by the CBN** to take part in the national clearing and settlement system.
- **6 key characteristics:**

1. **Membership of the clearing house** run by or under the CBN.
2. **Settlement account with the CBN**, used to settle inter-bank fund transfers and net clearing positions.
3. **Wide branch network** across the country.
4. **Large capital base** (must meet CBN minimum capital).
5. **Advanced technology** for payment processing.
6. **Regulatory compliance:** subject to strict CBN supervision.

- **Regulatory framework (6):** CBN (primary regulator: licences, examinations); **NDIC** (**insures deposits**, supervises, **resolves failed banks**); **BOFIA 2020** (main banking law; replaced BOFIA 1991); CBN Prudential Guidelines (capital, liquidity, lending limits); AML/CFT regulations; Consumer Protection Framework.

**Explain more**

- A clearing bank is **not** restricted to a single office in one locality; that describes a unit bank (Lesson 7.4). The settlement account at the CBN is **not** for safe custody, deposit insurance premiums or issuing currency.
- Commercial banks such as First Bank, Zenith, Access, UBA and GTBank are clearing banks; microfinance banks are not direct clearing house members.
- **Challenges (7):** cybersecurity; compliance costs; fintech disruption; cost of branch networks; credit risk (bad loans); customers' demand for 24/7 service; talent.
- **Future trends (7):** digital transformation; agility (flatter structures); **centralisation of operations** (back office consolidated into central processing centres and shared services, for **efficiency**); fintech partnerships and open banking; data analytics; ESG (green) banking; hybrid work.

### Lesson 7.2: Governance and management levels

**Must know**

The structure is hierarchical and examined at **3 levels:**

1. **Governance level:** the Board of Directors.
2. **Management level:** executive management.
3. **Operational level:** departments and units.

- **Board of Directors:** sits at the **apex**; responsible for **overall strategic direction and governance**.
- **Board composition:** **Board Chairman (non-executive)**; MD/CEO; Executive Directors (typically 3 to 5); independent Non-Executive Directors; representative of major shareholders (where applicable).
- **7 board functions:** formulating strategic policies and objectives; appointing and overseeing senior management; approving major capital expenditure and investments; ensuring regulatory compliance and corporate governance; risk oversight and approving the risk management framework; reviewing and approving annual budgets and financial statements; protecting shareholders' interests.

| Board committee | Job |
|---|---|
| Audit | Financial reporting, internal controls, external audit |
| Risk Management | Monitors and manages all categories of risk |
| Credit | Reviews and approves large credit facilities; sets credit policies |
| Governance and Nomination | Board appointments, succession planning, governance |
| Remuneration | Compensation packages for executive management |
| IT Governance | Technology strategy, cybersecurity, digital transformation |

- **Board vs executive management:** the board **sets strategy and oversees**; executive management **runs the bank day to day** and implements board policies.

| Executive | Responsibility |
|---|---|
| MD/CEO | Highest-ranking executive: implements board decisions, develops strategy, leads the management team, represents the bank externally, ensures profitability and growth |
| Deputy Managing Director (DMD) | Assists the MD/CEO and acts in his absence; often oversees strategic business units |
| ED, Commercial Banking | Commercial, corporate and SME banking |
| ED, Retail Banking | Retail operations, consumer lending, branch network |
| ED, Treasury and Financial Markets | Treasury, foreign exchange, investment portfolio, money market |
| Chief Financial Officer (CFO) | Financial planning, accounting, financial reporting, investor relations |
| Chief Risk Officer (CRO) | Enterprise risk management: credit, market, operational risk |
| Chief Compliance Officer (CCO) | Regulatory compliance, AML, KYC |
| CTO/CIO | IT infrastructure, digital banking platforms, technology strategy |
| Chief Operations Officer (COO) | Operations, service delivery, process optimisation, efficiency |
| Company Secretary/Legal Adviser | Legal counsel, corporate governance, regulatory filings |

**Explain more**

- **Traps:** the Chairman is **not** the MD/CEO; CBN governance rules keep the roles separate. The board does **not** process account-opening forms. Shareholders, not depositors, meet at the AGM.
- Scenario links: a ₦25 billion loan above management's limit goes to the **Credit Committee**; finding a successor to a retiring chairman is **Governance and Nomination**; overseeing external auditors is **Audit**; cybersecurity oversight is **IT Governance** (not Risk Management).
- Kemi, who heads AML and KYC, is the **CCO**, not the CRO. The CRO handles risk, not compliance.

### Lesson 7.3: Operational level, branch network and authority

**Must know**

**A. Front office (revenue generating; deals directly with customers)**

1. **Retail Banking:** personal accounts, consumer loans, cards.
2. **Corporate Banking:** large corporates, project and trade finance.
3. **Commercial Banking:** medium-sized enterprises, working capital.
4. **SME Banking:** small businesses, micro-finance.
5. **Treasury:** FX, money market, investment securities, asset-liability management.
6. **Investment Banking:** mergers and acquisitions advisory, capital raising, underwriting.
7. **Private Banking/Wealth Management:** high net-worth individuals, investment advisory, estate planning.

**B. Middle office (support and control)**

1. **Risk Management:** credit, market and operational risk.
2. **Compliance:** AML/CFT, sanctions, regulatory reporting.
3. **Internal Audit:** independent review of controls.
4. **Credit Administration:** **credit appraisal, loan documentation, collateral management, credit monitoring**.
5. **Finance and Planning:** budgeting, management reporting.
6. **Legal:** contracts, litigation, debt recovery.

**C. Back office (processing and settlement)**

1. **Operations:** transaction processing, account opening, cheque clearing, funds transfer.
2. **Payment and Settlement:** clearing house operations, RTGS, NEFT, inter-bank settlement.
3. **Reconciliation Unit:** account reconciliation, **nostro/vostro** accounts, **suspense account** resolution.
4. **Customer Service:** enquiries, complaints.
5. **Cards and Electronic Banking:** ATMs, POS, mobile banking.
6. **Trade Services:** **letters of credit**, bills for collection, trade documents.

**D. Support functions:** Human Resources; Information Technology; Procurement/General Services; Marketing and Corporate Communications; Business Development; Internal Control.

**Branch network (top down):** **Head Office → Regional/Zonal Office → Area/Divisional Office → Branches**, plus **Agency banking/agents**.

- **Head office:** executive management and central departments. **Regional/Zonal office:** **oversees clusters of branches in a geographical zone** (e.g. Lagos Zone); reports to head office. **Area office:** smaller clusters within a region. **Branches:** outlets serving customers directly (flagship, commercial, retail, mini). The **branch manager** has overall responsibility: business development, customer relations, approving loans within delegated limits.
- **Agency banking:** third-party agents (e.g. a shop owner) offering basic deposit and withdrawal services in underserved areas.

- **Line authority:** direct authority passed down the chain of command over people who carry out the bank's main business (e.g. MD → regional manager → branch manager).
- **Staff authority (staff function):** specialists who **advise** line managers but **cannot command** them (legal, research, HR training).
- **Centralisation:** decision-making concentrated at head office; branches refer major decisions upward. **Decentralisation:** authority delegated to branch managers, who decide locally.

**Explain more**

- **Traps:** Internal Audit is **middle office**, not front office. Payment and Settlement is **back office**, not a support function. Treasury and Private Banking **are** front office. Credit Administration is middle office, distinct from Corporate Banking which sells loans.
- An importer in Kano wanting a letter of credit is served by **Trade Services** (back office). A unit matching nostro and vostro entries is the **Reconciliation Unit**.
- A branch manager is a **line** officer; the head of legal advice, a research analyst and an HR training officer are **staff**. When the Legal Department reviews a loan contract but cannot order the branch to lend, it is a staff function.
- An Ibadan branch manager who must send every small loan to Lagos shows **excessive centralisation** (delay, frustrated customers). **Decentralisation** gives **faster decisions that respond to local needs**; centralisation gives tighter uniform control.

### Lesson 7.4: Types of banking systems

**Must know**

| System | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Unit banking | Each bank is a separate entity, usually a single office, with its own management; operations restricted to one locality (USA tradition) |
| Branch banking | One bank operates many branches across the country under one head office (Nigeria, UK) |
| Group banking | Two or more banks, each keeping its separate name, controlled by a **holding company**; the controlled banks are **operating banks** |
| Chain banking | Control of several separate banks by an **individual or group of individuals** through share ownership, with **no holding company** |
| Correspondent banking | Small (**respondent**) banks keep deposits with large city (**correspondent**) banks, which give them services; it knits unit banks together |

**Unit banking: merits**

1. Prevents monopoly banking.
2. Close personal relationship with, and knowledge of, local customers.
3. Quick local decisions; efficient management.
4. Funds are used locally for local development.

**Unit banking: demerits**

1. Cannot grant large loans (small resources).
2. Risk is not spread geographically.
3. Difficulty meeting a sudden rush of withdrawals.
4. Dependence on correspondent banks for remittance of funds, which is costly.
5. No economies of scale; savings facilities limited to one locality.

**Branch banking: merits**

1. Economies of large-scale operation.
2. **Geographical spreading of risk** (losses in one area covered by profits elsewhere).
3. Small cash reserves at each branch: **cash can be mobilised quickly from sister branches**.
4. Wider mobilisation of savings, including rural areas.
5. Cheap and easy remittance of funds.
6. Easier and more efficient **central bank control** of a few large banks.

**Branch banking: demerits**

1. Can create **monopolistic or oligopolistic and bureaucratic conditions**.
2. Delay and red tape: branch managers refer decisions to head office.
3. Lack of personal touch with local customers.
4. Funds drained from rural to urban areas.

**Group banking:** merits: economies of scale, central management, operating banks can **pool resources to finance large borrowings**. Demerits: may lead to **monopoly**; holding company may **divert surplus reserves to its non-banking interests**; **failure of one operating bank can affect others**.

**Explain more**

- **Group vs chain:** group banking uses a **holding company**; chain banking is control by **individuals** through share ownership (Chief Okonkwo and his brothers owning controlling shares in three banks). In both, the banks keep separate names.
- **Correspondent vs respondent:** the large city bank is the correspondent; the small community bank that keeps deposits with it is the **respondent**. The correspondent clears cheques, remits funds, gives investment advice and joins in large loans for the respondent. It does not replace the CBN as lender of last resort and does not put banks under one holding company.
- Nigerian commercial banks with hundreds of outlets across the 36 states and the FCT under one head office practise **branch banking**; some also sit under holding companies (e.g. Access Holdings, First HoldCo, formerly FBN Holdings).
- "Close personal relationship with local customers" is a **merit** of unit banking, not a demerit.

### Lesson 7.5: The clearing function and its history in Nigeria

**Must know**

- **Clearing system:** the process of **obtaining values for non-in-house cheques among deposit money banks**, usually carried out at the **clearing house of the CBN or its designated branches**. Put simply: banks exchange cheques drawn on one another and settle the **net** amounts owed.
- **Lagos Clearing House:** a **cheque sorting (exchange) centre**, not a stock exchange or commodity market. The CBN set up the first bankers' clearing house in **1961** for the Lagos zone.

**7 steps of cheque clearing (in order):**

1. **Collection:** branches collect cheques deposited by customers.
2. **Sorting:** by paying bank into **on-us** (drawn on the same bank, settled internally) and **other-bank** items.
3. **Presentation:** other-bank cheques are presented to the clearing house.
4. **Clearing session:** the clearing house coordinates the exchange between banks.
5. **Settlement:** **net positions settled through settlement accounts at the CBN** (debits and credits).
6. **Return items:** **unpaid cheques sent back through the clearing system with reasons**.
7. **Customer notification** of cleared or returned items.

**History (dates):**

| Period | Key facts |
|---|---|
| Manual (from 1961) | Bank representatives met physically to exchange cheques and present claims; up to 8 hours to know net positions; up to 27 days for customers to get value |
| MICR (from 1988) | Magnetic Ink Character Recognition, to speed machine processing and shorten clearing; phased from CBN in 1988; Lagos zone stopped conventional cheque exchange on 1 July 1994; reject rate about 30% for lack of cheque standards |
| NACS (2002) | Nigeria Automated Clearing System: automated clearing began in 2002 with 22 banks; NACS is a department of **NIBSS** (Nigeria Inter-Bank Settlement System) |
| New settlement framework (1 April 2004) | Banks split into settlement and non-settlement banks |
| Cheque truncation (2012) | Image-based clearing; less physical movement of cheques |

**NACS: 4 core objectives**

1. Efficiently and cost-effectively process clearing items using electronic data presentation.
2. **Significantly reduce the clearing float.**
3. Maintain and enforce standards for clearing procedures.
4. Encourage development and integration of other electronic payment products.

**NACS participants (3):** **Zonal Clearing House** (centre: registers participants, processes data, determines settlement positions, manages NACS infrastructure); **Direct participating bank** (through its Participating Bank Clearing Centre, PBCC, sends data straight to the ZCH; 21 at inception); **Indirect participating bank** (clears **through a direct participating bank**).

**2004 framework:** **settlement bank** = a bank that clears and settles cheques **on behalf of other banks**; only settlement banks keep **settlement accounts** with the CBN and deposit collateral with it. **Non-settlement banks** keep **only operations accounts** with the CBN (for FX, cash and cash reserve requirements, always in credit) and clear through an **agency arrangement** with a settlement bank, depositing at least ₦250 million collateral with it.

**Electronic systems:** **NEFT** (NIBSS Electronic Funds Transfer: **batch** processing); **RTGS** (Real-Time Gross Settlement: **individual large-value payments in real time**); **NIP** (NIBSS Instant Payment: **instant** transfer to another bank); **Cheque Truncation System** (electronic image-based clearing).

**Explain more**

- **Why the 2004 framework:** by 1995 over thirty banks persistently overdrew their CBN accounts through clearing; suspending a bank from clearing was too damaging, so the framework imposed market discipline to end overdrawn accounts. Settlement banks needed ₦15 billion (later ₦16 billion) clearing collateral and branches in all 22 clearing zones; seven were named before the 2005 consolidation (including UBA, First Bank, Union Bank, GTBank and Zenith).
- **Benefits of automation:** shorter clearing cycle and **smaller clearing float**, so customers get value faster (5 days cut to 3, now shorter); less rowdiness and late balancing; less fraud. Automation did **not** abolish cheques and did **not** remove banks' CBN accounts or allow unlimited overdrawing.
- **Today:** NIBSS (owned by the CBN and the banks) runs NACS, NIP and NEFT; the CBN runs RTGS and holds banks' settlement accounts. With cheque truncation, cheques now clear on the next working day (T+1).
- **Traps:** NACS is run by **NIBSS**, not CIBN, SEC or NDIC; NDIC does not settle clearing balances. A cheque drawn on Bank B paid into Bank A is an **other-bank item** presented through the clearing house; a cheque drawn on Bank A itself is **on-us**. RTGS is real-time and gross; NEFT is batch.

### One-minute recap

- Clearing bank = clearing house member + CBN settlement account + direct clearing. Three levels: governance (board), management (executives), operational (departments).
- Board at the apex; Chairman non-executive; six committees: Audit, Risk, Credit, Governance and Nomination, Remuneration, IT Governance.
- MD/CEO leads; DMD acts in his absence; CRO risk; CCO compliance, AML, KYC.
- Front office earns revenue; middle office controls (incl. Internal Audit, Credit Administration); back office processes (Operations, Payment and Settlement, Reconciliation, Trade Services).
- Branch network: head office → region/zone → area → branch, plus agents.
- Line commands; staff advises. Excessive centralisation causes delay; decentralisation speeds local decisions.
- Unit, branch (Nigeria), group (holding company, operating banks), chain (individuals), correspondent (respondent banks).
- Clearing: collect → sort → present → session → settle at CBN → returns → notify.
- Manual 1961, MICR 1988, NACS 2002 (NIBSS), settlement banks 1 April 2004, truncation 2012; NIP instant, RTGS real-time large value, NEFT batch.

### Theory practice

**Question:** Distinguish a settlement bank from a non-settlement bank under the CBN's April 2004 framework, and list the steps in clearing a cheque.

*Answer outline:* A settlement bank clears and settles cheques on behalf of other banks, keeps a settlement account with the CBN and deposits clearing collateral directly with it. A non-settlement bank keeps only an operations account with the CBN (always in credit), and clears through an agency arrangement with a settlement bank, depositing at least ₦250 million collateral with that bank. Steps: collection, sorting (on-us or other-bank), presentation, clearing session, settlement of net positions through CBN settlement accounts, return of unpaid items with reasons, customer notification.
