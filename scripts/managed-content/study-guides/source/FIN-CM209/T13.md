## Topic 13: Balance Sheet Structure and Interpreting Customers' Accounts

**What you must know:** why bank accounts must be uniform and what SAS 10 covered; the bank's financial statements; every asset and liability item in order; statutory reserve rules under BOFIA 2020; deposit and non-deposit sources of funds and the uses of funds; bank ratios (liquidity, cash reserve, excess reserve, loan-to-deposit); and how a lending banker reads a customer's accounts: liquidity, gearing, profitability and activity ratios, window dressing, break-up value and overtrading.

### Lesson 13.1: Bank financial statements and SAS 10

**Must know**

- **Why uniform bank accounts matter:** banks hold the public's savings, provide the means of payment and finance business and trade, so they must keep public confidence. Their statements must be **uniformly presented, reliable, factual and comparable**, so users can judge performance and regulators (CBN, NDIC) can classify and supervise them.
- **Statement of Accounting Standard (SAS) 10** (accounting by banks) focused on **3 main areas:**
  1. **Income recognition**
  2. **Loss recognition**
  3. **Balance sheet classification**
- **6 reasons a special standard was needed:**
  1. Importance of the banking sector to industrial and commercial development.
  2. **Allegedly overstated profits** reported by banks.
  3. **Inconsistent accounting policies** and reporting practices that made comparison of performance difficult.
  4. The need to sustain public confidence in the banking sector.
  5. Survival problems of troubled banks.
  6. A probable "shake-out" ahead from increased competition.
- **2 major financial statements:** the **balance sheet** and the **income statement**. Others: source and application of funds, notes to the accounts, five-year financial summary, statement of value added.
- **Balance sheet rule:** group assets and liabilities by **nature** and list them in **order of liquidity and maturity**, starting with **cash**. It shows the sources of funds and how much has gone into loans, securities and other uses **at one date**.
- **Income statement:** the cost of acquiring deposits and other funds (interest to depositors, staff, overheads, taxes) against revenue (interest on loans and advances, leases, fees), ending with **net earnings**, part reinvested and part paid as dividends.

**Explain more**

- Foreign exchange rate determination, staff matters, branch hours and fixing savings rates were **not** SAS 10 areas.
- SAS 10 is now history: Nigerian banks have reported under **IFRS** since 2012, where the balance sheet is called the **statement of financial position**. The ordering and the items you learn here still describe a bank's position.
- **Bank versus trading company:** a trading company's balance sheet is mainly stock and fixed assets funded largely by owners' capital. A bank's is **mainly financial assets** (cash, bills, loans) **funded largely by deposits**, with fixed assets a **small share**.
- **Off-balance-sheet (contingent) items:** guarantees (e.g. a performance guarantee for a contractor building a road for a state government), acceptances and letters of credit are **not** assets or liabilities until the customer defaults. They are **disclosed in the notes to the accounts**.

### Lesson 13.2: The asset side of a bank's balance sheet

**Must know**

Assets, in order of liquidity (most liquid first):

1. **Cash and short-term funds:** notes and coins in the strong rooms of all branches, regional offices and head office; the most liquid asset. The bank fixes its cash ratio and keeps vault cash to the barest minimum. **Short-term funds** = sums due from other banks in Nigeria or abroad, e.g. cheques on other banks credited to customers before clearing. Balances with the CBN and with other banks are also assets.
2. **Short-term investment:** **Treasury bills**, maturity within **91 days** (the shortest tenor; Nigerian Treasury bills are also issued for 182 and 364 days). Held because they are **safe (less risky), highly liquid and can be rediscounted with the CBN before maturity**.
3. **Long-term investment:** longer maturities but solid, e.g. **development stocks and bonds**.
4. **Bills discounted:** FGN Treasury bills, special bills, trade bills, bankers' acceptances, commercial papers, less provision for discount.
5. **Loans and advances:** the **risk assets**; credit facilities to customers. **About 70% or more of revenue** comes from them. Except term loans, all are technically **repayable on demand**. They earn **more interest than government securities** because they are riskier, so banks take **security (collateral)**.
6. **Other assets:** accrued interest, prepayments, **accrued interest receivable**.
7. **Fixed assets:** land and buildings, machinery, vehicles, fixtures and fittings, computers and other equipment, shown at **cost less depreciation** (net value) to give a true and fair view.
8. **Equipment on lease:** machinery, vehicles and other fixed assets leased out.

**Explain more**

- Traps: **deposits are never an asset** of the bank. Treasury bills do **not** earn the highest rate and are **not** secured on land; they mature, so they give no permanent income.
- Least liquid items sit at the bottom (fixed assets). Most liquid: cash and short-term funds.
- Accrued interest **receivable** is an asset (money owed to the bank); accrued interest **payable** to depositors is a liability.

### Lesson 13.3: The liability and capital side; statutory reserve

**Must know**

1. **Deposits:** the **largest liability** of any deposit money bank. Depositors are **creditors** of the bank who can call for most of their money **on demand or at short notice**. They include personal and business customers and other banks.
2. **Other liabilities:**
   - amount due to subsidiary companies
   - **provision for taxation**
   - **proposed dividends** to shareholders
   - cash security for letters of credit
   - cash security for collection
   - remittance deposits
   - interest reserves: interest on deposits accrued but not yet paid
   - **bank drafts and certified cheques** (issued, not yet paid)
3. **Capital:** owners' contribution. **Paid-up capital** = the amount actually injected by shareholders. **Authorised capital** = the **maximum** shareholders may inject.
4. **Statutory reserves:** a reserve fund every bank must keep by law, built from **net profit after tax, before any dividend is declared**.
5. **Capital reserves:** not mandatory but a prop for growth.
6. **Retained profits (retained earnings):** **undivided profits** left in the business **after dividends and all other appropriations**.

**Statutory reserve transfer (BOFIA 2020, section 15; formerly BOFIA 1991, section 16):**

| Reserve fund compared with paid-up share capital | Minimum transfer |
| --- | --- |
| Less than paid-up capital | 30% of net profit after tax |
| Equal to or more than paid-up capital | 15% of net profit after tax |

No transfer is made until all identifiable and accumulated losses have been made good.

**Explain more**

- **Customer balances:** Mr Eze's ₦2,000,000 credit balance is a **liability** (the bank owes him). Mrs Musa's ₦500,000 overdraft is an **asset** (she owes the bank). Loans, advances and overdrafts are always assets.
- **Worked example:** a bank earns net profit after tax of ₦10bn.
  - Reserve fund below paid-up capital: 30% × ₦10bn = **₦3bn** to transfer.
  - Reserve fund already equal to or above paid-up capital: 15% × ₦10bn = **₦1.5bn**.
- Watch the word **nominal**: in this course it is used for the amount already invested by owners, though in company law nominal capital usually means authorised capital.
- **Not** liabilities: loans and advances, Treasury bills, bills discounted, accrued interest receivable.

### Lesson 13.4: Sources and uses of a bank's funds

**Must know**

**Deposit funds (4 types):**

1. **Current account:** **non-interest-bearing demand deposits**, withdrawn at any time by cheque without notice, as long as the balance covers it.
2. **Savings account:** the **lowest interest rate** offered; a **minimal amount** opens it.
3. **Time or fixed deposit:** a **fixed maturity (term)** and a **stipulated interest rate**.
4. **Short-term investors:** investors seeking money market yields.

**Non-deposit funds:**

1. **Investment income:** from investments in other banks, government securities, subsidiaries, real estate.
2. **Rental income:** from bank property let to others.
3. **Borrowing:** **overnight call deposit** (arranged in minutes but at a **very high interest rate**), **interbank borrowing**, **long-term debenture**.
4. **Owners' capital:** founders' contribution or shares sold in the capital market.
5. **Retained earnings:** accumulated net income left after dividends.
6. **Interest income:** interest and fees on loans (the bulk of revenue), interest on placements and securities.
7. **Fees earned:** trust services, issuing houses work, account maintenance, bank drafts, **letters of credit**, cheque books, excess clearing charges.
8. **Sale of assets:** real estate, a subsidiary, collateral property. **Extraordinary income** because it is **usually non-recurring**.

**Uses of funds:**

1. Repayment of deposits on demand (current, savings, time).
2. Payment of interest to depositors.
3. Salaries and wages.
4. Loan repayment (principal and interest to other banks or debenture holders).
5. Investment in government securities and other approved investments.
6. Reserves required by regulators.
7. Fees and capital budget: new branches and information technology.

**Explain more**

- **Trap:** fees earned from letters of credit are a **source**, not a use.
- Mrs Adeyemi's ₦5,000,000 for 180 days at 12% a year with an early-withdrawal penalty is a **fixed (time) deposit**.
- **Capital history:** under the 2010 licensing regime a bank needed paid-up capital of ₦10bn (regional), ₦15bn (merchant), ₦25bn (national) or ₦50bn (international). The **2024 recapitalisation** (deadline **31 March 2026**) raised this to **₦500bn** (international commercial), **₦200bn** (national), **₦50bn** (regional and merchant), **₦20bn** and **₦10bn** (national and regional non-interest).

### Lesson 13.5: Reading a bank's balance sheet: ratios

**Must know**

- Liquidity ratio = Liquid assets ÷ Total deposit liabilities × 100
- Required liquid assets = Minimum liquidity ratio × Deposits; Shortfall = Required − Held
- Required cash reserve = Cash reserve ratio × Eligible deposits
- Excess reserve = Reserves actually held − Required reserve
- Loan-to-deposit ratio = Loans and advances ÷ Total deposits × 100; Maximum loans = Limit × Deposits

Liquid assets here: cash, balances with the CBN, Treasury bills (and net interbank placements). **Never** loans or fixed assets. Current CBN position: liquidity ratio **30%**; cash reserve ratio **45%** for deposit money banks. Exam questions usually state an assumed figure: use the one given.

**Explain more**

Surulere Bank: cash ₦6bn, CBN balances ₦15bn, Treasury bills ₦24bn, loans and advances ₦110bn, fixed assets ₦12bn; deposits ₦150bn.

1. **Liquidity ratio:** liquid assets = 6 + 15 + 24 = ₦45bn. Ratio = 45 ÷ 150 × 100 = **30%**. (Loans and fixed assets are left out.)
2. **Loan-to-deposit ratio:** 110 ÷ 150 × 100 = **73.33%** (2 d.p.).
3. **Maximum loans** under a 65% internal limit: 0.65 × ₦150bn = **₦97.5bn**.
4. **Shortfall:** minimum 30%, liquid assets held ₦40bn. Required = 0.30 × ₦150bn = ₦45bn. Shortfall = 45 − 40 = **₦5bn**.
5. **Cash reserve and excess:** assume CRR 20% on ₦150bn: required = 0.20 × 150 = ₦30bn. If the bank holds ₦33bn with the CBN, excess reserve = 33 − 30 = **₦3bn**.

Traps: liquidity ratio divides by **deposits**, not total assets; excess reserve is held minus required, not the required amount itself.

### Lesson 13.6: Interpreting a customer's accounts

**Must know**

- **Purpose:** the banker analyses the customer's balance sheet and profit and loss account (usually for three years) to judge whether the business **can repay the advance and interest from its operations and assets**, not to audit, set prices or compute tax.

| Question | Ratios |
| --- | --- |
| Can it meet short-term debts? (liquidity) | Current ratio, quick ratio, working capital |
| How much is borrowed? (gearing, solvency) | Gearing, debt-to-equity, interest cover |
| Is it profitable? | Net profit margin, ROCE |
| How efficiently does it use assets? (activity) | Stock days, stock turnover, debtor days |

**Formulas:**

- Current ratio = Current assets ÷ Current liabilities
- Quick (acid-test) ratio = (Current assets − Stock) ÷ Current liabilities
- Working capital = Current assets − Current liabilities
- Gearing = Long-term debt ÷ (Long-term debt + Shareholders' funds) × 100
- Debt-to-equity = Long-term debt ÷ Shareholders' equity
- Interest cover = Profit before interest and tax ÷ Interest (times)
- Net profit margin = Net profit ÷ Sales × 100
- ROCE = Profit before interest and tax ÷ Capital employed × 100; Capital employed = Shareholders' funds + Long-term loans
- Stock days = Stock ÷ Cost of sales × 365
- Stock turnover = Cost of sales ÷ Average stock (times)
- Debtor collection period = Trade debtors ÷ Credit sales × 365
- Shareholders' funds = Share capital + Reserves (including retained profits)

**Key ideas:**

- **Stock** is left out of the quick ratio because it is the **least liquid** current asset and may not turn into cash quickly at book value.
- **High gearing** = heavy reliance on borrowed funds relative to owners' funds: more risk to a new lender.
- **Break-up (forced-sale) basis:** a prudent banker values assets at what they would fetch if the business failed, not on a going-concern basis.
- **Tangible net worth** = Shareholders' funds − Intangibles; **goodwill is deducted** because it has little or no value if the business is wound up.
- **Window dressing:** arranging transactions just before the balance sheet date so the accounts look stronger than normal.
- **Overtrading:** sales growing faster than the working capital to support them: working capital falls, creditors and overdraft rise sharply, current ratio falls below 1.
- **Limitations of the balance sheet:** historical (possibly out-of-date) figures; position at **one date only**; open to window dressing. (Listing assets and liabilities is its purpose, not a limitation.)
- **Cash flow forecast:** loans are repaid out of **cash**; a profitable business can still fail if it runs out of cash.

**Explain more**

Ikeja Traders Ltd: stock ₦3,000,000, debtors ₦2,000,000, cash ₦1,000,000; trade creditors ₦2,500,000, overdraft ₦1,500,000; long-term loan ₦8,000,000; share capital ₦10,000,000, retained profits ₦6,000,000, goodwill ₦1,000,000; sales (all on credit) ₦36,500,000, cost of sales ₦21,900,000, net profit ₦2,920,000; profit before interest and tax ₦4,800,000, interest ₦800,000.

1. Current assets = 3,000,000 + 2,000,000 + 1,000,000 = ₦6,000,000. Current liabilities = 2,500,000 + 1,500,000 = ₦4,000,000 (the overdraft is a current liability).
2. **Current ratio** = 6,000,000 ÷ 4,000,000 = **1.5 : 1**.
3. **Quick ratio** = (6,000,000 − 3,000,000) ÷ 4,000,000 = **0.75 : 1**.
4. **Working capital** = 6,000,000 − 4,000,000 = **₦2,000,000**.
5. Shareholders' funds = 10,000,000 + 6,000,000 = ₦16,000,000. **Gearing** = 8,000,000 ÷ (8,000,000 + 16,000,000) × 100 = **33.3%** (1 d.p.). **Debt-to-equity** = 8,000,000 ÷ 16,000,000 = **0.5 : 1**.
6. **Interest cover** = 4,800,000 ÷ 800,000 = **6 times**. If you are given profit **before tax** after interest (say ₦4,000,000), add the interest back first: 4,000,000 + 800,000 = 4,800,000, then divide.
7. **Net profit margin** = 2,920,000 ÷ 36,500,000 × 100 = **8%**. If given gross profit and expenses (sales ₦50m, gross profit ₦15m, expenses ₦11m): net profit = 15 − 11 = ₦4m; margin = 4 ÷ 50 × 100 = **8%**.
8. **ROCE** = 4,800,000 ÷ (16,000,000 + 8,000,000) × 100 = **20%**.
9. **Stock days** = 3,000,000 ÷ 21,900,000 × 365 = **50 days**. **Stock turnover** = 21,900,000 ÷ 3,000,000 = **7.3 times**.
10. **Debtor days** = 2,000,000 ÷ 36,500,000 × 365 = **20 days**.
11. **Tangible net worth** = 16,000,000 − 1,000,000 = **₦15,000,000**.
12. **Working backwards:** current ratio 1.5 : 1 with current liabilities ₦4,000,000 gives current assets = 1.5 × 4,000,000 = ₦6,000,000, so working capital = ₦2,000,000.

Reading the signs:

- Current ratio 2.4 : 1 but quick ratio 0.4 : 1: most current assets are **tied up in stock**.
- Debtor days rising from 35 to 80 with sales unchanged: **credit control is weakening**, some debts may go bad, cash is squeezed.
- Bisi Trading borrows ₦10m from a director on 30 December to clear its overdraft and redraws the overdraft on 3 January to repay him: **window dressing**.
- Sales double, working capital falls, creditors and overdraft jump, current ratio below 1: **overtrading**.
- Traps: use **cost of sales** (not sales) for stock days and stock turnover; use **credit sales** for debtor days; gearing divides by debt **plus** equity, debt-to-equity by equity alone.

### One-minute recap

- SAS 10 covered income recognition, loss recognition and balance sheet classification; reasons include overstated profits and inconsistent policies. Banks now use IFRS.
- Bank assets run in order of liquidity: cash and short-term funds, Treasury bills, long-term investments, bills discounted, loans and advances, other assets, fixed assets, equipment on lease.
- Loans and advances are the risk assets earning about 70% or more of revenue; Treasury bills are safe, liquid and rediscountable.
- Deposits are the largest liability; overdrafts and loans are assets; guarantees are off-balance-sheet.
- Statutory reserve (BOFIA 2020): 30% of net profit after tax if the reserve is below paid-up capital, 15% if equal or above, before any dividend.
- Deposit funds: current, savings, fixed, short-term investors. Non-deposit: investment and rental income, borrowing, capital, retained earnings, interest, fees, sale of assets (extraordinary).
- Liquidity ratio = liquid assets ÷ deposits; excess reserve = held − required; LDR = loans ÷ deposits.
- Customer analysis: current, quick, working capital, gearing, interest cover, margin, ROCE, stock and debtor days.
- Prudent bankers value on a break-up basis, deduct goodwill, watch for window dressing and overtrading, and check cash flow.

### Theory practice

**Question:** Oshodi Ventures Ltd asks for an overdraft. Current assets ₦9,000,000 (including stock ₦4,000,000); current liabilities ₦6,000,000; long-term loan ₦5,000,000; shareholders' funds ₦15,000,000; profit before interest and tax ₦3,000,000; loan interest ₦500,000. Compute the current ratio, quick ratio, working capital, gearing and interest cover, and comment.

*Solution:*

- Current ratio = 9,000,000 ÷ 6,000,000 = **1.5 : 1**.
- Quick ratio = (9,000,000 − 4,000,000) ÷ 6,000,000 = 5,000,000 ÷ 6,000,000 = **0.83 : 1** (2 d.p.).
- Working capital = 9,000,000 − 6,000,000 = **₦3,000,000**.
- Gearing = 5,000,000 ÷ (5,000,000 + 15,000,000) × 100 = **25%**.
- Interest cover = 3,000,000 ÷ 500,000 = **6 times**.
- Comment: liquidity is adequate on the current ratio, but the quick ratio below 1 shows reliance on selling stock to pay short-term debts. Gearing is low and interest is well covered, so the business can carry more debt; the banker should still check the cash flow forecast and value security on a break-up basis.
