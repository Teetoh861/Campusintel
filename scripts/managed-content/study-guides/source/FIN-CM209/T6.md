## Topic 6: The Nigerian Money Market

**What you must know:** what a money market is and how it differs from the capital market; why Nigeria set one up; its structure and operators; every instrument with its date; discount houses and the lines of defence; growth factors, appraisal and challenges; Treasury bill, call money and repo computations.

### Lesson 6.1: Meaning and features of the money market

**Must know**

- **Money market:** a market for buying and selling **short-term financial instruments or investible funds**, usually with maturities of **up to one year**. Money is bought and sold like any other commodity. What is traded are **money market instruments**.
- **Capital market:** the market for buying and selling **medium- to long-term** instruments (2 years to 30 or more; equity is perpetual).
- **Features of the money market:**
  1. Short-term instruments (up to one year).
  2. Highly liquid: quickly turned into cash.
  3. Low risk: issuers are creditworthy (government, big banks, blue-chip firms).
  4. **Wholesale and professional:** large deals dominated by institutions with specialised knowledge.
  5. No single trading floor: deals are done by telephone and electronic platforms.

| Point | Money market | Capital market |
|---|---|---|
| Term | Short (up to 1 year) | Medium to long |
| Main purpose | Short-term **liquidity adjustment** | Turning savings into **long-term investment** |
| Apex regulator | **CBN** | **SEC** (Securities and Exchange Commission) |
| Dominant institutions | **Deposit money banks** | **Non-bank financial institutions** |
| Instruments | TBs, CPs, CDs, BAs, call money | Shares (equities), bonds, debentures, mortgages |

- The **abiding (most basic) distinction** is the **term and liquidity** of the instruments.
- Both markets have a **primary market** (new issues, raising new funds) and a **secondary market** (resale of existing securities).

**Explain more**

- Trap: shares, equities, debentures, bonds and mortgages are **capital market** instruments, never money market ones.
- Today most Nigerian money market trading (Treasury bills, commercial paper) is done over the counter on the FMDQ Securities Exchange platform, not on a stock exchange floor.

### Lesson 6.2: Why Nigeria needed a money market

**Must know**

- **Before the CBN (1958/59) Nigeria had no money market.** 4 consequences:
  1. Businessmen and industrialists had no local market to raise funds.
  2. Surplus funds were **sent abroad to the London money market**.
  3. Government had no machinery for mobilising resources as independence approached.
  4. Monetary policy was futile while foreign banks, run on conditions abroad, dominated finance.
- **Rationale (reasons for establishing it):**
  1. Government's need to **bridge the gap between expenditure needs and expected revenue** (Treasury bills even out seasonal revenue).
  2. **Nigerianisation of the credit base:** keep funds in Nigeria for local credit needs instead of letting them flow overseas.
  3. Firms' need for **working capital** and traders' need to **finance trade** (sometimes overnight).
  4. Machinery for government's short-term financing.
  5. A step to independent nationhood (**monetary autonomy**).
  6. Perform all traditional money market functions. Banks also gain a way to **economise on cash and adjust liquidity**, and the CBN a basis for **effective monetary policy**.
- On the supply side, banks and multinationals hold **excess funds** they want to invest short-term and safely.
- **4 prerequisites for a money market:**
  1. Short-term funds whose owners are willing to invest them.
  2. A **continuous and sufficient flow of assets of varying maturities**.
  3. **Intermediaries** to pool funds and invest them in short-term bills.
  4. A **lender of last resort** (the central bank) to guarantee the liquidity of the assets.
- **4 functions:**
  1. A basis for effective **monetary policy** and macroeconomic stability.
  2. Deficit institutions borrow and surplus institutions invest **temporarily**; less idle cash.
  3. Institutions hold part of their funds in liquid assets convertible to cash quickly.
  4. The CBN can **gauge scarcity or surplus of funds through movements in money market interest rates**.
- **Benefits:** (a) reduces **idle, barren cash**: people earn income yet stay liquid; (b) banks hold less idle cash and invest secondary reserves; (c) ready gauge of fund scarcity for the CBN; (d) efficient execution of monetary policy.
- **Price discovery:** the money market sets benchmark short-term interest rates that serve as reference rates for other instruments.

**Explain more**

- "All of the above" is the answer when options list government's revenue gap, firms' working capital and traders' finance: all three create the need.
- A requirement that instruments be listed on the stock exchange is **not** a prerequisite.
- How the money market helps monetary policy: the CBN **buys or sells short-term government securities (open market operations)** to inject or withdraw liquidity. It does not remove reserve requirements or fix prices of goods.

### Lesson 6.3: Structure and operators

**Must know**

- **Apex institution:** the **CBN** (current Governor: Olayemi Cardoso, since 2023).
- **Structure:**
  1. **Formal (organised) market:** CBN open market operations, CBN standing facilities, the **interbank market**, the discount market, primary and secondary markets in Treasury bills and other instruments.
  2. **Informal market:** esusu, ajo, isusu and other rotating contribution clubs, thrift collectors and money lenders.
- **Interbank market:** banks **lend to and borrow from each other** (often overnight) to manage daily liquidity, at rates set by supply and demand.
- **Standing facilities (CBN):** **Standing Lending Facility (SLF):** a bank short of funds borrows **overnight from the CBN** against eligible securities. **Standing Deposit Facility (SDF):** a bank with surplus places it overnight with the CBN.
- **Operators:** CBN; discount houses (historically); Federal and state governments; commercial, merchant, universal and (today) non-interest and other deposit money banks; specialised banks (community, now microfinance, banks; People's Bank); savings-type institutions; statutory boards and corporations; NSITF; mortgage and finance houses; insurance companies; pension funds; large corporations; individuals; local governments.
- **Lines of defence for a bank short of cash:**
  1. **First line:** **call money / interbank borrowing**.
  2. **Second line:** **discounting bills** (e.g. Treasury bills) for cash.
- **Discount house:** a non-bank institution "trading in and holding Treasury bills, commercial bills and other securities", acting as **intermediary between the CBN and licensed banks**.

**Explain more**

- Structure: **oligopolistic**, dominated (apart from government) by a few large banks, long the "big three" (First Bank, Union Bank, UBA). It is not perfectly competitive or a CBN monopoly.
- Stockbrokers, registrars, issuing houses, the stock exchange and the SEC are **capital market** operators. A group of "CBN, deposit money banks, Federal Government, large corporations" is wholly money market.
- An ajo scheme in Balogun Market, where traders pay ₦2,000 a day and each collects the pool in turn, is the **informal** money market.
- A merchant bank buying 182-day Treasury bills at the CBN auction is in the **primary** market; reselling them to a pension fund two weeks later is the **secondary** market.
- **Discount houses history:** first suggested in the 1960s but dropped for lack of business. **First Securities Discount House (FSDH)** was set up in **1992**, the first. Later came others such as Kakawa, Consolidated, Express and Associated Discount Houses. They bought government securities from the CBN, warehoused and sold them (so government got its full funds), took call loans from banks, and gave banks a place to **invest surplus short-term funds and get them back quickly**. By about 2015 the leading ones had converted into **merchant banks** (FSDH Merchant Bank, Coronation Merchant Bank from Associated, FBNQuest Merchant Bank from Kakawa). Treasury bill auctions now run through banks acting as primary dealers.
- **OMO sale** of Treasury bills by the CBN **reduces banks' reserves and withdraws liquidity**; an OMO purchase injects liquidity.

### Lesson 6.4: Money market instruments

**Must know**

| Instrument | Key facts |
|---|---|
| **Treasury bill (TB)** | Short-term **FG** debt issued by the **CBN on behalf of the Federal Government**; sold at a **discount**, redeemed at **face value**. First issued **1960**, ₦18 million. Standard tenors **91, 182, 364 days** (91 is the shortest). Sold by **auction**: authorised dealers (banks) tender for themselves and customers. |
| **Treasury certificate (TC)** | **1968**, ₦20 million; **1 to 2 years**, so higher return than TBs. Widened instruments and financed the **civil war and post-war reconstruction**. Discontinued by 1996. |
| **CBN certificate** | February **2001**; 180 and 360 days; minimum ₦250,000. |
| **CBN bills** | September **2005**; issued by the CBN for **monetary policy (to mop up excess liquidity)** in OMO; originally 14 to 182 days. |
| **Certificate of deposit (CD)** | Bank receipt for a time deposit, **3 months to 3 years**. Introduced **1975** as an **interbank** instrument channelling **commercial banks' surplus funds to merchant banks**. **Negotiable** (rate negotiated; can be **sold in the secondary market** before maturity) or **non-negotiable** (rate fixed by CBN; held to maturity). |
| **Commercial paper (CP)** | **Short-term unsecured promissory note** of a large, creditworthy (blue-chip) firm, sold at a discount; backed only by the **issuer's reputation**; up to 270 days. |
| **Bankers' acceptance (BA)** | A **bill of exchange drawn on a bank and accepted by it**: the bank writes **"Accepted"** with an authorised signature across the face; 30 to 270 days. |
| **Call money** | Very short loans (overnight or a few days) **repayable on demand**. |
| **Repo** | Sale of securities with an agreement to **buy them back** at a set price and date. |
| **Tenored deposits** | **Call deposit** (no fixed maturity; ended by notice of 24 hours, 7 days, 14 days, 1 month); **fixed deposit** (fixed period, fixed rate). |

- **Discontinued schemes:**
  1. **Call Money Fund** (1962 to 1974): banks lent surplus clearing balances to the fund at a CBN-fixed rate (between 2% and 2.5%) for investment in TBs; abolished because **government's financial position had improved**.
  2. **Produce Bills Finance Scheme** (from 1962, in the 1960s): a consortium of financial houses **discounted marketing boards' export produce bills**.
  3. **Bankers' Unit Fund** (1976 to 1988): banks pooled surplus funds with the CBN for investment mainly in **Federal Government stocks**; counted as liquid assets.
- **Eligible development stocks** (1975): FG development loan stocks with **less than three years to maturity**, counted as banks' **liquid assets** for the liquidity ratio.
- **Modern instruments:** **Electronic Funds Transfer** (via **SWIFT**) and the **smart card** ("cashless wallet" with a memory chip).

**Explain more**

- TBs and TCs are **primary sources of liquidity** for a bank. A TB investor's return is **face value minus price paid**: no dividends, no coupons.
- **CP vs commercial (trade) bill:** a trade bill arises from **an actual sale of goods on credit**; CP is **not tied to any trade transaction**. A highly rated food company needing ₦2 billion for 120 days, unsecured and cheaper than a bank loan, issues **CP**.
- A BA is **safer than CP** of the same maturity because a **bank has undertaken to pay**. BAs mainly finance **international trade** (an importer's draft accepted by a bank).
- **CBN bills vs TBs:** CBN bills mop up liquidity; TBs raise short-term funds for the FG.
- **Call deposit example:** Mrs Adeyemi places ₦5 million with no fixed maturity, ended by seven days' notice: a **call deposit**.
- Under the SLF the bank **borrows**; under the SDF it **deposits**. Don't swap them.

### Lesson 6.5: Money market computations

**Must know**

- Discount (bank discount method) = Face value x rate x days/365
- Price = Face value - Discount
- Simple yield = (Face value - Price) / Price x 365/days
- Interest (call money, interbank, deposits) = Principal x rate x days/365
- Repo rate = (Repurchase price - Sale price) / Sale price x 365/days

**Explain more**

**Example 1: Treasury bill price.** ₦20,000,000 face value, 182 days, 11% discount rate.
- Discount = 20,000,000 x 0.11 x 182/365 = ₦1,096,986.30 (rounded to kobo).
- Price = 20,000,000 - 1,096,986.30 = **₦18,903,013.70**.
- The same method prices commercial paper: the issuer receives the price.

**Example 2: yield.** Holding the bill above to maturity:
- Gain = 20,000,000 - 18,903,013.70 = ₦1,096,986.30.
- Yield = 1,096,986.30 / 18,903,013.70 x 365/182 = 0.058032 x 2.005495 = **11.64%** (2 d.p.).
- The yield is **above** the 11% discount rate, because the gain is measured on the smaller price. A ₦1,000,000 91-day bill bought for ₦975,000: 25,000/975,000 x 365/91 = **10.28%**.

**Example 3: interbank interest.** ₦30,000,000 lent for 3 days at 18%: 30,000,000 x 0.18 x 3/365 = **₦44,383.56**. For overnight use days = 1.

**Example 4: repo rate.** A bank sells TBs for ₦4,900,000 and buys them back for ₦5,000,000 after 30 days.
- Gain to the buyer = ₦100,000.
- Rate = 100,000 / 4,900,000 x 365/30 = 0.020408 x 12.166667 = **24.83%** (2 d.p.).

Traps: use 365, not 360 (360 gives wrong options); the discount is not the price; for a repo divide by the **amount received**, not the buy-back price.

### Lesson 6.6: Growth, appraisal and challenges

**Must know**

- **5 factors that helped growth:**
  1. Establishment and **enthusiasm of the CBN**.
  2. Policy of **Nigerianising the credit base**.
  3. The **teleprinter** service, which **speeded up transfers between CBN branches and its Lagos head office**.
  4. **Permissive legislation** allowing new and varied instruments.
  5. **Government's financial requirements**.
- **Appraisal (how well it performed):**
  1. Economic function (no idle funds): **deficient**: excess liquidity remains.
  2. Nigerianising credit: worked in the 1960s, weakened after the oil boom (capital flight, external borrowing).
  3. First line of defence: weak since the Call Money Fund ended in **1974**; the interbank market is smaller and dearer.
  4. Second line of defence (bill discounting): partly met since discount houses came.
  5. Channel for CBN cash and basis for **OMO**: partly met; OMO is now a key tool.
  6. Short-term funds for government: **most effective**.
- **Challenges:**
  1. **Dominance by government securities**.
  2. **Narrow range of instruments**.
  3. Lack of **depth, breadth and resilience**.
  4. Low **public awareness**.
  5. **Oligopolistic structure** dominated by big banks.
  6. Weak **institutional framework for probity, integrity and accountability**.
  7. **Information asymmetry**: unequal access to market information, weakening pricing.

**Explain more**

- "Listing of Treasury bills on foreign stock exchanges" was **never** a growth factor.
- "Lack of market space in some cities" is **not** a challenge: the market needs no physical location.
- An unregulated market, too many instruments or too many small investors are all wrong options; the real problems are government dominance and too few instruments.

### One-minute recap

- Money market = short-term (up to 1 year), liquid, low-risk, wholesale; CBN apex, banks dominate. Capital market = long-term; SEC apex, non-bank institutions dominate.
- No money market before the CBN; surplus funds went to London. Key aim: Nigerianise the credit base.
- 4 prerequisites end with a lender of last resort.
- First line of defence: call money/interbank; second: discounting bills.
- TBs 1960 (91/182/364 days); TCs 1968; Call Money Fund 1962-74; Produce Bills from 1962; CDs and eligible stocks 1975; BUF to 1988; CBN certificate 2001; CBN bills 2005; FSDH 1992.
- CP is unsecured, backed by reputation; BA is a bill accepted by a bank.
- OMO sale withdraws liquidity; SLF lends to banks, SDF takes their deposits.
- Discount = FV x r x days/365; price = FV - discount.

### Theory practice

**Question:** A ₦10,000,000 91-day Treasury bill is issued at a 10% discount rate. Find the price and the investor's simple yield (365-day year).

*Answer:*
- Discount = 10,000,000 x 0.10 x 91/365 = ₦249,315.07.
- Price = 10,000,000 - 249,315.07 = **₦9,750,684.93**.
- Yield = 249,315.07 / 9,750,684.93 x 365/91 = **10.26%** (2 d.p.).
