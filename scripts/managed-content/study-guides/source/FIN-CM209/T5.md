## Topic 5: The Nigerian Banking Structure, Savings and Investment

**What you must know:** the parts of the financial system and how intermediaries turn savings into investment; commercial, merchant and non-interest banks, the licence regime and every recapitalisation; the development finance institutions and their mergers; Peoples Bank, community banks and microfinance banks; the NDIC and distress resolution; the other regulators and non-bank institutions; simple interest, real rate, NHF and NDIC computations.

### Lesson 5.1: The financial system, savings and investment

**Must know**

- **3 basic types of financial institutions:** (1) **bank financial institutions (BFIs)**: deposit money banks and specialised banks; (2) **non-bank financial institutions (NBFIs)**: insurance companies, pension funds, finance houses, unit trusts, mutual funds, savings and loans; (3) **regulatory and supervisory institutions**. BFI liabilities (demand deposits) are spendable as money; NBFI liabilities are not.
- **4 components of the system:** institutions, markets (money and capital), instruments, regulators.
- **Formal sector:** licensed and regulated (banks, insurers, pension funds, capital market operators). **Informal sector:** unlicensed and unsupervised: **esusu, ajo, isusu** (rotating contributions), moneylenders, daily savings collectors.
- **Financial intermediation:** channelling funds from **surplus units** (savers) to **deficit units** (investors). Intermediaries promote the savings-investment process and so **capital formation** (acquiring capital assets to raise productivity) and growth.
- **Why intermediaries beat direct lending:** they pool small savings into bulky sums; spread risk (lower risk per saver, so lower cost of funds); give longer terms (**maturity transformation**: short-term deposits funding long-term loans); assess borrowers expertly; cut transaction costs; give savers liquidity and income. Direct sourcing means high cost, short term and high risk.
- **Factors for successful intermediation:** trust and confidence; convenience; price (interest and fees); nearness to market. Loss of confidence causes **disintermediation**.
- **3 goals of a DMB:** **profitability, liquidity, solvency.** Loans earn most but reduce liquidity; cash is liquid but earns nothing.
- **Savings products:** savings account, target savings, fixed (time) deposits, digital savings. **Channels of bank investment:** direct lending, securities (Treasury Bills, bonds), equity (within limits), trade finance.
- **Challenges to saving:** low income, inflation, financial exclusion, distrust after bank failures, competing assets (property, dollars, crypto).
- **Formulas:** Simple interest = P × R × T (T in years). Amount = P + interest. Real interest rate ≈ nominal rate − inflation rate.

**Explain more**

- Twenty Oshodi traders each paying ₦5,000 weekly, one collecting the pool in turn, with no licence or regulator: **informal** sector.
- A bank using thousands of withdrawable savings to fund a ten-year cement-factory loan shows **maturity transformation**.
- **Fixed deposit:** ₦1,000,000 for 3 months at 12% simple. Interest = 1,000,000 × 0.12 × 3/12 = ₦30,000. Amount paid = 1,000,000 + 30,000 = **₦1,030,000**. (₦30,000 alone is the interest, not the payment.)
- **Savings:** Chidinma's ₦800,000 at 6% for 9 months: 800,000 × 0.06 × 9/12 = **₦36,000** interest.
- **Real rate:** savings pay 12%, inflation is 21%: 12 − 21 = **−9%**. The saver loses purchasing power; this is why inflation discourages saving.

### Lesson 5.2: Deposit money banks: commercial, merchant, non-interest

**Must know**

- **Deposit money banks (DMBs)** = **commercial banks and merchant banks**. Nigeria runs **branch banking**: one bank, many offices nationwide, one serving as head office.
- **Commercial bank functions:** (1) accept deposits: current (demand), savings, fixed/time; (2) grant advances: loans, overdrafts, bill discounting; ancillary: agency (payments, remittances), executor and trustee, **safe-keeping of wills, jewellery and documents**, payment services, foreign exchange.
- **Merchant bank:** engaged in wholesale banking, medium- and long-term finance, equipment leasing, debt factoring, investment management, issue and acceptance of bills, unit trusts. Started 1960 (Philip Hill Nigeria and Nigerian Acceptances Ltd, merged 1969 as NAL Merchant Bank).
- **Merchant bank services:** *banking:* **acceptances** (the **traditional** function that marks them out: accepting bills of exchange to finance the trade of others), loans and advances including **bridging loans**, deposits (fixed/call, current for corporates, **NCDs**), **equipment leasing**, foreign exchange (letters of credit). *Corporate finance:* project financing and loan syndication, **issuing house**, investment and financial advisory (mergers, acquisitions).

| Feature | Merchant bank | Commercial bank |
|---|---|---|
| Type | Wholesale banker | Retail banker |
| Customers | Firms and institutions, large sums | Everyone: individuals, households, firms, government |
| Deposits | Mainly fixed | Demand, savings and fixed |
| Branches | Few, urban | Many, urban and rural |
| Clearing house | Traditionally not members | Members |
| Loans | Bulkier, medium term | Mostly short term |

- **Universal banking (2001 to 2010):** one bank could do commercial and merchant banking plus insurance and stockbroking. Advantages: removed the commercial/merchant dichotomy, created a **level playing field**, raised competition. Ended by the CBN's **Regulation on the Scope of Banking Activities (2010)**, which created licence types and confined banks to core banking (no insurance underwriting, no real estate except own premises, no non-permitted equity investment, no issuing currency) without CBN authorisation.
- **Licence categories:** commercial (**international, national, regional** authorisation), merchant, non-interest (national, regional). International = all states plus branches or subsidiaries abroad; national = all states, no offshore; regional = a limited set of states.
- **Non-interest (Islamic) banking:** no interest (**riba**); works on **profit-and-loss sharing**. Demand deposits earn nothing; investment (time) deposits get a **share of profit** at a ratio agreed in advance. Contracts: **Murabaha** (cost-plus sale with disclosed mark-up), **Ijarah** (leasing), **Mudarabah** (profit-sharing). **Jaiz Bank** is the pioneer; TAJ Bank and Lotus Bank followed.
- **Minimum capital history:** 1991 BOFID ₦50m commercial, ₦40m merchant; 1997 ₦500m both; 2001 universal ₦1bn (old) / ₦2bn (new); 2004 ₦2bn; **July 2004 consolidation: ₦25bn shareholders' funds by 31 December 2005**; 2010: regional ₦10bn, merchant ₦15bn, national ₦25bn, international ₦50bn.
- **Current (March 2024 programme, deadline 31 March 2026):** international commercial **₦500bn**, national **₦200bn**, regional **₦50bn**, merchant **₦50bn**, non-interest national **₦20bn**, regional **₦10bn**.
- **Present-day structure:** CBN at the apex; commercial, merchant and non-interest banks; MFBs; DFIs; primary mortgage institutions; other institutions. The **West African Currency Board** (Nigeria's currency issuer before the CBN, 1912 to 1959) is history, not part of today's structure.
- **Law:** **BOFIA 2020** (Banks and Other Financial Institutions Act) replaced BOFIA 1991.

**Explain more**

- **Consolidation** = banks merging, acquiring one another and raising fresh capital to meet a higher minimum. In 2004-2005, 89 banks became 25. Recapitalisation gives banks a **very strong capital base**. By the 2026 deadline the CBN reported 33 banks compliant, with a few still in regulatory or court processes.
- Musa's ₦4m van sold to him at ₦4.6m with the ₦600,000 mark-up disclosed (600,000 ÷ 4,000,000 × 100 = 15%), paid in instalments: **Murabaha**.
- Delta Foods needs cash now, to be repaid from a share issue in four months: **bridging loan**. A bank buying a machine and renting it to a firm: **equipment leasing**. Selling a client's new shares to the public: **issuing house**. **NCDs**: interbank instruments of **3 to 36 months** channelling commercial banks' surplus funds into merchant banks.
- Merchant banks are wholesale because they mobilise large funds from firms and institutions, not because they sell goods. Examples today: FBN Merchant Bank, Coronation Merchant Bank. International-licence examples: Access, Zenith, First Bank, UBA, GTBank.
- **Indigenisation:** in 1973 the Federal Government took **40%** of the three big expatriate banks (now First Bank, Union Bank, UBA), raised to **60% in 1976**.

### Lesson 5.3: Development finance institutions

**Must know**

- **Development banks (DFIs, specialised banks):** government-owned, set up **jointly by the Federal Government and the CBN**, to give **long-term finance to priority sectors** whose projects are too large or slow-maturing for conventional banks.
- **4 rationales:** (1) DMBs lend short because their deposits are short, leaving a **long-term finance gap**; (2) fund **socially desirable but commercially unprofitable** projects (housing, hence FMBN); (3) bridge the **information gap**, on-lending funds from the World Bank and African Development Bank; (4) push catalyst sectors (agriculture, industry) with big long-run multiplier effects.

| Institution | Year | Purpose |
|---|---|---|
| NIDB | 1964 (from ICON, 1959) | Long-term loans and equity to **medium and large** industry |
| NBCI | 1973 | Help Nigerians buy shares under the **Indigenisation (Enterprises Promotion) Decree 1972**; SMEs |
| NACB | 1973 as NAB, NACB 1978 | Agriculture and cooperatives |
| FMBN | 1977 (from Nigerian Building Society, 1956) | Housing finance; apex mortgage bank |
| NERFUND | 1989 | Foreign-currency funds for SMEs, on-lent through banks; a fund, not a bank |
| NEXIM (Nigerian Export-Import Bank) | 1991 (Act 38) | Export credit, **export credit insurance and guarantees** |
| UDBN | 1992 (Decree 51) | **Urban infrastructure**: markets, motor parks, mass transit, drainage, public toilets; now the Infrastructure Bank |
| BOI | 2001 | NIDB + NBCI + NERFUND |
| NACRDB, now **Bank of Agriculture** | 2000-2001 | NACB + Peoples Bank + FEAP |
| DBN | 2017 | Wholesale MSME funding through **participating financial institutions** |

- **NIDB functions:** identify and promote high value-added projects; mobilise funds and skills at home and abroad; long-term loans and equity. Services: loans to medium and large firms, equity stakes, World Bank funds for on-lending, information to foreign investors, technical help. It took **no current-account deposits**.
- **NBCI terms:** equity only if promoters provide **at least 30%**; loans repayable over **12 to 15 years**.
- **NACB aims:** food self-sufficiency, raw materials for industry, export crops for foreign exchange (all three).
- **BOI purposes:** empower SMEs, revitalise ailing industries with fresh funds and ideas, modernise and diversify enterprises not yet moribund. It is **not** a lender of last resort.
- **Mortgage system:** Mortgage Institutions Decree **53 of 1989** created a **two-tier** market: **FMBN apex** (wholesale funds, oversight), **primary mortgage institutions** second tier (take deposits, lend to people to buy, build or improve homes). FMBN's **regulatory role passed to the CBN** (1998); CBN now licenses and supervises PMIs. FMBN still **manages the National Housing Fund**.
- **NHF (Decree 3 of 1992):** workers contribute **2.5% of monthly basic salary**.
- **Mortgage problems:** title documents, weak capital, no long-term funds, low incomes, ignorance, poor manpower, poverty.

**Explain more**

- **Why the mergers:** the 1990s crises brought loan defaults and distress. A **2000 World Bank review** blamed non-commercial governance, **politicised lending**, poor credit discipline, poor internal direction, no diversification, **lack of quality staff**, inappropriate funding vehicles; 78% of loans were non-performing.
- NIDB = large industry; NBCI = small and medium firms and indigenisation.
- **NHF:** basic salary ₦180,000. Monthly = 180,000 × 2.5% = ₦4,500. Yearly = 4,500 × 12 = **₦54,000**.
- A cashew exporter fearing a European buyer's default goes to **NEXIM**. Funmi's bakery gets DBN money **through her commercial or microfinance bank**.
- African Continental Bank was a regional-government commercial bank, not a development bank.

### Lesson 5.4: Peoples Bank, community banks and microfinance banks

**Must know**

- **Specialised banks** for the urban poor and rural masses, giving funds in very small amounts.
- **Peoples Bank of Nigeria (1989):** loans **without collateral and without interest** (only a service charge), because the poor could not afford collateral or DMB interest rates. Repayment enforced by lending to members of **recognised trade groups** (peer pressure). **Challenges:** few skilled managers, high cost of many small loans, dependence on government funds, defaults, loss of funds placed with failed DMBs. Merged into NACRDB.
- **Community banks:** **Decree No. 46 of 1992**. Capital **₦250,000** (1992), raised to **₦5,000,000** (2003). Supervised by the **National Board for Community Banks (NBCB)**, which kept a fund for them to borrow in crisis. Same functions as commercial banks (deposits: savings, time, target; debentures; collections; ancillary services; credit) **except** (1) limited to their **own community**, (2) **no foreign exchange** without CBN approval, (3) **not in the clearing house**. Collapse causes: inflation, high costs, loan defaults, funds lost in distressed DMBs (not too much capital).
- **Microfinance Policy, December 2005 (launched 15 December):** community banks to **convert to microfinance banks within 24 months** by meeting new capital. Elements: states and LGAs set aside **at least 1% of annual budgets**; Microfinance Development Fund; **deposit insurance for MFBs**; consultative committee; accreditation; MFI database.
- **2005 categories:** **unit MFB (₦20m)** and **state MFB (₦1bn)**.
- **Goals of MFBs:** diversified, affordable, dependable services to the **economically active poor**; mobilise savings; create jobs and raise household income; channel government micro-credit; pay salaries, gratuities and pensions for government.
- **Objectives of the policy:** access for the excluded; mainstream the informal sector; serve MSMEs; rural transformation; **linkages** between universal/development banks and MFBs. CBN supervision also **enhances monetary stability**.
- **Revised tiers (2019):** Tier 1 unit ₦200m (urban), Tier 2 unit ₦50m (rural), state ₦1bn, national ₦5bn.
- **MFB prohibitions:** **foreign exchange**, real estate except own office, public-sector deposits (except for micro-credit programmes and payments).

**Explain more**

- MFBs do not finance multinationals' large projects or deal in dollars and domiciliary accounts; an Ibadan MFB selling US dollars is **not allowed**.
- History is tested: ₦250,000 (1992) to ₦5,000,000 (before conversion); December 2005; unit and state.

### Lesson 5.5: The NDIC and bank distress

**Must know**

- **NDIC:** set up by **Decree No. 22 of 1988**, began operations **1989**; law now the NDIC Act 2023 (replacing the 2006 Act). Linked to **SAP deregulation**, which swelled bank numbers (about 40 in 1986 to about 120 by the early 1990s).
- **Rationale:** (1) complement CBN supervision as banks multiplied; (2) protect depositors, **especially small ones**; (3) prevent bank failure and loss of confidence; (4) ensure **orderly exit** of failing banks, reducing disruption. **Not** to protect shareholders or government deposits.
- **Functions:** insure deposit liabilities; assist banks in difficulty (loans, guarantees, take over management, arrange mergers); guarantee payment to depositors; act as **liquidator** (after the CBN revokes the licence: pays insured depositors, then shares asset proceeds among depositors and creditors); help monetary authorities with banking policy. It does **not** issue currency or control money supply.
- **Premium:** **15/16 of 1%** of total deposit liabilities a year (flat rate, 1988; the 2006 Act capped it at 15/16 of 1%).
- **Cover per depositor:** ₦50,000 (1989) → ₦200,000 (2006) → **₦500,000 (2010)** → **₦5,000,000 for DMBs (2024)**; ₦2,000,000 for MFBs and primary mortgage banks. Balance above the cover is paid **pro rata as liquidation dividends**.
- **Subrogation:** once paid, the depositor's claim passes to the NDIC to that extent.
- **Sick bank:** cannot meet obligations to depositors/creditors as due, **or** losses have eroded or threaten shareholders' funds.
- **Supervision:** three concerns: **capital adequacy, loan concentration, liquidity ratio**. Capital adequacy 1:10 = **₦1 of capital supports at most ₦10 of loans**. **Off-site** = analysing periodic returns; **on-site** = examining books at the bank. NDIC and CBN examine jointly and share reports; under the 1988 Decree the CBN Governor chaired the NDIC Board (today the Board has its own chairman).
- **Distress:** severe financial, operational and managerial weakness leaving a bank unable to meet obligations as due. **CAMEL:** Capital adequacy, Asset quality, Management, Earnings, Liquidity.
- **Symptoms:** boardroom squabbles, customer complaints, borrowing above market rates, window dressing, **persistent adverse clearing positions**.
- **Causes:** *internal:* weak management, insider abuse, fraud, inadequate capital, poor asset-liability management. *External:* macroeconomic instability, political interference, weak laws, oil shocks.
- **Cures:** better management and internal controls, adequate capital, stable macro policy, good regulation, fraud reduction.
- **Resolution = rehabilitation or liquidation.** Options: (1) **moral suasion** (dialogue and persuasion); (2) **holding actions** (stop advertising for deposits, stop new loans, aggressive debt recovery, inject fresh capital, no new capital projects); (3) **change of management** (interim board); (4) **NDIC assumes control and management** (runs it through an interim board, restructures, sells or liquidates if a turnaround costs too much); (5) comprehensive supervisory intervention; (6) **purchase and assumption** (healthy bank buys assets and takes over deposits); (7) **bridge banks**; (8) **liquidation**, the **last option**.

**Explain more**

- **Premium:** deposits ₦64bn. 64,000,000,000 × 15/16 × 1/100 = **₦600,000,000**.
- **Payout:** deposit ₦7.2m, cover ₦5m. Insured payment = ₦5,000,000; remainder 7,200,000 − 5,000,000 = **₦2,200,000** claimed from liquidation dividends as assets are sold.
- **Capital adequacy:** ₦2bn capital at 1:10 supports at most 2bn × 10 = ₦20bn of loans.
- **Bridge banks (August 2011):** Mainstreet, Enterprise and Keystone took over Afribank, Spring Bank and Bank PHB.
- **Criticisms and replies:** premium too heavy and should be **risk-based** (reply: higher rates would crush weak banks the scheme protects); ₦50,000 too small (reply: comparable abroad, plus pro-rata dividends); exclude government securities (reply: they are **assets**, never insured deposits); scheme should be voluntary for big banks (reply: big banks fail too and their premiums **subsidise small banks**). Lack of offices in every state was **not** a criticism.
- **Achievements:** orderly resolution through rescues, sales, mergers and insured payouts. It never ended bank failure.

### Lesson 5.6: Other regulators and non-bank institutions

**Must know**

| Body | Regulates |
|---|---|
| CBN | Banks, MFBs, PMIs, BDCs, finance companies |
| NDIC | Deposit insurance |
| **SEC** | **Apex capital market regulator**: registers securities and operators, supervises exchanges, protects investors |
| **NAICOM** | Insurance companies, brokers, loss adjusters |
| **PenCom** | Pensions: licenses and oversees PFAs and custodians |

- **Pensions:** before 2004, civil service pensions were an **unfunded defined benefit** scheme paid from annual revenue. **Pension Reform Act 2004** created the **contributory** scheme (7.5% + 7.5%); **2014 Act**: **employer 10%, employee 8%**. Features: contributory; individual **retirement savings account (RSA)**; fully funded; managed by **PFAs**, assets held by **custodians (PFCs)**.
- **Finance companies:** leasing, hire purchase, factoring, LPO finance; licensed by CBN.
- **Bureaux de change (1989):** retail forex to small users; originally only **foreign bank notes, coins and buying travellers' cheques**.
- **Discount houses (from 1992, FSDH first):** **intermediaries between the CBN and banks**, trading in and holding Treasury Bills, commercial bills and short-term securities. Most later became merchant banks.
- **Unit trusts:** pool small savers' funds for diversified investment.
- **Insurance:** risk transfer plus financial intermediation; life and non-life.

**Explain more**

- A Lagos PFA seeking approval goes to **PenCom**, not SEC or NAICOM. FDI = **Foreign Direct Investment**.

### One-minute recap

- Intermediaries move savings to investment: pooling, maturity transformation, lower risk and cost.
- DMBs = commercial (retail) + merchant (wholesale; acceptances are their traditional function).
- Universal banking 2001-2010; then licence types. Capital: ₦25bn (2005); ₦500bn/₦200bn/₦50bn (2026). BOFIA 2020.
- BOI = NIDB + NBCI + NERFUND; Bank of Agriculture = NACB + Peoples Bank + FEAP.
- FMBN apex, PMIs second tier; NHF 2.5% of basic salary.
- Community banks (Decree 46, 1992) became MFBs (December 2005).
- NDIC 1988: premium 15/16 of 1%; cover ₦50,000 → ₦500,000 → ₦5m.
- Liquidation is the last resort; moral suasion is dialogue.
- SEC, NAICOM, PenCom regulate capital market, insurance, pensions.

### Theory practice

**Question:** A deposit money bank had total deposit liabilities of ₦48 billion. (a) What annual premium did it pay at 15/16 of 1%? (b) It later fails. A depositor holds ₦9 million. Using the ₦5 million cover, how is she paid?

*Solution:* (a) 48,000,000,000 × 15/16 × 1/100 = **₦450,000,000**. (b) The NDIC pays the insured **₦5,000,000**; the remaining 9,000,000 − 5,000,000 = **₦4,000,000** is claimed pro rata from liquidation dividends. Her claim passes to the NDIC for the ₦5m paid (subrogation).
