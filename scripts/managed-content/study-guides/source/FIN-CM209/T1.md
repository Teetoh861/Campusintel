## Topic 1: The Business of Banking and the Financial System

**What you must know:** what banking is and how it evolved; the functions, income, risks and regulation of commercial banks; credit creation and the money multiplier (with computations); the financial system, its components, functions and objectives; financial intermediation; non-bank financial institutions; financial markets; financial inclusion, innovation and globalisation.

### Lesson 1.1: Banking, its origin and the types of banks

**Must know**

- **Banking:** the business of accepting deposits from the public and lending or investing those funds (and providing payment and other financial services). Banks are intermediaries between savers (surplus units) and borrowers (deficit units).
- **Bank:** an institution whose main business is accepting deposits from the public and using them to grant loans and advances. Not a stock exchange, insurer or pension fund administrator.
- **"Bank"** comes from the Italian **banco**: the **bench or table** on which money changers did their business. Italians were the first bankers.

**Stages in the evolution of banking:**

1. Valuables (gold) kept for safety in **temples and places of worship** (ancient Rome, Greece, Mesopotamia, China, Japan).
2. **Money lending by merchants and money changers** (exchanged coins, later took deposits and gave loans).
3. **Goldsmiths** (17th-century England) accepting gold on deposit and issuing receipts.
4. Commercial (joint-stock) banking.
5. **Central banking** (issue of notes centralised, e.g. the Bank of England).

Barter replacing money is **not** a stage (money replaced barter).

- **Goldsmiths:** charged fees and gave receipts. They noticed that **depositors rarely withdrew all their gold at the same time**, so idle balances could be lent out at interest. They then paid interest on deposits to attract more. Their receipts circulated as payment and became **goldsmiths' notes**, the forerunners of **bank notes**.
- **IOU ("I owe you"):** an **informal written acknowledgement of a debt**; **not negotiable** (cannot be assigned, sold or traded). A **promissory note** is a **negotiable** instrument. Contents of an IOU: parties, payment information, signatures, termination date, (ideally) a lawyer's involvement.

**Types of banks**

| Type | Key feature |
|---|---|
| Central bank | Government-owned; regulates the banking system; does not serve the public (CBN) |
| Commercial bank | Accepts deposits, grants loans; income mainly the interest spread |
| Investment (merchant) bank | Capital-market work: underwriting, M&A advice, asset management, trading, research; income from **fees, commissions and profits on own investments** |
| Credit union | **Member-owned, not-for-profit cooperative**; members are owners and customers; better savings rates, lower fees |
| Savings and loan association | Savings deposits and mortgage loans |
| Development bank | Government-sponsored long-term finance for development |
| Microfinance bank | Small loans and savings for **low-income people and small businesses** |

**Explain more**

- Lending existed before goldsmiths; goldsmiths did not invent lending, but lending out part of deposited gold laid the foundation of modern banking. So it is fair to say goldsmiths "brought lending into banking".
- **Underwriting:** the investment bank buys a whole new issue at an agreed price and resells it, bearing the risk of unsold shares.
- Mama Ngozi, an unbanked Onitsha trader who opens a small savings account and later borrows ₦50,000, is a typical microfinance bank customer.
- Bank of Industry (BOI) was formed in **2001** by merging the **Nigerian Industrial Development Bank (NIDB)**, the Nigerian Bank for Commerce and Industry (NBCI) and NERFUND.
- Today banking in Nigeria is governed by the **Banks and Other Financial Institutions Act (BOFIA) 2020**, which replaced BOFIA 1991, and the **CBN Act 2007**. The CBN Governor is **Olayemi Cardoso** (since 2023). The 2024 recapitalisation set minimum capital of ₦500 billion (international), ₦200 billion (national), ₦50 billion (regional and merchant) and ₦20 billion / ₦10 billion (non-interest national / regional), with a deadline of **31 March 2026**.

### Lesson 1.2: What commercial banks do, how they earn, and their risks

**Must know**

**Two principal functions:** (1) **accepting deposits** from the public; (2) **granting loans and advances**.

- **Deposits:** **current (demand)** (withdraw any time by cheque, little or no interest); **savings** (interest, limited withdrawals); **fixed (time)** (fixed period and rate agreed at the start; early withdrawal loses interest; higher rate the longer the term).
- **Advances:** loans, **overdrafts** (draw beyond your balance up to a limit; interest only on the overdrawn amount) and **bills discounting** (buying a bill before maturity for less than face value, collecting the full value at maturity).

**Ancillary functions:**

1. **Agency:** collecting cheques, **dividends and interest**; paying insurance premiums and standing orders; buying and selling shares; remittances.
2. Acting as **executor, trustee or attorney** for customers' estates.
3. **Safe custody** of wills, jewellery and documents.
4. Other credit services: **debt factoring**, **loan syndication**, equipment leasing, direct credit, foreign exchange, advisory services.

Not commercial-bank functions: **issuing legal tender currency**, lender of last resort (both CBN), **writing wills** (lawyers write wills; banks only keep them or act as executors).

- **Debt factoring:** the bank **buys a customer's book debts at an agreed discount** and collects from the debtors.
- **Loan syndication:** a **lead bank** brings several banks together to finance a large project no single bank could conveniently fund.
- **Income:** mainly **net interest income** = interest on loans minus interest on deposits (the spread); plus fees and investment income.
- **Goals of deposit money banks:** **profitability, liquidity, solvency**.

**Risks**

| Risk | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Credit | Borrowers fail to repay | Floods ruin farmers who borrowed |
| Liquidity | Not enough cash for withdrawals | Funds locked in long mortgages; many withdraw at once |
| Interest rate | Rate changes hurt profit | |
| Operational | Failed processes, people, fraud, systems | Cashier and fraudsters; computer fails to flag transfers |
| Market | Losses from price, exchange-rate moves | |

**Regulation:** **prudential** (safety of each bank: capital, asset quality, liquidity); **consumer protection** (disclosure of charges, complaints resolution); **monetary policy regulation** (reserve ratios, rates). **Capital adequacy** = minimum capital **relative to risk-weighted assets**. **Deposit insurance** (NDIC) protects depositors up to a limit if a bank fails, preventing runs and keeping confidence.

**Explain more**

- **Worked example (net interest income):** loans ₦800m at 20%, deposits ₦1,000m at 8%. Interest earned = 20% × ₦800m = ₦160m. Interest paid = 8% × ₦1,000m = ₦80m. Net interest income = ₦160m − ₦80m = **₦80m**. (₦96m and ₦240m are traps.)
- **Worked example (overdraft):** Bisi has ₦100,000 credit and a ₦500,000 overdraft limit; she withdraws ₦400,000. Overdrawn amount = ₦400,000 − ₦100,000 = **₦300,000**; interest is charged only on this. At 24% a year simple interest for 3 months: ₦300,000 × 0.24 × 3/12 = **₦18,000**.
- **Worked example (bill discounting):** a ₦1,000,000 three-month bill is bought for ₦950,000. The bank's discount (income) = ₦1,000,000 − ₦950,000 = **₦50,000**.
- **Spread:** paying 3% on savings and charging 8% on loans gives a margin of 5%.
- **NDIC cover now (since 2024):** **₦5 million** per depositor in deposit money banks (also payment service banks and mobile-money pass-through), **₦2 million** in microfinance and primary mortgage banks (formerly ₦500,000 and ₦200,000).
- **CBN core functions** (so you can reject them as commercial-bank work): issuing legal tender, banker and adviser to government, banker to banks and **lender of last resort** (emergency cash to a sound bank facing a run), managing external reserves, monetary policy, supervision. The CBN does **not** take deposits from or lend to the public.
- **Direct monetary controls** (used before Nigeria moved to market-based management in 1993): credit ceilings, administered (fixed) interest rates, selective credit controls. **Open market operations** in treasury bills are **indirect** (market-based).

### Lesson 1.3: Credit creation and the money multiplier

**Must know**

- Banks create money **by lending**: each new loan creates a new **demand deposit**. They do **not** print notes or coins (only the CBN issues currency).
- Borrowing from banks abroad does not create money either: it only moves existing money from one holder to another. New money (deposits) arises only when a bank lends.
- **Assumptions:** (1) an initial **primary deposit in cash**; (2) only a **fraction** of each deposit is needed by depositors soon; (3) a fraction must by law be kept as **reserve**; (4) the rest, the **excess reserve**, is lent out; (5) credit can be created to a multiple of the initial deposit; (6) the multiple depends on the reserve ratio. Also: loans are redeposited in banks and there is no cash drain.

**Formulas** (r = cash reserve ratio as a decimal):

- Required reserve = r × deposit
- Excess reserve (amount lent at first stage) = deposit − required reserve = deposit × (1 − r)
- Money (deposit, credit) multiplier: k = 1 / r
- Total deposits created (including the initial deposit) = initial deposit × k = initial deposit / r
- New (derived) deposits = total deposits − initial deposit
- Reserve ratio from totals: r = initial deposit / total deposits; multiplier k = total / initial

- The multiplier is the **inverse** (reciprocal) of the reserve ratio. Reserve ratio and total credit (and the multiplier) are **inversely** related.
- **Limits to credit creation:** (1) **size of the initial cash deposit**; (2) **reserve ratio**; (3) **leakages** (cash kept outside banks). Also: shortage of creditworthy borrowers or lack of collateral, the public's preference for holding cash, fear of cash drain, and **central bank contractionary policy**. Borrowers **redepositing** loans in other banks does **not** limit creation; it keeps it going.

**Explain more**

- **Process, ₦1,000m at 25%:** Bank X keeps ₦250m, lends **₦750m**. Bank Y receives ₦750m, keeps ₦187.5m, lends ₦562.5m. Bank Z receives ₦562.5m, keeps ₦140.625m, lends ₦421.875m. Total deposits = ₦1,000m × 1/0.25 = **₦4,000m**. At 40%: ₦1,000m ÷ 0.40 = **₦2,500m**. At 10%: ₦10,000m.
- **₦2,000,000 at 20%:** total = 2,000,000 ÷ 0.20 = **₦10,000,000** (new deposits ₦8,000,000 is the trap).
- **₦5,000 at 25%:** k = 1 ÷ 0.25 = **4**; total = 5,000 × 4 = **₦20,000**.
- **₦500 at 15%:** k = 1 ÷ 0.15 = **6.67** (2 d.p.); total = 500 ÷ 0.15 = **₦3,333.33**; required reserve = 0.15 × 500 = ₦75; excess reserve = 500 − 75 = **₦425**.
- **₦1,000 at 15%:** 1,000 ÷ 0.15 = ₦6,666.67, about **₦6,667**.
- **Finding r:** ₦15m creates ₦240m: k = 240 ÷ 15 = 16, r = 1 ÷ 16 = **6.25%**. ₦35m creates ₦280m: r = 35 ÷ 280 = **12.5%**. ₦50m creates ₦500m: k = **10**, r = 10%.
- **Percentage changes (trap):** "increased by 100%" means **doubled**; "increased by 50%" means × 1.5 (10% becomes 15%, not 60%); "reduced by 50%" means halved; "decreased by 25%" means × 0.75; "increased by 25%" means × 1.25; "increased by 40%" means × 1.4.

| Case | New r | New initial deposit | Total created |
|---|---|---|---|
| ₦15m, 6.25% doubled | 12.5% | ₦15m | 15 ÷ 0.125 = ₦120m |
| ₦15m down 25%, 6.25% | 6.25% | ₦11.25m | 11.25 ÷ 0.0625 = ₦180m |
| ₦15m down 25%, r doubled | 12.5% | ₦11.25m | 11.25 ÷ 0.125 = ₦90m |
| ₦35m, 12.5% doubled | 25% | ₦35m | 35 ÷ 0.25 = ₦140m |
| ₦35m up 25%, 12.5% | 12.5% | ₦43.75m | 43.75 ÷ 0.125 = ₦350m |
| ₦35m down 25%, r doubled | 25% | ₦26.25m | 26.25 ÷ 0.25 = ₦105m |
| ₦10m at 10% | 10% | ₦10m | 10 ÷ 0.10 = ₦100m |
| ₦10m, 10% up 50% | 15% | ₦10m | 10 ÷ 0.15 = ₦66.67m |
| ₦10m, 10% down 50% | 5% | ₦10m | 10 ÷ 0.05 = ₦200m |
| ₦50m up 40%, 10% doubled | 20% | ₦70m | 70 ÷ 0.20 = ₦350m |

- To create more money banks must **lend out more** of their deposits; raising their cash ratio with the CBN reduces creation.

### Lesson 1.4: The financial system and financial intermediation

**Must know**

- **Financial system:** the network of **financial institutions, markets, instruments and regulations** that **channels funds from surplus units (savers) to deficit units (borrowers)**. The 1976 Committee on the Nigerian Financial System called it "the congeries of financial institutions and arrangements which serve the needs of an economy". It operates at firm, national and global levels.
- **4 components:** financial **institutions**, financial **markets**, financial **instruments**, financial **services**.
- **Instruments:** **equity** (shares), **debt** (bonds, debentures, loans), **derivative** (options, futures, forwards, swaps), **hybrid** (**convertible bonds**, preference shares).
- **3 basic types of institutions:** **bank financial institutions** (BFIs), **non-bank financial institutions** (NBFIs), **regulatory and supervisory institutions**.
- **Regulators:** CBN, Federal Ministry of Finance, **NDIC**, **SEC** (capital market), **NAICOM** (insurance), **PenCom** (pensions), FMBN (formerly mortgage institutions), FSRCC (coordinates regulators). NDIC and the **Debt Management Office** are **not** NBFIs.

**Functions of the financial system (8):** mobilisation of savings; efficient capital allocation; **liquidity provision** (turning assets into cash quickly without significant loss of value); risk management and transfer; price discovery and information; payment and settlement; monetary policy transmission; promoting financial inclusion. The seven functions of financial markets (Lesson 1.6: savings mobilisation, storage of wealth, liquidity, credit, payments, protection against risk, monetary policy) are also functions of the financial system. Fixing the prices of goods and services is **not** a function.

**Objectives of the Nigerian financial system (8):**

1. Provide **non-inflationary** support to the economy.
2. Facilitate effective management of the economy.
3. Assist in transforming the **rural community**.
4. Achieve greater **mobilisation of savings** and channel them efficiently.
5. Ensure that **no viable project is frustrated simply for lack of funds**.
6. Protect the economy from the vicissitudes of the international economy.
7. Sustain indigenisation and management of the economy.
8. Achieve greater integration and linkage in agriculture.

**Services:** intermediation, resource mobilisation, resource allocation, efficient payments, foreign exchange for trade.

- **Direct finance:** borrower raises funds straight from savers in the financial market (e.g. a company selling ₦5 billion of bonds to households). **Indirect finance (financial intermediation):** through an intermediary; the main route.
- **Financial intermediaries:** the general name for institutions that move funds from surplus to deficit units (BFIs and NBFIs both do this).
- **Weaknesses of direct financing:** much **time and effort** to find a lender; **high cost** (price) of funds; **short period**; **high risk** to the lender.
- **Advantages of intermediaries:** access to **bulky sums** (consortium if needed); **lower risk** per saver (pooling); **lower cost**; **longer term**.
- **How intermediaries promote savings, investment and capital formation:** by **pooling funds from surplus units and channelling them into productive investment** by deficit units; reducing risk, mobilising idle funds, giving liquidity and income to savers, and allocating funds expertly.
- **Why savers trust intermediaries:** they **pay interest**, have a **reputation to protect**, and let savers **withdraw when needed** (safety plus liquidity).
- **Success factors:** (1) **trust and confidence**; (2) **convenience** (little bureaucracy, nearness); (3) **price** (interest, fees). Loss of trust leads to **financial disintermediation** (mass withdrawal of funds).

**Explain more**

- **Maturity (securities) transformation:** banks lend for five years though most depositors leave money for months; they **bridge the different maturity (capital lock-up) periods**. NBFIs do it by buying long-term primary securities and issuing their own indirect securities with different maturities. Other NBFI services: **aggregation**, accessibility, specialisation, risk reduction.
- **Bank vs NBFI:** bank **liabilities (demand deposits withdrawable by cheque) are spendable as money**, so banks can create credit; NBFI liabilities are not. Both mobilise surplus funds for deficit units.
- **Demand-following hypothesis:** finance grows **in response to demand** from a growing real economy (colonial Nigeria, dominated by expatriate banks financing export trade). **Supply-leading:** institutions are **set up ahead of demand** to mobilise savings and stimulate growth (Patrick, 1966). Do not confuse either with financial repression (rates fixed below market, credit rationed).
- **Factors in the development of Nigeria's system:** independence, the CBN (1958), civil war, oil boom, government financing needs, monetary policy changes, regulation and deregulation, the 1976 Financial System Review Committee, the 1972 Indigenisation Decree, SAP (1986), NDIC, EFCC, 2004 bank consolidation, 2009 reform.

### Lesson 1.5: Non-bank financial institutions

**Must know**

NBFIs are **heterogeneous**: many different kinds, each with its own functions: insurers, pension funds and PFAs, finance companies, primary mortgage institutions, bureaux de change, discount houses, unit trusts, cooperatives.

- **Insurance:** two major types, **life** and **non-life**. Two functions: **risk transfer** and **financial intermediation**. Life covers loss from **death, disability or old age**; long-term; variants: **whole life** (paid on death) and **endowment assurance** (paid at the end of the term if alive, or to beneficiaries on earlier death). Non-life (**property**: fire, theft, marine; **liability**: negligence) is usually **yearly**, so non-life funds go into short-term assets and life funds into long-term assets. Regulator: **NAICOM** (since 1997).
- **NPF/NSITF:** the **National Provident Fund (1961)**, for private-sector workers, was replaced by the **Nigeria Social Insurance Trust Fund (NSITF) in 1993 by Decree No. 73**, because of **poor performance** (poor records, employers not remitting contributions). NSITF's **7 areas:** retirement pension, retirement grant, invalidity pension, invalidity grant, survivors' pension, survivors' grant, funeral grant.
- **Pension Reform Act 2004:** created the **National Pension Commission (PenCom)**, plus **Pension Fund Administrators (PFAs)** and **Pension Fund Custodians (PFCs)**. Each worker opens a **Retirement Savings Account (RSA) with a PFA of his choice**; the PFA invests, the **PFC keeps the assets in custody**. PenCom **licenses and supervises PFAs and PFCs**.
- **Defined contribution:** benefit depends on **contributions plus investment returns** in the RSA (worker bears investment risk). **Defined benefit:** benefit **fixed in advance by formula** (final salary, years of service, rank).
- **Primary mortgage institutions:** set up under **Decree No. 53 of 1989** to mobilise savings and give housing loans; first regulated by the **Federal Mortgage Bank of Nigeria (FMBN)**; the CBN took over in the late 1990s. Now called primary mortgage banks, regulated by the CBN.
- **Finance houses/companies:** borrow **placements** and lend at higher rates; services include **LPO financing**, leasing, project financing, debt factoring; under CBN oversight since 1991 (CBN Decree 24, BOFI Decree 25); may not take public deposits.
- **Bureaux de change (1989):** deal in foreign exchange for **small users**. Purposes: cater for small users; bring the **parallel (black) market** under official purview and regulate it; eliminate the parallel-market premium by merging the rates. Also: give legal recognition to small dealers, free access for small buyers, better forex statistics, less pressure on the official market. Not for big corporate allocations, deposits or overdrafts. CBN oversight since 1997.
- **Discount houses (1993):** dealt mainly in **short-term government securities** in the money market, between banks and the CBN.
- **Unit trusts:** pool many small savers' funds into a **diversified portfolio**, with an independent trustee.

**Explain more**

- **Current position:** the **Pension Reform Act 2014** replaced the 2004 Act (minimum contributions 10% employer, 8% employee; the 2004 Act had 7.5% each). NSITF now runs the employees' compensation scheme. Discount houses were phased out in the mid-2010s, several becoming merchant banks.
- Finance houses failed in the mid-1990s from speculation, rates up to 60%, loans without collateral and fund mismatch (short placements in fixed assets).

### Lesson 1.6: Financial markets; inclusion, innovation and globalisation

**Must know**

- **Financial market:** institutional arrangements that **mobilise funds from surplus units and channel them to deficit units**. **7 functions:** savings mobilisation, storage of wealth, liquidity, credit, payments system, **protection against risk**, executing monetary policy. Not: fixing prices or issuing currency.

| Money market | Capital market |
|---|---|
| Short-term funds (up to one year) | Medium- to long-term funds |
| Treasury bills, commercial paper, bankers' acceptances, call money | Shares (equities), bonds, **debentures** |
| Liquidity, working capital | Expansion, factories (e.g. 15-year funds) |

- **Primary market:** **new issues**; the **only segment where capital formation occurs**. **Secondary market:** trading of **existing** securities.
- **Capital market:** market for raising medium- to long-term funds for investment. Principal institutions: **SEC** (apex regulator) and the stock exchange. Participants include issuing houses, stockbrokers, **underwriters**, registrars; finance houses are **not** capital-market participants.
- **Benefit of a capital market to Nigeria:** it **channels medium- to long-term funds to firms and governments** for expansion and development projects.
- **Financial dualism:** organised and unorganised (informal: esusu, ajo, moneylenders) markets existing side by side.
- Bond prices move **inversely** with interest rates: a rise in the price of fixed-interest securities means the market rate has **fallen**.

**Explain more**

- **Current names:** the Nigerian Stock Exchange (NSE) demutualised in **2021** into **Nigerian Exchange Group**, with **Nigerian Exchange Limited (NGX)** as the exchange. SEC now operates under the **Investments and Securities Act 2025** (replaced ISA 2007).
- **Financial inclusion:** all sections of society, especially low-income and vulnerable groups, have **access to appropriate, affordable financial services**. Helped by **agent banking, mobile money and simple low-cost accounts** in underserved areas (Nigeria's National Financial Inclusion Strategy dates from 2012). Collateral for savings accounts or closing rural branches does the opposite.
- **Financial innovation:** new financial products, services or processes. **Globalisation:** growing interconnection of countries through flows of goods, services, finance, people and ideas.

### One-minute recap

- Banking = taking deposits and lending; "bank" from Italian banco (bench); goldsmiths brought lending in; receipts became bank notes.
- Principal functions: deposits and advances; agency (collect dividends); safe custody; no currency issue, no will writing.
- Net interest income = interest earned − interest paid; overdraft interest only on the overdrawn amount.
- Risks: credit, liquidity, interest rate, operational, market.
- k = 1/r; total deposits = initial ÷ r; excess reserve = deposit × (1 − r); "increased by 100%" = doubled.
- Limits: initial deposit, reserve ratio, leakages.
- Financial system = institutions, markets, instruments, services; 3 institution types: BFIs, NBFIs, regulators.
- Intermediation succeeds on trust, convenience, price; loss of trust = disintermediation.
- NPF 1961 → NSITF 1993 (Decree 73); PRA 2004 → PenCom, PFAs, PFCs; PMIs 1989 under FMBN.
- Money market short-term, capital market long-term; primary market = capital formation; SEC is the capital-market apex regulator.

### Theory practice

**Question:** A bank receives a fresh cash deposit of ₦4,000,000. The cash reserve ratio is 20%. Find (a) the excess reserve of the first bank, (b) the money multiplier, (c) the total deposits the banking system can create, and (d) the total if the reserve ratio is increased by 25%.

*Solution:*
(a) Required reserve = 0.20 × ₦4,000,000 = ₦800,000; excess reserve = ₦4,000,000 − ₦800,000 = **₦3,200,000**.
(b) k = 1 ÷ 0.20 = **5**.
(c) Total = ₦4,000,000 × 5 = **₦20,000,000** (new deposits ₦16,000,000).
(d) New r = 20% × 1.25 = 25%; total = ₦4,000,000 ÷ 0.25 = **₦16,000,000**.
