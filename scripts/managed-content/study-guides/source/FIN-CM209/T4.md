## Topic 4: The Central Bank of Nigeria

**What you must know:** what a central bank is and its canons; the WACB and the road to the CBN (1958 Act, 1 July 1959); the CBN Act 2007, Board, Governor, Deputy Governors and MPC; traditional and developmental functions; monetary policy, its objectives and instruments with simple computations; the CBN-led reforms of 2004, 2009, 2010 and 2024.

### Lesson 4.1: Central banking, its canons and the birth of the CBN

**Must know**

- **Central bank:** the apex monetary authority of a country, with the sole right to issue currency and responsibility for regulating the banking and monetary system. It is the government's bank and the bankers' bank; it deals mainly with government and banks, not the public.
- In Nigeria the apex monetary authority is the **Central Bank of Nigeria (CBN)**, headquartered in Abuja.

**Canons (principles) of central banking:**

1. It must be the **sole bank of issue**.
2. It must **not do ordinary commercial banking or compete with commercial banks** (no profit-seeking deposits from the public); it is a bankers' bank and the government's bank.
3. It controls financial institutions with **monetary tools** (bank rate, open market operations and others).
4. It keeps **some assets in liquid form** so it can discount short-term commercial and government bills.
5. It should be **free from government interference** (independent).

Also accepted as central banking principles: acting as **lender of last resort** and being responsible for the **stability of the financial system**.

**West African Currency Board (WACB):** set up in **1912** (after the **Lord Emmott** committee of 1911) to issue sterling-backed currency for four British colonies: Nigeria, Gold Coast (Ghana), Sierra Leone, The Gambia. Based in London; the **Bank of British West Africa** was its currency distributor in Nigeria. Before the CBN, the WACB issued Nigeria's legal tender.

| Advantages of the WACB | Disadvantages of the WACB |
|---|---|
| A unified currency standard accepted by the people | One currency for four colonies caused problems as they became independent at different dates |
| Relieved colonial governments of currency management | 100% sterling backing invested abroad deprived the colonies of earning assets at home |
| Removed inflation and foreign exchange crises | Failed to train Africans in monetary management |
| Paved the way for easy acceptance of new currencies at independence | No independent monetary policy: Nigeria's money supply simply moved with its sterling (trade) earnings |

**Road to the CBN (the three missions):**

1. **1952:** K. O. Mbadiwe's motion in the House of Representatives asked for a central bank.
2. **Fisher Report, 1953** (J. L. Fisher, Bank of England adviser): against a central bank then. "The financial environment hardly exists in Nigeria for the establishment of a Central Bank." Reasons: the financial system was **too underdeveloped/narrow** for a central bank to control the money supply, and **trained staff were lacking**.
3. **World Bank (IBRD) Mission** (report 1955): too early for a full central bank; recommended a **State Bank of Nigeria** that could later become a central bank.
4. **Loynes Report, 1957** (J. B. Loynes, Bank of England): **favoured** a central bank; its recommendations formed the draft bill presented to the House in **March 1958**.

There was **no "Johnson's Commission 1954"**.

- **CBN Act (No. 24) of 1958** established the CBN. It **commenced operations on 1 July 1959**.
- First Governor: **Roy Pentelow Fenton** (1958-1963). First Nigerian Governor: **Aliyu Mai-Bornu** (1963-1967), followed by **Clement Isong** (1967-1975). Current Governor (2026): **Olayemi Cardoso**, appointed 2023.

**Explain more**

- By 1956 (self-rule) the government was persuaded a central bank was needed to **harness the financial resources of the economy, give leadership and direction to banks and other financial institutions, and create institutions, instruments and markets** for the growth of the financial system. If asked which of these, the answer is "all of the above".
- Watch the dates: **Act 1958, operations 1959**. Options like 1 October 1960 (independence) or 1 July 1958 are traps.

### Lesson 4.2: Legal framework, objects and organisational structure

**Must know**

- **Laws:** CBN Act 1958, replaced by CBN Decree No. 24 of 1991, amended 1993, 1997, 1998, 1999, now the **CBN Act 2007** (in force). The CBN also administers the **Banks and Other Financial Institutions Act (BOFIA) 2020**, which replaced BOFIA 1991.

**Principal objects under the CBN Act 2007 (5):**

1. Ensure **monetary and price stability** (the first and primary object).
2. Issue **legal tender currency** in Nigeria.
3. Maintain **external reserves** to safeguard the international value of the currency.
4. Promote a **sound financial system** in Nigeria.
5. Act as **banker and provide economic and financial advice to the Federal Government**.

**Board of Directors (apex of the structure):** chaired by the **Governor**; members are the Governor, the **4 Deputy Governors**, **5 non-executive directors**, the **Permanent Secretary of the Federal Ministry of Finance** and the **Accountant-General of the Federation**.

- **Governor** (and Deputy Governors): appointed by the **President, subject to Senate confirmation**, for **5 years in the first instance, renewable once** for not more than 5 years.
- **4 Deputy Governors (2007 Act):**

| Deputy Governor | Departments include |
|---|---|
| Economic Policy | Monetary Policy, Research, Statistics, Financial Markets |
| Financial System Stability | **Banking Supervision**, Other Financial Institutions Supervision |
| Operations | Banking Services, Currency Operations, Reserve Management, Payments System Management |
| Corporate Services | Human Resources, Finance |

**Monetary Policy Committee (MPC):** formulates **monetary and credit policy**. Set up formally by the CBN Act 2007.

- Members (**12**): Governor (Chairman) 1 + Deputy Governors 4 + Board members 2 + President's appointees 3 + Governor's appointees 2 = **12**.
- Must meet **at least four times a year**; in practice it meets about every two months.

**Licensing under BOFIA:** to carry on banking business you must apply **in writing to the Governor of the CBN** for a licence. A bank may open or close a branch only with the **prior written consent of the CBN**. Mergers and acquisitions also need the Governor's prior approval.

**Why banks are heavily regulated (8):** protect depositors; protect customers from unfair dealing; promote monetary stability (control cost, volume and availability of credit); win public confidence; keep banks solvent; prevent crises that would damage the economy; safety of deposits; develop an integrated, efficient financial system. Regulation does **not** guarantee every bank a profit.

**Explain more**

- The 2007 Act gave the CBN greater autonomy.
- You may see the MPC described as "2 CBN appointees and 5 presidential appointees"; that split is not the Act's, but the total is still 12. The 2007 Act wording is 2 Board members, 3 presidential and 2 Governor's appointees.
- The NDIC (1988, operations 1989) shares bank oversight but is a separate body.

### Lesson 4.3: Traditional functions of the CBN

**Must know**

The CBN's functions fall into three groups: **traditional**, **regulatory (supervisory)** and **developmental** (Lesson 4.5).

**Five major functions under the 1958 Act:** (1) issue currency notes and coins (legal tender); (2) banker to the government; (3) banker to other banks; (4) manage the country's external reserves; (5) manage domestic monetary policy. Today you also add **regulation and supervision** of banks and other financial institutions, and **financial adviser to government**.

1. **Issue of legal tender:** sole issuer of the naira. The CBN **issues** currency; **Nigerian Security Printing and Minting Plc prints and mints** it.
2. **Banker to government:** keeps federal, state and local government accounts; **manages the public debt** (issues Treasury Bills, Treasury Certificates, development stocks); grants **Ways and Means advances** (temporary direct advances to the FG to cover revenue shortfalls); runs the **Treasury Single Account (TSA)**: all MDAs close separate bank accounts and pay receipts into one consolidated account at the CBN.
3. **Banker to banks:** keeps banks' current/reserve accounts, provides **clearing** facilities, rediscounts their bills, and is **lender of last resort**: emergency funds to a **solvent but temporarily illiquid** bank.
4. **External reserves:** holds gold and convertible currencies to safeguard the naira's international value.
5. **Monetary stability and a sound financial system:** monetary policy plus licensing, examination (on-site and off-site) and enforcement.

**Things the CBN does NOT do:** accept deposits from or lend to individuals, households or businessmen; insure deposits or pay depositors of failed banks (**NDIC**); register stockbrokers or list shares (**SEC** and the Exchange); register companies (**CAC**); collect taxes; examine and qualify bankers (**CIBN**). A central bank is **not** a merchant bank.

| Central bank | Commercial bank |
|---|---|
| One per country; apex of the system | Many in one country |
| Monopoly of currency issue | Cannot issue currency |
| Not profit-driven | Main objective is profit |
| Deals with government and banks | Deals with the public |
| **Controls** credit | **Creates** credit |

**Explain more**

- **Currency history:** Nigerian pound issued **1959**; **1 January 1973** decimalisation: the **naira and kobo**, at **₦2 = £1**; **₦20 note 1977** honouring **General Murtala Mohammed**; ₦1,000 note 2005.
- **Reserves history:** sterling backing received from the WACB in 1959; from **1973** held in as many traded currencies as possible (not only US dollars and pounds) to **earn new income and reduce losses** from a fall in any one currency.
- **Domiciliary accounts:** since deregulation, exporters and others may keep foreign exchange in domiciliary accounts with banks. Two types: **Export Domiciliary Account** and **Ordinary Domiciliary Account**.
- **Ways and Means limit:** the CBN Act 2007 caps outstanding advances at **5% of the FG's previous year's actual revenue** (the 1991 Decree said 12.5% of estimated recurrent revenue). Example: previous year's revenue ₦10 trillion; limit = 5% × ₦10,000 billion = ₦500 billion. Heavy deficit financing by the CBN pumps money into the economy and weakens its control of prices: this is **fiscal dominance**.

### Lesson 4.4: Monetary policy and its instruments

**Must know**

- **Monetary policy:** the use of the central bank's control over the **quantity, availability and cost of money and credit** to achieve general economic objectives. Not fiscal policy, which uses **taxation, government expenditure and public debt** (Federal Ministry of Finance).
- Monetary and fiscal policy are both **economic stabilisation policies**.
- **Four objectives:** (1) **stable prices**, (2) **high growth rate of output**, (3) **full employment**, (4) **balance of payments equilibrium**. Secondary: stable exchange rate, income redistribution. Population growth and bank profits are **not** objectives. In practice the CBN's primary focus is **price stability**.
- **Stable prices:** the general price level avoids wide fluctuations and changes only gradually (not frozen prices).
- **Full employment:** all able and willing to work find jobs; some frictional and seasonal unemployment remains.
- **Types:** **expansionary** (more money and credit; for recession) and **contractionary** (less; for inflation).

**Instruments**

| Quantitative (general, traditional) | Qualitative (selective) |
|---|---|
| Open market operations (OMO) | Moral suasion |
| Discount rate / bank rate (now MPR) | Selective credit control |
| Reserve requirements (cash reserve ratio, liquidity ratio) | Margin requirement |
| Special deposits | Loan (credit) ceiling |

- **OMO:** buying and selling government (or CBN) securities to change bank reserves and money supply. **Sell** = withdraw money (contract). **Buy** = inject money (expand). Main **indirect, market-based** tool since **June 1993**. Uses Nigerian Treasury Bills, bonds and **CBN Bills** (issued by the CBN itself to mop up liquidity).
- **Repo:** CBN sells and agrees to buy back = **temporarily withdraws** liquidity. **Reverse repo:** temporarily injects.
- **Discount rate = bank rate:** the rate at which the central bank lends to banks or rediscounts their eligible bills. Lower it to expand credit; raise it to contract. In Nigeria the **Minimum Rediscount Rate (MRR)** was replaced in **December 2006** by the **Monetary Policy Rate (MPR)**, the signalling rate.
- **Standing facilities corridor:** **SLF** (Standing Lending Facility) = overnight borrowing from the CBN against collateral, **above** MPR. **SDF** (Standing Deposit Facility) = overnight deposit with the CBN, **below** MPR. Formulas: SLF rate = MPR + upper band; SDF rate = MPR − lower band (100 basis points = 1%).
- **Cash reserve ratio (CRR):** minimum % of deposits a bank must keep **with the CBN**. **Liquidity ratio:** minimum % of deposit liabilities held in **liquid assets** (cash in vault, balances with the CBN, Treasury Bills). Raise either: excess reserves fall, lending and money supply contract.
- **Simple deposit multiplier = 1 ÷ reserve ratio.** Maximum lendable from new deposits = deposits × (1 − CRR). Liquidity shortfall = (liquidity ratio × deposits) − liquid assets held.
- **Special deposits:** cash kept with the central bank **over and above** required reserves, **not counted** toward them.
- **Moral suasion:** persuasion or friendly appeal to banks to act in the national interest, **without legal compulsion**. It is the classic **non-market** instrument.
- **Selective credit control:** administrative guidelines directing credit to sectors in set amounts at set rates (pre-**1986 SAP**, to agriculture and manufacturing).
- **Margin requirement:** fixes how much security is needed for advances, to stop lending against commodities hoarded for speculation. **Loan ceiling:** total advances may not rise above the level reached on a date the CBN names.
- **Direct controls of bank liquidity:** supplementary reserves, variable cash ratio, variable liquidity ratio.

**Current position (September 2026 MPC):** MPR **23%**; corridor **+50/−300** basis points; CRR **45%** for deposit money banks, **16%** merchant banks, **75%** on non-TSA public sector deposits; liquidity ratio **30%**.

**Explain more**

- Interest rates and spending move oppositely: **higher rates lead to lower spending; lower rates to higher spending**. To pump money in, the CBN **reduces** the discount rate or MPR, **buys** securities, or **cuts** reserve ratios. To fight inflation it **sells** securities (OMO), **raises** MPR or reserve ratios, uses selective credit control; a **surplus budget** is a fiscal anti-inflation tool. **Cutting the MPR is not** an anti-inflation tool. Taxation and budgeting are fiscal, not monetary.
- Reserve requirements change the **multiplier**; OMO changes the **monetary base**.
- **Direct controls (1959-1986):** credit ceilings, sectoral allocation, fixed interest rates. **Market-based era** from SAP (1986), OMO from 1993.

**Worked example 1 (multiplier):** CRR 10%: multiplier = 1 ÷ 0.10 = 10, so ₦10,000 of new cash supports ₦10,000 × 10 = ₦100,000 of deposits. If the CRR rises to 20%: multiplier = 1 ÷ 0.20 = 5, supporting ₦10,000 × 5 = ₦50,000. Raising the CRR from 10% to 20% cuts the multiplier from 10 to 5.

**Worked example 2 (CRR):** CRR 40%, new deposits ₦250 billion. Required reserve = 40% × ₦250bn = ₦100bn. Maximum lending = ₦250bn − ₦100bn = **₦150bn**.

**Worked example 3 (liquidity ratio):** Ekiti Trust Bank: deposits ₦600bn, liquid assets ₦150bn, liquidity ratio 30%. Required = 30% × ₦600bn = ₦180bn. Shortfall = ₦180bn − ₦150bn = **₦30bn**.

**Worked example 4 (corridor):** MPR 20%, corridor +500/−100 bps. SLF = 20% + 5% = **25%**; SDF = 20% − 1% = **19%**. September 2026: SLF = 23% + 0.5% = 23.5%; SDF = 23% − 3% = 20%.

### Lesson 4.5: Developmental functions of the CBN

**Must know**

- **Developmental functions:** roles beyond the traditional ones, suited to a developing economy, to build the financial system and finance key sectors. Example: special financing schemes for agriculture and industry.

**Three major developmental functions:**

1. **Provision of financial infrastructure:** creating **markets and instruments**: Treasury Bills (1960), call money fund scheme (1962), Treasury Certificates (1968), certificates of deposit, bankers' acceptances, discount houses (1993); encouraged the **Lagos Stock Exchange (1961)**; helped found NIDB, the NDIC and SEC.
2. **Provision of special funds and schemes** (see below).
3. **Provision of information and training:** publications (**Statistical Bulletin, Annual Report, CBN Briefs, Economic and Financial Review**, Bullion), seminars, surveys and the **Monetary Policy Forum** (from 2000); manpower development.

**Special funds and schemes:**

| Scheme | Key facts |
|---|---|
| **Rural Banking Scheme** (1977, 4 phases to 1993) | Commercial banks **directed to open branches in rural areas** to mobilise rural savings |
| **Agricultural Credit Guarantee Scheme Fund (ACGSF)** (1977) | Guarantees **75% of the net amount in default** on agricultural loans; share capital subscribed 60% Federal Government, 40% CBN |
| **NERFUND** (National Economic **Reconstruction** Fund) | Medium/long-term funds, partly in foreign exchange; merged into Bank of Industry (2001) |
| **SMIEIS** (Small and Medium Industries Equity Investment Scheme) | CBN initiative via the **Bankers' Committee**: banks set aside **10% of profit before tax** for equity in small and medium industries |
| **Rediscounting and Refinancing Facility (RRF)** (1987) | Lets banks rediscount or refinance **export** credit |
| **Foreign Input Facility (FIF)** (1989) | Helps producers get foreign exchange to **import capital goods and raw materials** |
| **Anchor Borrowers' Programme** (2015) and others (Real Sector Support Facility, MSMEDF) | Development finance interventions for farmers and the real sector |

**Explain more**

- RRF and FIF passed to **NEXIM** in 1991. There is **no** "National Economic Rejuvenation Fund".
- **Community banking (1990)** replaced rural banking and later became **microfinance banking**.
- The CBN helped set up development banks: NIDB 1964 (now Bank of Industry), NACB 1973 (now Bank of Agriculture), Federal Mortgage Bank 1977.
- **Manpower:** the CBN chairs the Bankers' Committee and supports the **CIBN** and the **Financial Institutions Training Centre (FITC)**, seconds staff and runs its own training programmes.
- **Payments:** cashless policy (2012); **Bank Verification Number (BVN)**, launched with the Bankers' Committee in **February 2014**, gives each customer one biometric identity across all banks.
- Since 2023-2024 the CBN under Cardoso has scaled back its intervention lending to concentrate on price stability.
- Paying insured depositors of failed banks is the **NDIC's** work, not a CBN achievement.

### Lesson 4.6: CBN-led banking reforms

**Must know**

- **Consolidation:** a reduction in the number of banks, with the remaining banks becoming **larger and more concentrated**.
- **2004-2005 consolidation:** announced **6 July 2004** by Governor **Charles Soludo** to the Bankers' Committee. Minimum capital raised from **₦2 billion to ₦25 billion**, deadline **31 December 2005** (18 months). Reason: banks were **small, fragile, poorly developed, with a weak capital base** compared with their expected role. Allowed modes: **mergers and outright acquisitions/takeovers** (plus fresh capital); a **mere group arrangement was not acceptable**. Result: **89 banks fell to 25**; **14** failed and were liquidated.

| For consolidation | Against consolidation |
|---|---|
| Revenue and cost efficiency gains | More risk-taking through leverage and off-balance-sheet activity |
| Removes weak banks | Very large banks are complex and costly to manage |
| Better diversification of risk | Scale economies are not unlimited |

- **2009 crisis:** caused by **heavy lending concentrated in the capital market (margin loans) and oil and gas**, plus **weak risk management and corporate governance**. A **joint CBN-NDIC special examination** (Governor **Sanusi Lamido Sanusi**, August 2009) found **very high non-performing loans** and **illiquid** banks depending on CBN support (Expanded Discount Window) and interbank borrowing. Five, then three more, banks had their managements removed and received CBN capital injections; **AMCON** (2010) bought their bad loans.
- **2010 licensing regime** (universal banking ended): commercial bank with **international** authorisation **₦50 billion**; **national** ₦25 billion; **regional** ₦10 billion; merchant bank ₦15 billion.
- **Current (2024 recapitalisation, deadline 31 March 2026):** international commercial **₦500 billion**; national ₦200 billion; regional ₦50 billion; merchant ₦50 billion; non-interest national ₦20 billion, regional ₦10 billion.

**Explain more**

- Before 2004, existing banks needed ₦1 billion and new banks ₦2 billion; one South African bank (ABSA) had more assets than all 89 Nigerian banks.
- The five banks first examined in 2009 (Afribank, Finbank, Intercontinental, Oceanic, Union) had liquidity ratios **below** the 25% minimum then.

### One-minute recap

- Central bank = apex monetary authority; sole issuer; bankers' bank; government's bank; does not compete with commercial banks.
- WACB 1912; Fisher 1953 (against), World Bank 1955 (State Bank), Loynes 1957 (for); CBN Act 1958; operations 1 July 1959.
- CBN Act 2007: first object is monetary and price stability; Board chaired by the Governor; 4 Deputy Governors; Governor appointed by the President with Senate confirmation for 5 years, renewable once.
- MPC: 12 members, meets at least 4 times a year; MPR replaced MRR (2006).
- Quantitative tools: OMO, bank rate, reserve requirements, special deposits; qualitative: moral suasion, selective credit control, margin requirement, loan ceiling.
- Sell securities or raise CRR to contract; buy securities or cut rates to expand. Multiplier = 1 ÷ reserve ratio.
- Developmental: infrastructure, special funds and schemes (ACGSF 75%, SMIEIS 10% of PBT, RRF exports, FIF imports, rural banking), information and training.
- 2004: ₦25 billion, 89 to 25 banks; 2010: international ₦50 billion; 2024: international ₦500 billion by 31 March 2026.

### Theory practice

**Question:** Lekki Bank has deposit liabilities of ₦800 billion. The CRR is 45% and the liquidity ratio 30%. (a) How much must it keep with the CBN as cash reserve? (b) What minimum liquid assets must it hold? (c) If the CBN cuts the CRR to 40%, how much is released for lending?

*Solution:*
(a) 45% × ₦800bn = ₦360bn kept with the CBN.
(b) 30% × ₦800bn = ₦240bn in liquid assets (cash, balances with the CBN, Treasury Bills).
(c) New CRR: 40% × ₦800bn = ₦320bn. Released = ₦360bn − ₦320bn = ₦40bn. A lower CRR is expansionary: lending capacity and money supply rise.
