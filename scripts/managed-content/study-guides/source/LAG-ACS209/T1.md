## Topic 1: Introduction to Statistics and Data Collection

**What you must know:** what statistics is and its branches; uses in business; data, elements, variables and observations; types of variable; the four scales of measurement; population, sample, parameter and statistic with their symbols; primary and secondary data and how they are collected; the sampling methods.

### Lesson 1.1: What statistics is, its branches and its uses

**Must know**

- **Statistics:** the science of collecting, organising, presenting, analysing and interpreting data to assist in making more effective decisions. A shorter version: a field of study that deals with the collection, organisation, presentation, analysis and interpretation of data. It is also called the art and science of collecting, analysing, presenting and interpreting data.
- **Statistic** (no "s") is different: a single numerical measure calculated from a sample (Lesson 1.4).
- **Descriptive statistics:** methods of organising, summarising and presenting data in an informative way: the **tabular, graphical and numerical** methods (tables, charts, mean, median, mode). Main objective: to **describe the data collected**. Conclusions are about the **same group only**.
- **Inferential statistics:** methods used to determine something about a **population** on the basis of a **sample**. Main objective: to **infer something about the population from a sample**. It generalises from samples to populations using probability, and covers estimation, hypothesis testing, finding relationships between variables and making predictions. It is the harder branch.
- **Statistical inference:** a statistical procedure to determine the characteristics of a population by studying a sample.
- **Mathematical (theoretical) statistics:** develops statistical methods and their theory. **Applied statistics:** uses those methods on real problems (business, economics, health).

**Five business applications**

1. **Accounting:** public accounting firms use statistical **sampling** procedures when auditing clients.
2. **Economics:** economists use statistical information to **forecast** the economy.
3. **Marketing:** electronic **point-of-sale scanners** at checkout counters collect data for marketing research.
4. **Production:** statistical **quality control charts** monitor the output of a production process.
5. **Finance:** financial advisers use **price-earnings ratios and dividend yields** to guide investment recommendations.

Also **personnel management** (human resources): analysis of labour turnover and performance appraisal.

**Explain more**

- Descriptive or inferential? Ask: is the conclusion about the group actually measured, or about a bigger group?
  - "A census shows that 70% of the people in a country live in rural areas" is **descriptive**: every person was counted, nothing is estimated.
  - "The average age of all staff, from staff records" and "the percentage of skilled workers currently employed in a factory" are **descriptive**.
  - "An estimate, based on a sample, of the increase in prices next year", "20% of next month's bulbs are expected to be defective", "50% of people are likely to vote" are **inferential**: they reach beyond the data, often into the future.
  - A pollster interviews 1,000 randomly chosen registered voters in Lagos State to predict the governorship result: **inferential**.
- Trap: a course adviser who averages the scores of her 80 students and reports it only as a summary of that class is doing **descriptive** statistics. Calculating an average does not make it inferential; what matters is whether you generalise beyond the group.
- Matching applications: a Lagos chartered accountancy firm checking a random portion of invoices = accounting; an Ikeja supermarket studying scanner data = marketing; quality control charts on a bottling line = production; an adviser in Victoria Island using P/E ratios and dividend yields = finance; forecasting next year's inflation = economics.

### Lesson 1.2: Data, variables and constants

**Must know**

- **Data:** the facts and figures collected, summarised, analysed and interpreted.
- **Data set:** all the data collected in a particular study.
- **Elements:** the entities on which data are collected (e.g. a single UNILAG student, a quoted company).
- **Variable:** a characteristic of interest for the elements that can take different values (age, income, score).
- **Constant:** a quantity whose value does not change; its value is **fixed**.
- **Observation:** the set of measurements collected for a particular element (one row of a data table).
- **Number of data values in a data set = number of elements × number of variables.**
- **Random variable:** a variable whose values are determined by chance.

**Types of variable**

| Type | Meaning | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Qualitative | values are non-numeric categories or labels | brand preference, eye colour, method of payment |
| Quantitative | values are numeric (how many or how much) | sales, height, number of customers |
| Discrete (quantitative) | only certain separate, countable values; "how many"; found by **counting** | number of students in a class, customers served in a day |
| Continuous (quantitative) | any value within a range, infinitely many; "how much"; found by **measuring** | weight, temperature, rainfall, time |
| Independent | the variable that provides the basis for estimating or predicting another | advertising spend |
| Dependent | the variable being predicted or estimated | sales |

- Discrete and continuous are the two types of **quantitative** variable.

**Explain more**

- **Worked example (data values).** A table lists 5 quoted companies (rows) with 3 columns: stock exchange, annual sales (₦ million) and earnings per share (₦). Elements = the 5 companies; variables = the 3 columns; each row is an observation. Data values = 5 × 3 = 15. "Annual sales" is a **variable**, not an element.
- **Bank-style:** 4 variables (age, hall, monthly allowance, CGPA) for 25 UNILAG students gives 25 × 4 = **100** data values.
- Qualitative examples: "liking or disliking of a product by 500 persons", brand preference. Height, income, wheat yield, monthly revenue and units sold are quantitative.
- "Number of students in a statistics class" is **discrete** quantitative; temperature, a baby's weight and monthly rainfall in mm are **continuous**.
- **Recording continuous values.** A continuous value is always rounded, so the recorded figure stands for an interval. The boundaries have **one more decimal place than the data and end in 5**: true value lies from (recorded value − half a unit) up to, but not including, (recorded value + half a unit).
  - 64 kg to the nearest kg: half a unit = 0.5, so 63.5 ≤ x < 64.5.
  - 2.47 m to two decimal places: half a unit = 0.005, so 2.465 ≤ x < 2.475.
  - 3.6 kg to one decimal place: half a unit = 0.05, so 3.55 ≤ x < 3.65.
- Trap: 2.47 ≤ x < 2.48 and 2.46 ≤ x < 2.48 are wrong; the interval is centred on the recorded value.

### Lesson 1.3: Scales (levels) of measurement

**Must know**

- The scale **determines the amount of information** in the data and **indicates which data summarisation and statistical analyses are most appropriate**.
- Four scales, **lowest to highest: nominal, ordinal, interval, ratio.**

1. **Nominal:** labels or names that identify an attribute of an element; categories are mutually exclusive and no order or ranking can be imposed. A non-numeric label or numeric code may be used.
2. **Ordinal:** has the properties of nominal data, and the **order or rank** is meaningful. The relative magnitude of values is meaningful, but **differences between values are not comparable**. Label or numeric code.
3. **Interval:** has the properties of ordinal data, and the interval between observations is expressed in a **fixed unit of measure**. Differences are comparable, but the **zero point is arbitrary**, so ratios are meaningless. Always numeric.
4. **Ratio:** has all the properties of interval data, and the **ratio of two values is meaningful** because there is a **true (absolute) zero** meaning nothing exists for the variable. Always numeric.

**Three characteristics of a scale:** **origin** (marks the specific beginning of a scale; in the best scales an absolute zero), **order**, **distance** (relative positions on the scale).

| Scale | Origin | Order | Distance | Meaningful operations | Methods |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Nominal | no | no | no | classifying and counting | nonparametric |
| Ordinal | no | yes | no | the above plus ranking | nonparametric |
| Interval | no (arbitrary zero) | yes | yes | the above plus addition and subtraction | parametric |
| Ratio | yes (true zero) | yes | yes | all arithmetic, including ratios | parametric |

- **Parametric statistics:** methods that require data measured on an interval or ratio scale. **Non-parametric statistics:** methods used for nominal or ordinal data.
- **Qualitative (categorical) data** use the **nominal or ordinal** scale and **can be numeric or non-numeric**; analysis is rather limited. **Quantitative data** use the interval or ratio scale, are always numeric, ordinary arithmetic is meaningful, and they offer **more alternatives** for statistical analysis.

**Explain more**

- **Nominal:** gender, department, method of payment (cash, card, transfer), state of origin coded 1 = Lagos, 2 = Ogun, 3 = Oyo. The codes are only names; 3 is not "more" than 1.
- **Ordinal:** class level (100L, 200L, 300L), position in a firm (1 = Managing Director, 2 = Manager, 3 = Supervisor, 4 = Clerk), ranking of five soft drinks in a taste test, drink size small/medium/large, Likert responses (strongly agree ... strongly disagree). You know MD is above Manager, but the "gap" from 1 to 2 is not the same kind of quantity as from 3 to 4.
- **Interval:** temperature in °C or °F (0 °C does not mean "no heat"; 20 °C is not twice as hot as 10 °C), calendar year, SAT-type test scores ("Melissa scored 115 points more than Kevin" is meaningful).
- **Ratio:** height, weight (a bag of rice in kg), distance, time, money (sales, profit, costs in ₦), financial ratios (P/E, inventory turnover), interest rates. **Example:** Shop A in Balogun Market sold ₦400,000 last week and Shop B ₦100,000. Ratio = 400,000 ÷ 100,000 = 4, so "Shop A sold four times as much" is meaningful: ratio scale.
- Traps:
  - "Not ratio" among height, profit, inventory turnover and **calendar year**: calendar year (interval; year 0 is not "no time").
  - What separates ratio from interval is only the **true zero**; both have a fixed unit and comparable differences.
  - Classify a variable on two levels at once: method of payment = qualitative and nominal; drink size = qualitative and ordinal; annual sales and earnings per share = quantitative and ratio.
  - Some tables list multiplication and division under interval data; strictly, ratios of interval values carry no meaning, which is exactly why the ratio scale exists.

### Lesson 1.4: Population, sample, parameter and statistic

**Must know**

- **Population:** the set of all units (elements) of interest in a study; all subjects possessing a common characteristic being studied. It need not be people (all bulbs, all invoices).
- **Sample:** a part (subset) of the population selected for study.
- **Census:** collecting data from **every** member of the population. A sample survey studies only part of it.
- **Parameter:** a numerical characteristic of a **population**, computed from population data (population mean, standard deviation, proportion). A measure of central tendency from the whole population is a population parameter.
- **Statistic:** a numerical characteristic of a **sample**, calculated from sample data. A measure of central tendency from sample data is a sample statistic. Its value is used to estimate the parameter.
- **Sampling:** the process of selecting a sample from a population. **Sampling frame:** a complete list of all elements in the population from which the sample is drawn.
- **Estimation:** using sample data to estimate a parameter. **Estimator:** the rule or formula used. **Estimate:** the numerical value it produces.
- **Process:** a set of activities that repeatedly converts inputs into outputs under the same conditions.

**Symbols:** parameters use **Greek letters**, statistics use **Roman letters**.

| Measure | Population (parameter) | Sample (statistic) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Mean | mu (μ) | x-bar (x̄) |
| Variance | sigma squared (σ²) | s² |
| Standard deviation | sigma (σ) | s |
| Proportion | P | p-bar (p̄) |
| Size | N | n |

(rho, ρ, is the population correlation coefficient, not a variance.)

**Explain more**

- **Why sample?** Populations are usually large, data for every element are often impossible to get, and every item surveyed adds cost and time. A good sample gives **reasonably accurate** (not totally accurate) information about the population.
- **Bulb example:** a Nigerian bulb maker tests 200 new bulbs and uses their average life to judge whether all bulbs of the new design last longer. Population = **all bulbs of the new design**; sample = the 200 bulbs tested; their average life = a statistic.
- The Students' Union interviews 250 randomly selected students on a hostel fee: the 250 are the **sample**; all UNILAG students are the population.
- The average rent of 120 randomly selected Oshodi traders, ₦85,000, is a **statistic** (it describes the sample).
- A researcher applies the sample-mean formula and gets 34 years: the **formula is the estimator**, 34 years is the **estimate**.
- The official register of all students obtained from the Faculty Officer is the **sampling frame**.
- Population or sample? "Average fuel consumption of cars driven by **all** UNILAG students" and "range of income of **all** residents of Yaba" are populations; "percentage of **500 students selected at random**" is a sample.

### Lesson 1.5: Data sources and methods of collection

**Must know**

- **Primary data:** information collected directly, for the first time, for a specific purpose, usually by the person who needs it (direct observation, surveys, interviews).
- **Secondary data:** information taken from an existing source where it was already recorded; data already collected by someone else, for a different purpose. It **saves time and money**.
- **Internal** data come from within the organisation (sales records, payroll); **external** data come from outside it. So, to a business, government and non-government publications and statistics of the IMF or ILO are **external secondary** data.

**Six existing (secondary) sources**

1. Within a firm (almost any department).
2. Business database services.
3. Government agencies.
4. Industry associations.
5. Special-interest organisations.
6. The Internet.

**Methods of collecting primary data:** personal (face-to-face) interview, telephone interview, mail (postal) questionnaire, door-to-door survey, direct observation, experiments.

**Two kinds of statistical study**

1. **Experimental:** the variable of interest is identified, then one or more other variables are **controlled** to see how they influence it.
2. **Observational (non-experimental):** **no attempt** is made to control or influence the variables. A survey is a good example.

**Time dimension:** **cross-sectional data** are collected at the same or approximately the same point in time; **time series data** are collected over several time periods.

**Three data acquisition considerations**

1. **Time requirement:** searching can be time-consuming; information may no longer be useful when it arrives.
2. **Cost of acquisition:** organisations often charge for information even when it is not their main business.
3. **Data errors:** using any data that happen to be available, or were acquired with little care, can mislead.

**Explain more**

- Nigerian secondary sources: the National Bureau of Statistics (inflation, unemployment), the Central Bank of Nigeria (statistical bulletins), industry bodies such as the Manufacturers Association of Nigeria, published census reports and newspapers. A student using NBS inflation figures uses **secondary** data.
- "Copying figures from published government reports" is **not** primary collection; telephone interviews, mail questionnaires and door-to-door surveys are.
- **Personal interviews** best reduce misrepresentation and misunderstanding of questions, because the interviewer can explain and probe; they cost more than post or telephone.
- **Questionnaire design:**
  - **Open-ended questions** let respondents answer in any way they like; **closed-ended** (multiple-choice, itemised) questions give fixed options.
  - A **structured questionnaire** has fixed, predetermined questions with defined response options.
  - Test a new draft on a small group first: a **pilot survey**.
- Extra tutorials for one group and none for another, then comparing scores: **experimental**. Recording students' study hours and scores without influencing them: **observational**.
- Students per UNILAG faculty in the 2026 session: **cross-sectional**. One supermarket's sales for each of 36 months: **time series**.
- A firm whose market information arrives after the decision is made has ignored **time requirement**; a manager who uses unchecked figures "lying around" has ignored **data errors**. "Sample mean" is not an acquisition consideration.

### Lesson 1.6: Sampling methods

**Must know**

**Probability (random) sampling:** chance methods or random numbers decide who is selected.

1. **Simple random sampling:** every member has an equal chance; for a finite population, every possible sample of size n has the same probability of being chosen. Lottery (names in a hat) or random numbers. It is the **preferred** method but often difficult, because it needs a **complete list of every element**.
2. **Systematic sampling:** select every **k-th** element from a list after a random start. **k = N ÷ n** (population size ÷ sample size).
3. **Stratified sampling:** divide the population into groups (**strata**) by some **characteristic**, then sample **from every stratum**.
4. **Cluster sampling:** divide the population into groups (**clusters** or blocks), usually **geographical**; randomly select **some** clusters and use **every element** in them.

**Non-probability sampling:** selection depends on judgement or convenience, not chance.

1. **Convenience (accidental):** readily available data, the first people met; easiest but the worst method.
2. **Purposive (judgement):** the researcher chooses the units thought typical.
3. **Quota:** fill set numbers from each group, chosen non-randomly.
4. **Snowball:** respondents recruit further respondents.

- The **five types of sampling** most often listed together: **random, systematic, convenience, cluster and stratified**.

| Stratified | Cluster |
| --- | --- |
| groups formed by a characteristic | groups usually geographical |
| every group is sampled | only some randomly chosen groups are used, in full |

**Explain more**

- **Worked example (systematic).** A sample of 60 from a list of 1,200 students: k = N ÷ n = 1,200 ÷ 60 = **20**. Pick a random start between 1 and 20, then every 20th name.
- A Surulere bank has 1,500 account holders and wants 50: k = 1,500 ÷ 50 = 30.
- Recognising methods:
  - A hall warden draws 30 names from slips in a box: **simple random** (lottery method).
  - A bank splits customers into salary earners, traders and students and samples randomly **from each**: **stratified**.
  - A researcher randomly picks 4 Lagos LGAs and interviews **every** trader in them: **cluster**.
  - A reporter at the UNILAG main gate interviews the first 40 people: **convenience**.
- "Random sampling" is the odd one out in a list of purposive, judgement and accidental sampling, because it is a probability method.

### One-minute recap

- Statistics: collecting, organising, presenting, analysing and interpreting data for better decisions. Descriptive describes the group measured; inferential generalises from a sample to a population.
- Applications: accounting (audit sampling), economics (forecasts), marketing (scanners), production (control charts), finance (P/E and dividend yields), personnel (labour turnover).
- Data values = elements × variables. Discrete = counted; continuous = measured; boundaries = recorded value ± half a unit.
- Scales, low to high: nominal, ordinal, interval, ratio. Only ratio has a true zero. Nominal and ordinal are qualitative and use nonparametric methods.
- Parameter (population, Greek: μ, σ, σ²); statistic (sample, Roman: x̄, s, s²). Census = every member.
- Primary = first-hand for this purpose; secondary = already collected, saves time and money. IMF data to a firm = external secondary.
- Experimental studies control variables; observational studies do not. Cross-sectional = one time point; time series = many periods.
- Probability sampling: simple random, systematic (k = N ÷ n), stratified (all strata), cluster (some clusters, in full). Non-probability: convenience, purposive, quota, snowball.

### Theory practice

**Question:** A Yaba supermarket has 2,400 loyalty-card customers and wants a systematic sample of 80. Find the sampling interval, list the first four customers chosen if the random start is 13, and say whether this is probability sampling.

*Solution:* k = N ÷ n = 2,400 ÷ 80 = 30. Selected numbers: 13, 13 + 30 = 43, 43 + 30 = 73, 73 + 30 = 103. Yes, it is probability sampling: the random start means chance, not the researcher, decides who is chosen.
