## Topic 2: Tabulation and Graphical Representation

**What you must know:** the three methods of presenting data; raw data, arrays and the four bases of classification; tabulation (objectives, limitations, parts, types of table); frequency distributions and every class term (limits, boundaries, width, midpoint, inclusive and exclusive, open-ended, relative and cumulative frequency); diagrams (one-, two- and three-dimensional, bar types, pie charts, pictograms, cartograms); frequency graphs and special charts (histogram, frequency polygon, ogive, line graph, scatter plot, box plot, stem-and-leaf, Pareto chart) with their calculations.

### Lesson 2.1: Presenting and classifying data

**Must know**

- **Primary data:** information collected directly for a specific purpose, usually by the person who needs it. **Secondary data:** information taken from an existing source where it was already recorded or stored.
- After data are collected they must be presented clearly so their main features are seen quickly. The **three methods of presenting data:**

1. **Textual** method: words and sentences.
2. **Tabular** method: tables of rows and columns.
3. **Graphical** (diagrammatic) method: charts and graphs.

- **Raw data:** data collected in their original form, a listing of the data in the form in which they were collected, before any arrangement or grouping.
- **Ordered array:** raw data arranged in ascending or descending order of magnitude.
- **Classification:** the process of arranging data into classes or groups on the basis of common characteristics.
- **Four bases of classification** (the four types of tabulation):

1. **Qualitative:** by attributes or traits such as sex, religion, nationality, physical status, social status, blood group.
2. **Quantitative:** by measurable (numerical) characteristics such as income, production, cost, height, weight, age.
3. **Temporal (chronological):** by time: years, months, weeks, days, hours.
4. **Spatial (geographical):** by location: country, state, Local Government Area, town, village.

- Qualitative classification is of two types: **simple** classification (one attribute splitting the data into two classes, e.g. employed or unemployed) and **manifold** classification (more than two classes, or several attributes at once, e.g. sex, then marital status within each sex).

**Explain more**

- Range = largest value − smallest value. For the marks 36, 55, 73, 95, 42, 60, 78, 25, 62, 75: array 25, 36, 42, 55, 60, 62, 73, 75, 78, 95, so range = 95 − 25 = 70. Arranging large data sets in order is slow, which is why we tabulate.
- Spot the basis from the example: traders in each Lagos LGA = **spatial**; a Lekki supermarket's sales January to December 2025 = **temporal**; UNILAG students grouped 15-19, 20-24, 25-29 years = **quantitative**; Jaja Hall students by blood group = **qualitative**.
- "Inferential" is **not** a basis of classification. Do not confuse "spatial" (place) with "temporal" (time).

### Lesson 2.2: Tabulation and the parts of a table

**Must know**

- **Tabulation:** a systematic and logical arrangement of data in rows and columns with respect to the characteristics of the data. It is orderly, compact and self-explanatory, and coordinates data for further statistical treatment and decision-making.
- **Objectives of tabulation (5):**

1. To simplify complex data.
2. To bring out the essential features of the data.
3. To facilitate comparison.
4. To facilitate statistical analysis.
5. To save (economise) space.

Reducing the chances of errors is also an objective. Presenting each individual item of raw data is **not** an objective.

- **Three limitations of a table:**

1. **Lacks description:** shows only figures, not attributes; ignores the qualitative aspects of the facts.
2. **Incapable of presenting individual items:** it presents aggregate data.
3. **Needs special knowledge:** a layman cannot easily use it.

- **Eight main parts of a table** (in order):

1. **Table number:** the very first item at the top, for easy identification and reference.
2. **Title:** second item, just above the table; narrates the contents; clear, brief, carefully worded.
3. **Head note:** third item, after the title, usually in brackets; gives the units (e.g. "amount in naira", "quantity in tonnes").
4. **Captions (column headings):** at the top of each column, explaining its figures.
5. **Stubs (row headings):** titles of the horizontal rows.
6. **Body:** the numerical information; columns read top to bottom, rows read left to right.
7. **Source note:** brief statement of where the data were obtained.
8. **Footnote:** explains a feature not self-explanatory and not explained elsewhere, e.g. points of exception.

- **Types of table:**

| Basis | Types |
|---|---|
| Number of characteristics | **Simple (one-way)**: one characteristic. **Complex**: two or more (two-way, three-way, **manifold**) |
| Origin of figures | **Original (primary)**: figures as collected. **Derived**: totals, ratios or percentages worked out from an original table |
| Purpose | **General purpose (reference)**: large, for record. **Special purpose (summary)**: small, for one analysis |

**Explain more**

- Students in each UNILAG faculty broken down by level **and** gender = complex (manifold) table. Students per faculty only = simple table.
- A line at the bottom of a hostel table saying "Figures for Moremi Hall exclude 40 rooms under renovation" is the **footnote**; "Source: Student Affairs Division" would be the **source note**.
- Traps: the head note gives **units**, not the source; captions are **column** headings, stubs are **row** headings; the table number, not the title, comes first.
- **Bivariate frequency table:** cross-classifies two variables (e.g. age and monthly allowance). The row and column totals, giving the frequencies of x alone and of y alone, are **marginal frequencies**.

### Lesson 2.3: Frequency distributions and class terms

**Must know**

- **Frequency:** the number of times a value or class of values occurs.
- **Frequency distribution:** the organisation of raw data in table form with classes and frequencies; a tabular summary showing the number of items in each of several non-overlapping classes. It is the comprehensive way of classifying raw data and shows how often each value or range of values occurs.
- **Kinds:** **categorical** (nominal or ordinal data, e.g. blood groups); **ungrouped** (numerical, each value listed, e.g. number of children); **grouped** (several values per class). **Univariate** = one variable; **bivariate** = two variables cross-classified.
- **Class interval (class):** a group such as 30-39. **Class limits:** the smallest (lower limit) and largest (upper limit) numbers that can belong to the class; they appear in the data and may leave gaps.
- **Class boundaries:** have one more decimal place than the data, never appear in the data, leave no gaps. Lower boundary = lower limit − 0.5; upper boundary = upper limit + 0.5 (for whole-number data; generally, half the gap between classes).
- **Class width (size):** upper boundary − lower boundary of a class; also the difference between lower limits (or upper limits, or midpoints) of consecutive classes. It is **not** upper limit − lower limit of the same inclusive class.
- **Class mark (midpoint)** = (lower limit + upper limit) ÷ 2 = (lower boundary + upper boundary) ÷ 2.
- The inclusive and exclusive methods are the two ways of forming **class intervals**. **Inclusive method:** e.g. 10-19, 20-29; both limits belong to the class; used for discrete data. **Exclusive method:** e.g. 10-20, 20-30; the upper limit of one class is the lower limit of the next and belongs to the next class; keeps data continuous, so used for continuous variables.
- **Open-ended class:** no stated lower limit or no stated upper limit ("below 20", "60 and above"); a distribution with one is open-ended. For its midpoint, assume the same width as the neighbouring class.
- **Width when building a table:** class width = (largest value − smallest value) ÷ number of classes = range ÷ number of classes. If the division is exact, use the exact quotient (e.g. 2.5); if not, round **up** to a convenient whole number.
- **Sturges' rule** (Herbert Sturges) for the number of classes: k = 1 + 3.322 log n.
- **Relative frequency** = class frequency ÷ total frequency (multiply by 100 for a percentage).
- **Cumulative frequency (less than):** running total from the top; the number of values less than the **upper boundary** of the class.
- **More than cumulative frequency:** totals added from the bottom (highest class) upwards; the number of values above the **lower boundary**.
- **Cumulative relative frequency** = cumulative frequency ÷ total frequency.

**Explain more**

*Worked example 1 (all class terms).* Classes 10-19, 20-29, 30-39, 40-49, 50-59 with frequencies 5, 9, 14, 8, 4 (Σf = 40).

| Class | f | Boundaries | Midpoint | Rel. f | Less than cf | More than cf | Cum. rel. f |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 10-19 | 5 | 9.5-19.5 | 14.5 | 0.125 | 5 | 40 | 0.125 |
| 20-29 | 9 | 19.5-29.5 | 24.5 | 0.225 | 14 | 35 | 0.35 |
| 30-39 | 14 | 29.5-39.5 | 34.5 | 0.35 | 28 | 26 | 0.70 |
| 40-49 | 8 | 39.5-49.5 | 44.5 | 0.20 | 36 | 12 | 0.90 |
| 50-59 | 4 | 49.5-59.5 | 54.5 | 0.10 | 40 | 4 | 1.00 |

- Width = 19.5 − 9.5 = 10 (or 20 − 10 = 10), not 19 − 10 = 9.
- Less than cf of 30-39 = 5 + 9 + 14 = 28. Values below 49.5 = 36.
- More than cf at 29.5 = 14 + 8 + 4 = 26 (equivalently 40 − 14).
- Cumulative relative frequency of 30-39 = 28 ÷ 40 = 0.70.

*Worked example 2 (more boundaries and midpoints).*
- 25-34, 35-44, 45-54: width = 35 − 25 = 10. Midpoint of 35-44 = (35 + 44) ÷ 2 = 39.5. Boundaries of 50-59 = 49.5 and 59.5.
- Keke fares (₦'000) 15-19, 20-24: width = 20 − 15 = 5 (not 4).
- One-decimal data 2.5-2.9, 3.0-3.4: gap = 0.1, half-gap = 0.05, so 3.0-3.4 has boundaries 2.95 and 3.45.
- Midpoints 12, 17, 22: width = 17 − 12 = 5, so the class with midpoint 17 runs from 17 − 2.5 = 14.5 to 17 + 2.5 = 19.5.
- Exclusive class 20-30: width 30 − 20 = 10, midpoint (20 + 30) ÷ 2 = 25. Midpoint of 40-50 = 45.
- Incomes (₦'000) "less than 20", 20-30, 30-40: next width is 10, so the open class is taken as 10-20, midpoint 15.

*Worked example 3 (choosing the width).*
- Largest 60, smallest 40, 5 classes: (60 − 40) ÷ 5 = 4.
- Salaries of 15 workers from ₦2,000 to ₦3,000, 10 classes: (3,000 − 2,000) ÷ 10 = ₦100.
- Largest 85, smallest 65, 8 classes: 20 ÷ 8 = 2.5. Largest 45, smallest 25, 4 classes: 20 ÷ 4 = 5.
- Garri bags from 34 kg to 98 kg, 7 classes: 64 ÷ 7 = 9.14, rounded up = **10**. Rounding up ensures the classes cover the whole range. When the division is exact, use the exact quotient.
- Sturges: for n = 50, k = 1 + 3.322 × log 50 = 1 + 3.322 × 1.699 = 6.64, about 7 classes (rounded).

*Worked example 4 (tallying).* Heights of 50 students, classes 150-154, 155-159, 160-164, 165-169, 170-174, tally to frequencies 12, 9, **14**, 10, 5 (total 50). Three coins tossed 30 times, number of heads 0, 1, 2, 3 has frequencies 6, **10**, 9, 5. Children in 20 Bariga households (2, 3, 1, 4, 2, 0, 3, 2, 5, 2, 1, 3, 2, 4, 0, 2, 3, 1, 2, 3): 0 → 2, 1 → 3, 2 → **7**, 3 → 5, 4 → 2, 5 → 1; fewer than 2 children = 2 + 3 = **5**. Always check that the frequencies add up to n.

- Relative frequency: 12 of 80 Yaba shop owners in a class gives 12 ÷ 80 = 0.15. Relative frequency is **not** frequency ÷ class width (that is frequency density).

### Lesson 2.4: Diagrams and their rules

**Must know**

- **Diagrammatic presentation:** a visual method of presenting numerical data that makes it easier to grasp than tables or text; attractive, easy to understand, gives a bird's-eye view and quick comparison, but less precise than tables.
- **Three types of diagram:** **geometric** (bar charts, pie charts), **frequency** (histograms, frequency polygons, ogives), **arithmetic line graphs** (time-series graphs). Common forms: **pictograms**, **cartograms**, **bar diagrams**, **pie diagrams**.
- **Pictogram (pictograph, ideograph):** pictures or symbols, each standing for a fixed number of items. **Cartogram:** statistical data shown on a map (regions shaded, coloured or resized).
- **General guidelines (6):** title (small, self-explanatory); size (neither too small nor too big); paper used (generally blank paper); scale (the y-axis matters most in bar diagrams as it shows magnitude); index (a legend or key whenever two or more variables use different shading or lines); selection of the proper type of diagram.
- **Advantages (6):** attractive and impressive; easy to remember; save time; simplify data; useful for comparison; more informative (bring out hidden facts and relations).
- **Disadvantages (4):** lack of precision (approximate values only); limited data (clutter with large sets); risk of misrepresentation (e.g. truncated axes); supplementary tool (diagrams are derived from tabulated data and supplement it, they do not replace it).

| Dimension | What varies | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **One-dimensional** | Length (height) only | Simple, multiple, subdivided, percentage, broken-scale, deviation bar diagrams; lines |
| **Two-dimensional** (area or surface diagrams) | Length and breadth (area) | Rectangles, squares, circles, pie charts |
| **Three-dimensional** (volume diagrams) | Length, breadth and depth (height) | Cubes, spheres, cylinders |

- **Six bar diagrams:**

1. **Simple:** one bar of equal width per category.
2. **Multiple:** bars side by side to compare two or more related variables (imports and exports by year).
3. **Subdivided (component):** each bar split in the ratio of its components.
4. **Percentage:** subdivided bars on a percentage basis; every bar totals 100.
5. **Broken-scale:** when one value is far larger, its bar is broken to save space; values written on top.
6. **Deviation:** net positive and negative values (profit or loss, trade surplus or deficit) above and below a zero line.

- **Pie chart:** circle = total; sector angle = (component ÷ total) × 360°. Percentage = (angle ÷ 360) × 100. Value = (angle ÷ 360) × total.

**Explain more**

*Worked example 5 (pie chart in ₦).* A 200L student's monthly budget of ₦60,000: feeding ₦24,000, books ₦12,000, transport ₦9,000, data ₦6,000, others ₦9,000.
- Feeding = 24,000 ÷ 60,000 × 360 = 144°. Books = 12,000 ÷ 60,000 × 360 = 72°. Transport = 9,000 ÷ 60,000 × 360 = 54°. Data = 36°. Others = 54°.
- Check: 144 + 72 + 54 + 36 + 54 = 360°.
- Reverse: a salaries sector of 108° in total spending of ₦500,000 is 108 ÷ 360 × 500,000 = ₦150,000. A 72° sector is 72 ÷ 360 × 100 = 20% of the total. 25 keke users out of 120 students = 25 ÷ 120 × 360 = 75°. ₦12,000 of ₦48,000 = 90°.

*Worked example 6 (percentage bar).* A hall spends ₦72,000: food ₦36,000, utilities ₦18,000, maintenance ₦18,000. Shares: 36,000 ÷ 72,000 × 100 = 50%; utilities 18,000 ÷ 72,000 × 100 = 25%; maintenance 25%.

*Worked example 7 (pictogram).* One bag symbol = 500 bags of rice at Daleko Market. 2,250 bags needs 2,250 ÷ 500 = 4.5 symbols (four whole symbols and half a symbol).

- Three-dimensional diagrams are less common because **volume is harder to judge visually than length or area**. A cube is **not** two-dimensional.
- Bars are one-dimensional because **only length** is considered; their width is chosen for looks and carries no information.
- Choose by message: parts of a whole (rent, salaries, materials) → pie chart; comparison → bar; trend → line.

### Lesson 2.5: Frequency graphs and special charts

**Must know**

- **Histogram:** a bar chart for continuous grouped data; bars **touch** and the **area** of each bar represents the frequency. Horizontal axis: class boundaries. With unequal widths, height = **frequency density** = frequency ÷ class width (or adjusted frequency = frequency × standard width ÷ actual width).
- **Bar chart vs histogram:** bar charts have gaps and suit categorical or discrete data; histograms have touching bars and suit continuous data.
- **Frequency polygon:** straight lines joining the midpoints of the tops of histogram bars; each class is plotted at (midpoint, frequency). Useful for comparing several data sets.
- **Ogive (cumulative frequency curve):** the graph of a cumulative frequency distribution. **Less than** ogive: plotted against **upper** class boundaries, starts at zero at the lowest boundary and rises to the total (or 1.00 for relative). **More than** ogive: plotted against **lower** class boundaries, starts at the total and falls to zero. Used to estimate the median, quartiles, deciles and percentiles graphically; the two ogives cross at the median.
- **Arithmetic line graph (time series graph):** values against time on the horizontal axis, consecutive points joined by straight lines; shows trends (monthly sales). **Single** line graph: one variable; **multiple** line graph: two or more on the same axes.
- **Scatter plot:** relationship (correlation) between two quantitative variables.
- **Box plot (box-and-whisker):** shows minimum, Q1, median, Q3, maximum. The box is the **interquartile range** (middle 50%) with a line at the median; whiskers show the rest; points beyond are outliers.
- **Stem-and-leaf display:** an exploratory data analysis tool; the **stem** is the leading digit(s), the **leaf** the trailing digit. It sorts data quickly, keeps the original values and shows the shape.
- **Pareto chart:** bar graph for qualitative data with bars arranged in order of frequency.
- **Good practice:** right chart for the message; clear title and labelled axes with units; honest scale with the vertical axis **starting at zero**; legend or key; simplicity (no **chart junk**); cite the source.

**Explain more**

*Worked example 8 (unequal-width histogram).* Loans (₦'000): 0-20 (f = 30), 20-30 (f = 25), 30-60 (f = 45).
- Densities: 30 ÷ 20 = 1.5; 25 ÷ 10 = 2.5; 45 ÷ 30 = 1.5. The 20-30 bar is tallest, though 30-60 has the largest frequency.
- Adjusted to the standard width 10: 1.5 × 10 = 15; 25; 1.5 × 10 = 15. A class 50-80 with f = 45 among width-10 classes gets 45 × 10 ÷ 30 = 15.
- Similarly, BRT waits 0-10 (20), 10-30 (40), 30-35 (15) give heights 2, 2 and 3, so 30-35 is tallest.

*Worked example 9 (stem-and-leaf).* Key 2 | 4 = 24.

```
2 | 4 7
3 | 1 3 3 8
4 | 0 2 5 9
5 | 6
```

- n = 11 values: 24, 27, 31, 33, 33, 38, 40, 42, 45, 49, 56 (already in order).
- Median position = (n + 1) ÷ 2 = 12 ÷ 2 = 6th value = **38**. (For an even n, average the two middle values.)
- Aged 40 or more: 40, 42, 45, 49, 56 = **5**.

*Worked example 10 (ogive and polygon).* For example 1 in Lesson 2.3, the less than ogive is plotted at (9.5, 0), (19.5, 5), (29.5, 14), (39.5, 28), (49.5, 36), (59.5, 40). The class 40-49 with f = 8 goes on the polygon at (44.5, 8). For a graphical median of grouped data, read across from n ÷ 2 = 20 on the cumulative axis to the curve.

- **Chart junk:** decorations, excessive gridlines or 3D effects that do not represent data; the term was coined by **Edward Tufte**, who also urged a high **data-ink ratio**. A legend, scale or source note is not chart junk.
- Today these charts are drawn with software such as Microsoft Excel, Tableau and Microsoft Power BI; the principles (clarity, accuracy, efficiency) are unchanged.
- Traps: a frequency polygon is not a "line chart of cumulative frequencies" (that is the ogive); a less than ogive starts at zero, not at the first frequency; heights and test scores (continuous) suit a histogram or frequency polygon, not a pie chart.

### One-minute recap

- Three presentation methods: textual, tabular, graphical. Raw data → ordered array → frequency distribution.
- Four bases of classification: qualitative, quantitative, temporal (chronological), spatial (geographical); qualitative is simple or manifold.
- Table parts in order: number, title, head note (units), captions (columns), stubs (rows), body, source note, footnote. Three limitations: lacks description, no individual items, needs special knowledge.
- Boundaries = limits ± 0.5; width = upper boundary − lower boundary; midpoint = (lower + upper) ÷ 2; width for building = range ÷ number of classes, rounded up when not exact.
- Inclusive (10-19) for discrete; exclusive (10-20) for continuous. Relative f = f ÷ Σf. Less than cf at upper boundaries; more than cf at lower boundaries.
- One-dimensional = length (bars); two-dimensional = area (squares, circles, pie); three-dimensional = volume (cubes).
- Pie angle = (part ÷ total) × 360°. Histogram height = f ÷ width when widths differ.
- Polygon joins midpoints of histogram tops; ogive finds median and quartiles; box plot shows the five-number summary; stems are leading digits, leaves trailing.

### Theory practice

**Question:** Classes 20-29, 30-39, 40-49, 50-59 have frequencies 6, 10, 16, 8. Find (a) the class width, (b) the boundaries and midpoint of 40-49, (c) the less than cumulative frequency of 40-49, (d) its relative frequency, and (e) its pie-chart angle.

*Solution:*
- Σf = 6 + 10 + 16 + 8 = 40.
- (a) Width = 30 − 20 = 10.
- (b) Boundaries 39.5 and 49.5; midpoint = (40 + 49) ÷ 2 = 44.5.
- (c) Less than cf = 6 + 10 + 16 = 32.
- (d) Relative frequency = 16 ÷ 40 = 0.4 (40%).
- (e) Angle = 16 ÷ 40 × 360 = 144°.
