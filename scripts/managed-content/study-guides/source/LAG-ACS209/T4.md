## Topic 4: Measures of Dispersion

**What you must know:** what dispersion means and why it is needed; absolute versus relative measures; the range, interquartile range and quartile deviation; the mean deviation; variance and standard deviation (population and sample, ungrouped and grouped); the coefficient of variation and the other coefficients; how adding or multiplying by a constant changes each measure; merits and demerits of each measure.

### Lesson 4.1: Meaning of dispersion; absolute and relative measures

**Must know**

- **Dispersion:** the extent to which the data values are spread (scattered) about the central location value (the average). Also called **variation**, **spread** or **scatter**. "The degree to which numerical data tend to spread about an average value" is **variation**.
- Three features describe a data set: **location** (where values concentrate), **dispersion** (how spread out they are) and **shape** (skewness).
- **Why we need it:** an average alone does not tell the whole story. Dispersion shows **how far the average can be relied on to represent the data**. Small dispersion = the average is a good representative; large dispersion = it is a poor one.
- **Large dispersion:** values widely spread from the mean; many extreme values. **Small (little) dispersion:** values cluster closely around the mean; the data are **relatively uniform** (consistent). **Zero dispersion:** all values equal.
- A measure of dispersion is **never negative**. It can be zero (all values equal) or positive.
- **Random variation:** variation in outcomes caused by uncontrolled (chance) factors. **Systematic variation:** variation with an identifiable, assignable cause (for example a faulty machine setting).

Measures of dispersion are classified into **two** groups:

| | Absolute measures | Relative measures |
|---|---|---|
| Meaning | Measure variation in the **unit of the variable** (₦, kg, marks) | Measure variation **relative to the average**; ratios or percentages |
| Units | Same unit as the data (variance: unit squared) | **Unit-free** (pure numbers) |
| Use | Spread of one data set | Comparing data sets with different units or very different averages |
| Examples | Range, quartile deviation (and interquartile range), mean deviation, variance, standard deviation | Coefficient of range, coefficient of quartile deviation, coefficient of mean deviation, coefficient of standard deviation, coefficient of variation |

**Explain more**

- Picture an electronics shop in Lagos recording daily sales in naira for a year. The average daily sales is a measure of central location; how much daily sales swing above and below that average is a measure of dispersion.
- Two shops can both average ₦50,000 a day. Shop A sells between ₦48,000 and ₦52,000 every day; Shop B sells ₦5,000 one day and ₦120,000 another. Same mean, very different dispersion. The mean describes Shop A well and Shop B poorly.
- Which set has the least variation: 46, 48, 50, 52, 54 or 48, 49, 50, 51, 52? Both centre on 50, but the second set is packed tighter (range 4 against 8), so it has the least variation.
- Trap: mean, median, mode and geometric mean are measures of **location**, not dispersion. Skewness and kurtosis describe **shape**. Every "coefficient of ..." in this topic is **relative**; every measure without "coefficient" is **absolute**.

### Lesson 4.2: The range and coefficient of range

**Must know**

- **Range:** the difference between the highest (largest) and lowest (smallest) values.
- Range = H − L, where H = highest value and L = lowest value.
- Rearranged: H = L + range; L = H − range.
- **Coefficient of range** (relative) = (H − L) ÷ (H + L).
- For grouped data, H = upper boundary of the top class and L = lower boundary of the bottom class.

| Merits of the range | Demerits of the range |
|---|---|
| Simplest to understand | Uses **only two observations** (the extremes) and ignores the rest |
| **Quickest** to compute | **Most affected by extreme values** |
| Used in quality control and daily price or temperature swings | Cannot be found for open-ended classes; unstable from sample to sample |

**Explain more**

*Worked example.* A Yaba provision kiosk sells (₦'000) 24, 31, 18, 40, 27, 35 on six days.

- H = 40, L = 18.
- Range = 40 − 18 = 22, that is ₦22,000.
- Coefficient of range = (40 − 18) ÷ (40 + 18) = 22 ÷ 58 = 0.3793 (4 d.p.).

*Reverse use.* If the minimum is 9 and the range is 57, then H = 9 + 57 = 66.

- Always pick out H and L carefully; the data need not be in order. In 5, 12, 3, 19, 8, H = 19 and L = 3, so the range is 16 (not 19 − 5).
- A quality controller at a bottling plant who checks each batch by subtracting the smallest fill volume from the largest is using the **range**. Quality control charts use the range because it is fast.

### Lesson 4.3: Interquartile range and quartile deviation

**Must know**

- **Interquartile range (IQR)** = Q3 − Q1. It is the spread of the **middle 50%** of the observations.
- **Quartile deviation (QD)**, also called the **semi-interquartile range** = (Q3 − Q1) ÷ 2.
- Rearranged: Q3 = Q1 + 2 × QD; Q1 = Q3 − 2 × QD.
- **Coefficient of quartile deviation** (relative) = (Q3 − Q1) ÷ (Q3 + Q1).
- **Ungrouped data, (n + 1) position method:** arrange in ascending order; Q1 is at position (n + 1) ÷ 4, Q3 at position 3(n + 1) ÷ 4. If the position is not a whole number, interpolate between the two neighbouring values.
- **Grouped data:** Q1 position = N ÷ 4, Q3 position = 3N ÷ 4 (N = Σf), then
  - Qk = L + ((kN/4 − F) ÷ f) × h
  - L = lower class boundary of the quartile class; F = cumulative frequency before that class; f = frequency of the quartile class; h = class width; k = 1 or 3.

| Merits of QD | Demerits of QD |
|---|---|
| **Least affected by extreme values** | **Ignores the lowest 25% and highest 25%** of observations |
| **Best measure for open-ended classes** (e.g. "Below 10", "60 and above") | Not based on all observations |
| Easy to understand | Not suitable for further algebraic treatment |

**Explain more**

*Worked example 1 (whole-number positions).* Monthly data spending (₦'00) of 11 students: 15, 8, 22, 12, 30, 18, 10, 25, 14, 20, 16.

- Ordered: 8, 10, 12, 14, 15, 16, 18, 20, 22, 25, 30 (n = 11).
- Q1 position = (11 + 1) ÷ 4 = 3rd value = 12.
- Q3 position = 3 × 12 ÷ 4 = 9th value = 22.
- IQR = 22 − 12 = 10; QD = 10 ÷ 2 = 5.
- Coefficient of QD = (22 − 12) ÷ (22 + 12) = 10 ÷ 34 = 0.2941 (4 d.p.).

*Worked example 2 (interpolation).* Ordered marks of 10 students: 12, 15, 17, 20, 22, 25, 28, 30, 35, 40.

- Q1 position = 11 ÷ 4 = 2.75: Q1 = 2nd value + 0.75 × (3rd − 2nd) = 15 + 0.75 × (17 − 15) = 16.5.
- Q3 position = 33 ÷ 4 = 8.25: Q3 = 8th value + 0.25 × (9th − 8th) = 30 + 0.25 × (35 − 30) = 31.25.
- IQR = 31.25 − 16.5 = 14.75; QD = 7.375.
- Read it as: the middle 50% of students scored within a span of 14.75 marks, between 16.5 and 31.25. It does **not** mean half scored below 14.75.

*Worked example 3 (grouped).* Classes 0–10 (4), 10–20 (6), 20–30 (10), 30–40 (12), 40–50 (8). N = 40. Cumulative frequencies: 4, 10, 20, 32, 40.

- Q1 position = 40 ÷ 4 = 10, which falls in 10–20 (cf reaches 10 there). L = 10, F = 4, f = 6, h = 10.
- Q1 = 10 + ((10 − 4) ÷ 6) × 10 = 10 + 10 = 20.
- Q3 position = 3 × 40 ÷ 4 = 30, which falls in 30–40. L = 30, F = 20, f = 12, h = 10.
- Q3 = 30 + ((30 − 20) ÷ 12) × 10 = 30 + 8.3333 = 38.3333.
- QD = (38.3333 − 20) ÷ 2 = 9.1667 (4 d.p.).

*Quick reverse.* Q1 = 104 and QD = 8 give Q3 = 104 + 2 × 8 = 120.

- Conventions: ungrouped questions here use the (n + 1) method; other books use n ÷ 4 or other interpolation rules, which can give slightly different quartiles. Grouped questions use N ÷ 4 and 3N ÷ 4 (not N + 1).
- Trap: (Q3 − Q1) ÷ 2 is the QD, not the IQR. Q1 − Q3 is negative and is never used.

### Lesson 4.4: The mean deviation

**Must know**

- **Mean deviation (MD)**, also called **mean absolute deviation**: the arithmetic mean of the **absolute** deviations of the observations from an average. It is the measure based on the **absolute values of deviations**.
- MD can be taken about the **mean, the median or the mode** (all three are possible).
- About the mean (ungrouped): MD = Σ|x − x̄| ÷ n.
- About the median: MD = Σ|x − median| ÷ n.
- Grouped or frequency data: MD = Σf|x − x̄| ÷ Σf (x = value or class midpoint).
- **Why absolute values:** the sum of deviations from the mean, with signs kept, is **always zero**, so signs must be ignored.
- **Minimum property:** MD is **smallest when measured from the median**.
- **Coefficient of mean deviation** (relative) = MD ÷ average used. About the mean: MD ÷ x̄.

| Merits of MD | Demerits of MD |
|---|---|
| Based on all observations | **Ignores algebraic signs**, so unsuitable for further algebraic treatment |
| Easy to understand | Affected by extreme values (less than the SD) |
| Less affected by extremes than the SD | Cannot be found for open-ended classes |

**Explain more**

*Worked example 1 (about the mean).* Textbooks sold by a campus bookshop: 6, 8, 11, 13, 17.

- Mean = 55 ÷ 5 = 11.
- Deviations: −5, −3, 0, 2, 6 (they sum to 0, as always).
- Absolute deviations: 5, 3, 0, 2, 6; total 16.
- MD = 16 ÷ 5 = 3.2.
- Coefficient of MD = 3.2 ÷ 11 = 0.2909 (4 d.p.).

*Worked example 2 (about the median).* Waiting times (minutes): 4, 6, 9, 15, 26.

- Median = 9 (middle of 5 ordered values).
- Absolute deviations from 9: 5, 3, 0, 6, 17; total 31.
- MD about median = 31 ÷ 5 = 6.2.
- Check the minimum property: mean = 60 ÷ 5 = 12; absolute deviations from 12 are 8, 6, 3, 3, 14 = 34; MD about mean = 6.8, larger than 6.2.

*Worked example 3 (frequency data).* x: 2, 4, 6, 8 with f: 1, 4, 3, 2 (Σf = 10).

- Σfx = 2 + 16 + 18 + 16 = 52; mean = 52 ÷ 10 = 5.2.
- |x − 5.2|: 3.2, 1.2, 0.8, 2.8.
- f|x − 5.2|: 3.2, 4.8, 2.4, 5.6; total 16.
- MD = 16 ÷ 10 = 1.6.

- For grouped classes use midpoints as x. Mean deviation and arithmetic mean together: "MD in the numerator, mean in the denominator" is the **coefficient of mean deviation**. For example MD 5 and mean 110 give 5 ÷ 110 = 0.045 (3 d.p.).

### Lesson 4.5: Variance and standard deviation

**Must know**

- **Variance:** the **mean of the squared deviations** of the observations from their mean.
- **Standard deviation (SD):** the **positive square root of the variance**; the **root-mean-square deviation from the mean**. Deviations are taken **only from the mean**. It is the **most commonly used** measure of dispersion.
- Symbols: population variance σ² ("sigma squared"), population SD σ; sample variance s², sample SD s. So variance = σ², SD = √variance.
- **Sum of squares:** Σ(x − x̄)², the sum of squared deviations from the mean.
- **Population** (divide by N): σ² = Σ(x − μ)² ÷ N.
- **Sample** (divide by n − 1): s² = Σ(x − x̄)² ÷ (n − 1). Dividing by n − 1 gives an **unbiased estimate of the population variance**.
- **Shortcut (population):** σ² = Σx² ÷ N − (mean)².
- **Frequency or grouped data (population):** σ² = Σf(x − x̄)² ÷ Σf = Σfx² ÷ Σf − (mean)², with x = class midpoint.
- Converting: sample variance = population variance × n ÷ (n − 1).
- **Units:** SD has the same unit as the data; **variance is in squared units** (naira squared), so variance is the measure whose unit differs from that of the data.
- SD is **zero when all observations are equal** and **can never be negative**.

| Merits of SD | Demerits of SD |
|---|---|
| **Based on all observations** | Harder to compute than range or QD |
| **Suitable for further algebraic treatment** (used in sampling, skewness, normal distribution) | Affected by extreme values (squares magnify them) |
| Least affected by sampling fluctuations; most widely used | Cannot be found for open-ended classes; is an absolute measure, so not for comparing different units |

**Explain more**

*Convention.* Unless a question says "sample" or "n − 1", treat the data as a population and divide by n. When it says sample, divide by n − 1. Read every question for this word.

*Worked example 1 (ungrouped).* Weekly hours of five staff: 4, 7, 9, 12, 18.

- Mean = 50 ÷ 5 = 10.
- Deviations: −6, −3, −1, 2, 8.
- Squares: 36, 9, 1, 4, 64; sum of squares = 114.
- Population variance = 114 ÷ 5 = 22.8; population SD = √22.8 = 4.7749 (4 d.p.).
- Sample variance = 114 ÷ 4 = 28.5; sample SD = √28.5 = 5.3385 (4 d.p.).
- Shortcut check: Σx² = 16 + 49 + 81 + 144 + 324 = 614; 614 ÷ 5 − 10² = 122.8 − 100 = 22.8.
- Conversion check: 22.8 × 5 ÷ 4 = 28.5.

*Worked example 2 (from totals).* N = 10, Σx = 120, Σx² = 1,600: mean = 12; variance = 1,600 ÷ 10 − 12² = 160 − 144 = 16; SD = 4.

*Worked example 3 (grouped).* Monthly savings (₦'000) of 20 traders:

| Class | f | Midpoint x | fx | fx² |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0–20 | 3 | 10 | 30 | 300 |
| 20–40 | 7 | 30 | 210 | 6,300 |
| 40–60 | 6 | 50 | 300 | 15,000 |
| 60–80 | 4 | 70 | 280 | 19,600 |
| Total | 20 | | 820 | 41,200 |

- Mean = 820 ÷ 20 = 41.
- Variance = 41,200 ÷ 20 − 41² = 2,060 − 1,681 = 379.
- SD = √379 = 19.4679, that is about ₦19,468 (4 d.p. in ₦'000).
- For classes written 10–19, 20–29, use midpoints 14.5, 24.5 and so on.

*Square and square root.* SD 6 gives variance 36; variance 49 gives SD 7; variance 16 gives SD 4; SD 64 gives variance 4,096 (not 8). SD ₦7,000 gives variance 49,000,000 naira squared.

*Effect of changing the data (change of origin and scale):*

| Change to every value | SD | Variance | Range, QD, MD | Mean |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Add or subtract a constant c | Unchanged | Unchanged | Unchanged | Changes by c |
| Multiply by k | × k (size of k) | × k² | × k (size of k) | × k |

- SD 40, add 5 to every number: new SD = 40. SD ₦12,000, every salary up by ₦5,000: SD stays ₦12,000.
- SD 8, double every value: new SD = 16. SD ₦4, triple every price: ₦12. Multiply by 5: SD is multiplied by 5.
- Variance 9, double every value: variance = 9 × 2² = 36.
- If y = −2x + 10 and SD of x = 5: SD of y = 2 × 5 = 10. The +10 has no effect, and SD is never negative, so drop the minus sign.
- Trap: five students who all scored 14, or five salaries all ₦55,000, have SD = 0.
- The term "standard deviation" was introduced by Karl Pearson.

### Lesson 4.6: Coefficient of variation and comparing distributions

**Must know**

- **Coefficient of variation (CV):** the standard deviation expressed as a **percentage of the mean**. It is **relative and unit-free**.
- CV = (SD ÷ mean) × 100%.
- Rearranged: SD = CV × mean ÷ 100; mean = SD × 100 ÷ CV.
- **Coefficient of standard deviation** = SD ÷ mean (the same ratio, not multiplied by 100).
- **Use:** comparing the relative variability of data sets with **different units or scales** or very different means. It is the **best measure for comparing the variability of two series**.
- **Lower CV = more consistent (uniform, stable). Higher CV = more variable.**
- Adding a constant changes the mean but not the SD, so the **CV changes**.

| Relative measure | Formula |
|---|---|
| Coefficient of range | (H − L) ÷ (H + L) |
| Coefficient of quartile deviation | (Q3 − Q1) ÷ (Q3 + Q1) |
| Coefficient of mean deviation | MD ÷ mean (or MD ÷ median) |
| Coefficient of standard deviation | SD ÷ mean |
| Coefficient of variation | (SD ÷ mean) × 100% |

**Explain more**

*Worked example 1.* Using the savings data above: CV = (19.4679 ÷ 41) × 100% = 47.48% (2 d.p.).

*Worked example 2 (comparing).* Firm A pays a mean salary of ₦120,000 with SD ₦18,000; Firm B pays a mean of ₦60,000 with SD ₦12,000.

- CV of A = 18,000 ÷ 120,000 × 100% = 15%.
- CV of B = 12,000 ÷ 60,000 × 100% = 20%.
- Firm A's salaries are **more consistent**, even though its SD is larger. Firm B's are relatively more variable. Never compare raw SDs when the means differ.

*Worked example 3 (reverse).*

- Mean 10, CV 5%: SD = 5 × 10 ÷ 100 = 0.5.
- Mean 64, CV 25%: SD = 25 × 64 ÷ 100 = 16.
- SD 8, CV 20%: mean = 8 × 100 ÷ 20 = 40.
- Mean ₦80,000, SD ₦12,000: CV = 12,000 ÷ 80,000 × 100% = 15%.

*Worked example 4 (adding a constant).* Mean 50, SD 10: CV = 20%. Add 10 to every value: mean 60, SD still 10, CV = 10 ÷ 60 × 100% = 16.67% (2 d.p.).

- Heights in cm and weights in kg of UNILAG freshers cannot be compared with SDs or variances because the units differ; use the CV.
- A machine with CV 40% is more variable relative to its mean than one with CV 20%. That does not say it has twice the mean, or a larger SD in naira.

**Summary of the measures**

| Fact | Answer |
|---|---|
| Uses only two observations; quickest; most affected by extremes | Range |
| Middle 50%; least affected by extremes; open-ended classes | Quartile deviation (and IQR) |
| Absolute deviations; least when taken about the median | Mean deviation |
| Root-mean-square deviation from the mean; most commonly used | Standard deviation |
| Unit is the square of the data unit | Variance |
| Unit-free; best for comparing series | Coefficient of variation |

### One-minute recap

- Dispersion (variation, spread, scatter) = how far values spread about the average; never negative; zero when all values are equal.
- Absolute measures keep the data's unit; relative measures (all "coefficients") are unit-free.
- Range = H − L; coefficient = (H − L) ÷ (H + L); quickest, two values only, most hurt by extremes.
- IQR = Q3 − Q1 (middle 50%); QD = (Q3 − Q1) ÷ 2; coefficient = (Q3 − Q1) ÷ (Q3 + Q1); best for open-ended classes.
- MD = Σ|x − x̄| ÷ n; signs ignored because Σ(x − x̄) = 0; least about the median.
- Population variance = Σ(x − μ)² ÷ N = Σx² ÷ N − mean²; sample variance divides by n − 1 (unbiased).
- SD = √variance; variance is in squared units.
- Adding a constant: SD unchanged. Multiplying by k: SD × k, variance × k².
- CV = SD ÷ mean × 100%; lower CV = more consistent.

### Theory practice

**Question:** The daily sales (₦'000) of a roadside stall over five days were 8, 12, 15, 9 and 16. Treating them as a population, find the standard deviation and the coefficient of variation.

*Solution:*

- Mean = (8 + 12 + 15 + 9 + 16) ÷ 5 = 60 ÷ 5 = 12.
- Deviations: −4, 0, 3, −3, 4.
- Squares: 16, 0, 9, 9, 16; sum of squares = 50.
- Variance = 50 ÷ 5 = 10.
- SD = √10 = 3.1623 (4 d.p.), about ₦3,162.
- CV = 3.1623 ÷ 12 × 100% = 26.35% (2 d.p.).
