## Topic 5: Skewness and Kurtosis

**What you must know:** what skewness and kurtosis mean; how the mean, median and mode sit in symmetrical, positively and negatively skewed distributions; the empirical relationship Mode = 3Median − 2Mean; Pearson's two coefficients, Bowley's coefficient and the moment coefficients of skewness; central moments; β2, excess kurtosis and the percentile coefficient of kurtosis; leptokurtic, mesokurtic and platykurtic curves.

### Lesson 5.1: Skewness and the position of the averages

**Must know**

- **Shape:** data are described by three characteristics: **location** (where values are concentrated), **dispersion** (how spread out they are about the centre) and **shape** (skewness). Skewness tells you whether very large or very small values, relative to the average, have occurred.
- **Skewness:** the **lack of symmetry** in a frequency distribution; the degree to which a distribution departs from symmetry about its central value. A **skewed** distribution has most of its values spread more to one side (left or right) of the mode.
- **Symmetrical distribution:** the two halves either side of the centre are mirror images. Mean = median = mode. Skewness = 0. The normal curve is the standard example.
- **Asymmetrical (skewed) distribution:** the mean, median and mode are **not** equal.

| Shape | Long tail | Order of averages | Sign of coefficient |
|---|---|---|---|
| Symmetrical | none (equal tails) | Mean = Median = Mode | 0 |
| Positively (right) skewed | to the **right** (few very high values) | Mean > Median > Mode | + |
| Negatively (left) skewed | to the **left** (few very low values) | Mean < Median < Mode | − |

- In a moderately skewed distribution the **median lies between the mean and the mode**.
- The **mean is pulled towards the tail** because it uses every value, so: mean > median means positive skew; mean < median means negative skew.

**Explain more**

- Picture salaries in a Lagos firm: most workers earn modest amounts, but a few directors earn extremely high salaries. Those few high values stretch the tail to the right and drag the mean up, so the salaries are **positively skewed** (mean > median > mode). Rents and household incomes behave the same way.
- Now picture an easy quiz in a UNILAG class: most students score near the maximum but a few score very low. The few low values make a long tail to the left, so the scores are **negatively skewed** (mean < median < mode).
- Quick reads from summary figures:
    - Mean ₦75,000, median ₦60,000: mean > median, so **positively skewed**.
    - Second-hand phones at Computer Village: mean ₦32,000, median ₦35,000, mode ₦37,000. Mean < median < mode, so **negatively skewed**.
    - If the mode is greater than the median and the median is greater than the mean, that is the same order read backwards: **negatively (left) skewed**.
- A coefficient of skewness of −0.8 means the distribution is **skewed to the left**; +0.8 would mean skewed to the right; 0 means symmetrical.
- **Trap:** the direction of skew is named after the **tail**, not after the hump. In a right-skewed distribution the hump (mode) sits on the left and the tail runs right.
- **Trap:** "more peaked" or "flatter" describes **kurtosis**, not skewness. "Mesokurtic" and "platykurtic" are never answers to a question about skew.

### Lesson 5.2: The empirical relationship between mean, median and mode

**Must know**

For a **moderately skewed** (not too asymmetrical) unimodal distribution, Karl Pearson's empirical relationship is:

- **Mode = 3 Median − 2 Mean**
- equivalently **Mean − Mode = 3(Mean − Median)**

Rearranged to find any one of the three:

1. Mode = 3Median − 2Mean
2. Median = (Mode + 2Mean) ÷ 3
3. Mean = (3Median − Mode) ÷ 2

**Explain more**

- The relationship says the distance from the mean to the mode is about **three times** the distance from the mean to the median, which is why the median sits between the other two, about one-third of the way from the mean to the mode.
- **Worked example 1 (find the mode).** Mean = 11, median = 13.
  Mode = 3(13) − 2(11) = 39 − 22 = **17**.
- **Worked example 2 (find the median).** Mode = 14, mean = 5.
  Median = (14 + 2 × 5) ÷ 3 = (14 + 10) ÷ 3 = 24 ÷ 3 = **8**.
- **Worked example 3 (find the mean).** Daily bread sales: median 30 loaves, mode 27 loaves.
  3Median − Mode = 2Mean, so Mean = (3 × 30 − 27) ÷ 2 = (90 − 27) ÷ 2 = 63 ÷ 2 = **31.5 loaves**.
- **Worked example 4 (decimals).** Household sizes in Ajegunle: mean 6.0, median 5.6.
  Mode = 3(5.6) − 2(6.0) = 16.8 − 12.0 = **4.8**.
- **Check your answer:** the median must fall between the mean and the mode. In example 2, 5 < 8 < 14. If your median falls outside, you have made an algebra slip.
- **Trap:** the options often swap the multipliers, e.g. "2(Mean − Median)" or "3(Median − Mode)". Only **Mean − Mode = 3(Mean − Median)** is correct.

### Lesson 5.3: Pearson's coefficients of skewness

**Must know**

- **Pearson's first coefficient of skewness (mode method):**
  Sk1 = (Mean − Mode) ÷ Standard deviation
- **Pearson's second coefficient of skewness (median method):**
  Sk2 = 3(Mean − Median) ÷ Standard deviation
- Both use the **mean** and the **standard deviation**; the first adds the **mode**, the second the **median**. Neither uses the interquartile range or quartiles.
- Sign: positive = positive skew; negative = negative skew; zero = symmetrical. Sk2 lies between −3 and +3; in practice values are usually well inside ±1.
- Sk2 is used when the mode is ill-defined (or there is more than one mode). By the empirical relationship the two give about the same value in a moderately skewed distribution.

**Explain more**

- **Worked example 1 (first coefficient).** Weekly earnings of okada riders: mean ₦52,000, mode ₦46,000, SD ₦8,000.
  Sk1 = (52,000 − 46,000) ÷ 8,000 = 6,000 ÷ 8,000 = **0.75** (positive skew).
- **Worked example 2 (second coefficient).** GST test scores: mean 45, median 48, SD 6.
  Sk2 = 3(45 − 48) ÷ 6 = 3(−3) ÷ 6 = −9 ÷ 6 = **−1.5** (negative skew).
  Trap: forgetting the 3 gives −0.5; always multiply by 3 before dividing.
- **Worked example 3 (mode not given).** Mean 75, median 72, SD 12; find Sk1.
  Step 1: Mode = 3(72) − 2(75) = 216 − 150 = 66.
  Step 2: Sk1 = (75 − 66) ÷ 12 = 9 ÷ 12 = **0.75**.
  Notice Sk2 = 3(75 − 72) ÷ 12 = 9 ÷ 12 = 0.75 too: the empirical relationship makes them agree.
- **Worked example 4 (working backwards).** Sk2 = 0.6, mean 50, median 47; find the SD.
  0.6 = 3(50 − 47) ÷ SD = 9 ÷ SD, so SD = 9 ÷ 0.6 = **15**.
- **Convention:** use the standard deviation exactly as the question gives it. If you must calculate it, use the divisor the question specifies (population, n; or sample, n − 1). Topic 4 covers both.
- **Trap:** the **coefficient of variation** (SD ÷ mean × 100) is a measure of **relative dispersion**, not of skewness.

### Lesson 5.4: Bowley's (quartile) coefficient of skewness

**Must know**

- **Bowley's coefficient of skewness** is based on the **quartiles**:
  SkB = (Q3 + Q1 − 2Q2) ÷ (Q3 − Q1)
  which is the same as [(Q3 − Q2) − (Q2 − Q1)] ÷ (Q3 − Q1), where Q2 = median.
- It always lies between **−1 and +1**.
- Positive when the upper quartile is farther from the median than the lower quartile; negative when the reverse; zero when Q1 and Q3 are equally far from the median.
- It is the most suitable measure when the distribution has **open-ended classes** (e.g. "Under ₦10,000" or "₦100,000 and above"), or extreme values, because the mean and SD cannot then be found reliably, but the quartiles can.

**Explain more**

- **Worked example 1.** Monthly shop rents in a Lagos plaza (₦'000): Q1 = 20, Q2 = 30, Q3 = 50.
  Numerator = 50 + 20 − 2(30) = 70 − 60 = 10. Denominator = 50 − 20 = 30.
  SkB = 10 ÷ 30 = **0.3333** (positive skew; the top quarter is spread out).
- **Worked example 2.** Q1 = 40, median = 55, Q3 = 60.
  Numerator = 60 + 40 − 110 = −10. Denominator = 60 − 40 = 20.
  SkB = −10 ÷ 20 = **−0.5** (negative skew).
- **Worked example 3 (grouped data, full working).** Weekly sales (units) of 40 sales representatives:

| Class | Boundaries | f | Cumulative f |
|---|---|---|---|
| 10-19 | 9.5-19.5 | 4 | 4 |
| 20-29 | 19.5-29.5 | 7 | 11 |
| 30-39 | 29.5-39.5 | 12 | 23 |
| 40-49 | 39.5-49.5 | 10 | 33 |
| 50-59 | 49.5-59.5 | 5 | 38 |
| 60-69 | 59.5-69.5 | 2 | 40 |

  Formula: Quartile = L + [(position − F) ÷ f] × c, where L = lower class boundary, F = cumulative frequency before the class, f = class frequency, c = class width (10). For grouped data the positions are n/4, n/2 and 3n/4 (here 10, 20 and 30).
  Q1: position 10 lies in 19.5-29.5. Q1 = 19.5 + [(10 − 4) ÷ 7] × 10 = 19.5 + 8.571 = 28.07.
  Q2: position 20 lies in 29.5-39.5. Q2 = 29.5 + [(20 − 11) ÷ 12] × 10 = 29.5 + 7.5 = 37.0.
  Q3: position 30 lies in 39.5-49.5. Q3 = 39.5 + [(30 − 23) ÷ 10] × 10 = 39.5 + 7 = 46.5.
  SkB = (46.5 + 28.07 − 74.0) ÷ (46.5 − 28.07) = 0.57 ÷ 18.43 = **0.031** (to 3 d.p.).
  The distribution is **very slightly positively skewed**, close to symmetrical but not exactly 0.
- For ungrouped data, Topic 3 uses position (n + 1)/4 for Q1 and (n + 1)/2 for the median; use whichever convention the question states.
- **Trap:** Bowley's uses quartiles; Pearson's use mean, mode or median and SD; the moment coefficient uses the third and second central moments. Exams swap these.

### Lesson 5.5: Moments and the moment coefficient of skewness

**Must know**

- **r-th central moment (about the mean):** mr = Σ(x − mean)^r ÷ n; for a frequency table mr = Σf(x − mean)^r ÷ Σf.
    1. **m1 = 0** always (deviations from the mean sum to zero).
    2. **m2 = variance** (so √m2 = standard deviation).
    3. **m3** measures **skewness** (its sign gives the direction).
    4. **m4** measures **kurtosis**.
- **Raw moments** (about zero): m′r = Σx^r ÷ n. Conversions:
  m2 = m′2 − (m′1)²
  m3 = m′3 − 3m′2m′1 + 2(m′1)³
  m4 = m′4 − 4m′3m′1 + 6m′2(m′1)² − 3(m′1)⁴
- **Moment coefficient of skewness:** β1 = m3² ÷ m2³. Because m3 is squared, **β1 is never negative**; β1 = 0 for a symmetrical distribution.
- **γ1 = m3 ÷ m2^1.5 (= m3 ÷ SD³)** keeps the sign of m3; γ1 = ±√β1.
- For a symmetrical distribution, **m3 = 0 and β1 = 0**.

**Explain more**

- **Worked example (raw data).** Weekly sales of five kiosks (₦'000): 2, 4, 6, 8, 20.
  Mean = 40 ÷ 5 = 8. Deviations (x − 8): −6, −4, −2, 0, 12 (sum 0, so m1 = 0).

| x | d = x − 8 | d² | d³ | d⁴ |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2 | −6 | 36 | −216 | 1,296 |
| 4 | −4 | 16 | −64 | 256 |
| 6 | −2 | 4 | −8 | 16 |
| 8 | 0 | 0 | 0 | 0 |
| 20 | 12 | 144 | 1,728 | 20,736 |
| Total | 0 | 200 | 1,440 | 22,304 |

  m2 = 200 ÷ 5 = 40 (variance; SD = √40 = 6.325).
  m3 = 1,440 ÷ 5 = 288 (positive: the ₦20,000 kiosk makes a long right tail).
  m4 = 22,304 ÷ 5 = 4,460.8.
  β1 = 288² ÷ 40³ = 82,944 ÷ 64,000 = **1.296**.
  γ1 = 288 ÷ 40^1.5 = 288 ÷ 252.98 = **1.138** (to 3 d.p.), positive skew.
  Check with raw moments: m′1 = 8, m′2 = 520 ÷ 5 = 104, m′3 = 8,800 ÷ 5 = 1,760. m3 = 1,760 − 3(104)(8) + 2(512) = 1,760 − 2,496 + 1,024 = 288. Same answer.
- **Worked example (given moments).** m2 = 4, m3 = −4.
  β1 = (−4)² ÷ 4³ = 16 ÷ 64 = **0.25**. The negative sign disappears in β1; γ1 = −4 ÷ 4^1.5 = −4 ÷ 8 = −0.5 shows the negative skew. If asked for β1, answer 0.25.
- **Convention:** moments here divide by n (or Σf), not n − 1.

### Lesson 5.6: Kurtosis

**Must know**

- **Kurtosis:** the degree of **peakedness or flatness** of a frequency curve compared with the **normal curve**. Skewness concerns **symmetry**; kurtosis concerns **peakedness** (and tail weight).
- **Moment coefficient of kurtosis:** β2 = m4 ÷ m2².
- **Excess kurtosis:** γ2 = β2 − 3 (0 for a normal distribution).

| Type | Shape | β2 | Excess (β2 − 3) |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Leptokurtic** | more peaked than normal, fatter tails | > 3 | > 0 |
| **Mesokurtic** | as peaked as normal (the normal curve) | = 3 | = 0 |
| **Platykurtic** | flatter than normal, thinner tails | < 3 | < 0 |

- **Percentile coefficient of kurtosis:** k = QD ÷ (P90 − P10), where QD = (Q3 − Q1) ÷ 2 is the quartile deviation (semi-interquartile range). For the normal curve **k = 0.263**; k < 0.263 suggests leptokurtic, k > 0.263 platykurtic.

**Explain more**

- Memory aids: **lepto** = slender (tall, thin peak); **platy** = flat (like a plate); **meso** = middle.
- **Worked example 1.** m2 = 4, m4 = 60. β2 = 60 ÷ 4² = 60 ÷ 16 = **3.75**. Leptokurtic (excess 0.75).
- **Worked example 2.** m2 = 5, m4 = 50. β2 = 50 ÷ 25 = 2. Excess = 2 − 3 = **−1**. Platykurtic.
- **Worked example 3.** Daily returns on a Lagos-listed share (NGX): m2 = 3, m4 = 36. β2 = 36 ÷ 9 = **4**, greater than 3, so **leptokurtic**. Share returns often have fat tails: big jumps happen more often than a normal curve predicts.
- **Worked example 4.** Exam marks: m2 = 2, m4 = 8. β2 = 8 ÷ 4 = **2**, less than 3, so **platykurtic**.
- **Worked example 5 (skewness and kurtosis together).** m2 = 9, m3 = 0, m4 = 243.
  β1 = 0² ÷ 9³ = 0, so symmetric. β2 = 243 ÷ 81 = 3, so mesokurtic. The distribution is **symmetric and mesokurtic**, like the normal curve.
- **Worked example 6.** β2 = 4.2. Excess = 4.2 − 3 = **1.2**, positive, so leptokurtic. Trap: "1.2, platykurtic" has the right number but the wrong label.
- **Worked example 7 (percentile coefficient).** Q1 = 30, Q3 = 50, P10 = 20, P90 = 70.
  QD = (50 − 30) ÷ 2 = 10. P90 − P10 = 70 − 20 = 50. k = 10 ÷ 50 = **0.2** (below 0.263, leptokurtic).
  Another: Q1 = 20, Q3 = 44, P10 = 10, P90 = 50. QD = 12; k = 12 ÷ 40 = 0.3 (above 0.263, platykurtic).
- From Lesson 5.5's kiosks: β2 = 4,460.8 ÷ 40² = 4,460.8 ÷ 1,600 = 2.788 (to 3 d.p.), slightly platykurtic (excess −0.212).
- **Traps:** for the normal curve β2 = **3** (not 0 or 1), excess = **0**, k = **0.263**. Excess kurtosis is β2 − 3, never β1 − 3 or β2 + 3. m4 ÷ m2 (not squared) is not β2.

### One-minute recap

- Skewness = lack of symmetry; kurtosis = peakedness or flatness compared with the normal curve.
- Symmetrical: mean = median = mode. Positive (right tail): mean > median > mode. Negative (left tail): mean < median < mode. The median lies between the mean and mode.
- Empirical relationship: Mode = 3Median − 2Mean, i.e. Mean − Mode = 3(Mean − Median).
- Pearson: Sk1 = (Mean − Mode) ÷ SD; Sk2 = 3(Mean − Median) ÷ SD.
- Bowley: (Q3 + Q1 − 2Q2) ÷ (Q3 − Q1), between −1 and +1; best for open-ended classes.
- Central moments: m1 = 0, m2 = variance, m3 for skewness, m4 for kurtosis.
- β1 = m3² ÷ m2³ (never negative; 0 if symmetric); γ1 = m3 ÷ m2^1.5 keeps the sign.
- β2 = m4 ÷ m2²: > 3 leptokurtic, = 3 mesokurtic (normal), < 3 platykurtic. Excess = β2 − 3.
- Percentile coefficient k = QD ÷ (P90 − P10); 0.263 for the normal curve.
- Coefficient of variation measures relative dispersion, not skewness.

### Theory practice

**Question:** The monthly allowances of UNILAG students have mean ₦38,000, median ₦35,000 and standard deviation ₦10,000. Find the mode using the empirical relationship, then Pearson's first and second coefficients of skewness, and describe the shape.

*Solution:*
Mode = 3(35,000) − 2(38,000) = 105,000 − 76,000 = ₦29,000.
Sk1 = (38,000 − 29,000) ÷ 10,000 = 9,000 ÷ 10,000 = 0.9.
Sk2 = 3(38,000 − 35,000) ÷ 10,000 = 9,000 ÷ 10,000 = 0.9.
Mean > median > mode and both coefficients are positive, so the allowances are **positively (right) skewed**: a few students receive much larger allowances than the rest.
