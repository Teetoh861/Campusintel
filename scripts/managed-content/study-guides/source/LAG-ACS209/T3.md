## Topic 3: Measures of Location

**What you must know:** what location and central tendency mean; the arithmetic, weighted, combined, geometric and harmonic means; the median and the mode; quartiles, deciles and percentiles; every formula for ungrouped data, data with frequencies and grouped data; which average suits which data.

### Lesson 3.1: Location, central tendency and choosing an average

**Must know**

- **Three characteristics** describe the data profile of a random variable: (1) **location** (central and non-central), (2) **spread or dispersion**, (3) **shape (skewness)**. Correlation is **not** one of them.
- **Location:** where the data values are **concentrated**. Dispersion = how widely values spread about the centre. Skewness = shape or degree of symmetry.
- **Measure of central tendency (average, central value):** a single value that represents the whole data set; the centre around which observations tend to cluster.
- **Central location statistic:** a single number that gives a sense of the "centrality" of the data values in a sample.
- **Central location measures:** (a) the mean (arithmetic, geometric, harmonic), (b) the median (also called the second quartile, middle quartile or 50th percentile), (c) the mode (modal value). **Non-central location measures (fractiles):** quartiles, deciles, percentiles; they identify "off-centre" reference points.
- **Mathematical averages:** arithmetic, geometric and harmonic means (calculated from every value). **Positional averages:** median, quartiles, deciles, percentiles (found by position), and the mode.
- **Notation:** population mean = **μ** (Greek mu), Σx ÷ N; sample mean = **x̄** (x-bar), Σx ÷ n. Greek letters for population parameters, Roman letters for sample statistics.
- **Outlier (extreme value):** an unusually high or low value that stands out conspicuously from the rest.
- **Three data layouts:** Type I ungrouped data without frequency; Type II ungrouped data with frequency; Type III grouped data with frequency.

| Measure | Data types | Affected by outliers? | Always exists? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Arithmetic mean | numeric (interval, ratio) only | yes, distorted | yes, unique |
| Median | numeric only | no | yes, unique |
| Mode | categorical and numeric | no | no; may not exist or may not be unique |

**Choosing the best central measure** depends mainly on (a) the **data type** and (b) **whether outliers are present**.

1. Categorical (nominal or ordinal) data: the **mode** is the only valid measure.
2. Numeric data with no outliers: the **mean**.
3. Numeric data with genuine outliers: the **median** (preferred to the mode because it can be used in further analysis).
4. Outliers proved to be recording errors and removed: the **mean** is again the best measure.

**Explain more**

- Think of a Lagos electronics dealer's daily sales over a year: the average daily sales is a measure of central location, how far daily sales swing around that average is dispersion, and whether a few very large or very small days occurred is skewness.
- Statements and the measure they use: "the average salary per grade" or "the mean age of our employees" = mean; "the most popular healthcare plan" or "the detergent brand customers buy most often" = **mode**; "half of our employees spend less than ₦17,000 a month commuting" = **median**.
- The mean is the average that takes into account the **actual magnitude of every value**, so it is the **most sensitive to extreme values** and the most affected by an outlier in a small data set. The median is the most **robust** (resistant) measure when outliers are present.
- For extreme values, also remember this guide: very high extreme value, harmonic mean is suitable; moderately high extreme value, geometric mean; no extreme value, arithmetic mean.
- Standard deviation, range and variance are measures of **dispersion**, not central tendency, and they do **not** divide ordered data into equal parts.

### Lesson 3.2: The arithmetic mean and its relatives

**Must know**

- **Arithmetic mean:** the sum of the values divided by the number of values. Ungrouped: mean = Σx ÷ n.
- **With frequencies:** mean = Σfx ÷ Σf.
- **Grouped data:** mean = Σfx ÷ Σf, where **x is the class midpoint** = (lower limit + upper limit) ÷ 2.
- **Assumed mean (shortcut) methods:** with d = x − A, mean = A + Σfd ÷ Σf. With coded deviations u = (x − A) ÷ h (h = class width), **mean = A + h × (Σfu ÷ Σf)**. Some questions call the coded deviation d; whenever the deviation is defined as (x − A) ÷ h, multiply by h.
- **Weighted mean:** mean = Σwx ÷ Σw (w = weights such as credit units, units sold, numbers of people).
- **Combined (pooled) mean:** mean = (n1 × mean1 + n2 × mean2) ÷ (n1 + n2).
- **Rearranging:** Σx = n × mean; n = Σx ÷ mean.
- **Properties:**
  1. Σ(x − mean) = **0** always.
  2. Σ(x − mean)² is **less** than the sum of squared deviations from any other value (least squares property).
  3. If every value is multiplied by a and then b is added, the new mean = a × old mean + b.
- **2 advantages:** it uses **all** the data values; it is an **unbiased** statistic (on average it represents the true mean). These make it the most widely used measure.
- **2 drawbacks:** not appropriate for categorical (nominal or ordinal) data, so only for **interval and ratio** data; it is **distorted by outliers**.

**Explain more**

*Ungrouped:* 2, 4, 5, 8, 5, 10, 5, 4. Σx = 43, n = 8, mean = 43 ÷ 8 = 5.375. An outlier example: the mean of 3, 4, 6, 7 is 5, but the mean of 3, 4, 6, 39 is 13, which is not typical of most values. Likewise 10, 12, 14, 16 have mean 13 and median 13; replace 16 by 64 and the mean jumps to 100 ÷ 4 = 25 while the median stays 13.

*With frequency:* x = 2, 4, 5, 8, 10 with f = 1, 2, 3, 1, 1. fx = 2, 8, 15, 8, 10; Σfx = 43, Σf = 8, mean = 5.375. Trap: never divide Σfx by the number of distinct x values.

*Grouped (54 observations):*

| Class | f | Midpoint x | fx | u = (x − 27.5) ÷ 5 | fu |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 5-10 | 3 | 7.5 | 22.5 | −4 | −12 |
| 10-15 | 5 | 12.5 | 62.5 | −3 | −15 |
| 15-20 | 8 | 17.5 | 140 | −2 | −16 |
| 20-25 | 10 | 22.5 | 225 | −1 | −10 |
| 25-30 | 12 | 27.5 | 330 | 0 | 0 |
| 30-35 | 7 | 32.5 | 227.5 | 1 | 7 |
| 35-40 | 6 | 37.5 | 225 | 2 | 12 |
| 40-45 | 2 | 42.5 | 85 | 3 | 6 |
| 45-50 | 1 | 47.5 | 47.5 | 4 | 4 |
| Total | 54 | | 1365 | | −24 |

Direct: mean = 1365 ÷ 54 = 25.28 (2 d.p.). Coding: mean = 27.5 + 5 × (−24 ÷ 54) = 27.5 − 2.22 = 25.28. Same answer. With d = x − A instead, Σfd = 5 × (−24) = −120 and mean = 27.5 + (−120 ÷ 54) = 25.28. If classes are written as limits such as 10-19, the midpoint is (10 + 19) ÷ 2 = 14.5. A quick coding check: A = 35, h = 5, Σfu = 300, n = 15 gives mean = 35 + 5 × 20 = 135.

*Weighted mean:* a Yaba shop sells product P (₦4 contribution per unit, 200 units), Q (₦7, 150 units), R (₦10, 50 units). Σwx = 800 + 1,050 + 500 = 2,350; Σw = 400; weighted mean = ₦5.875 ≈ ₦5.88 per unit. The simple mean (4 + 7 + 10) ÷ 3 = ₦7 is wrong because it ignores how many of each were sold. The same reasoning applies to a credit-weighted grade (weights = credit units) and to an overall pass rate for departments of different sizes: use the **weighted** mean, not the simple mean.

*Combined mean:* a branch of 30 staff earns a mean of ₦80,000 and another of 20 staff earns ₦95,000. Combined = (30 × 80,000 + 20 × 95,000) ÷ 50 = 4,300,000 ÷ 50 = ₦86,000. It lies nearer the larger group's mean.

*Rearranging (reverse questions):*
- n = 30, mean = 15: Σx = 450. Σx = 350, mean = 25: n = 350 ÷ 25 = 14.
- Missing value: five figures have mean 12, so Σx = 60; four are 10, 15, 8, 14 (sum 47), so the fifth = 60 − 47 = 13.
- Correcting an error: mean of 20 values = 45, so Σx = 900. A value 64 was recorded as 46: correct Σx = 900 − 46 + 64 = 918; correct mean = 918 ÷ 20 = 45.9.
- Subgroup mean: 50 students average 60 (total 3,000); 20 boys average 54 (total 1,080); the 30 girls average (3,000 − 1,080) ÷ 30 = 64.
- Transformation: mean wage ₦20 (thousand); multiply each by 3 and add ₦4 thousand: new mean = 3 × 20 + 4 = 64.

*Sums trap:* for 5, 6, 2, 1, 9, 8, 3, Σx = 34, **Σx² = 25 + 36 + 4 + 1 + 81 + 64 + 9 = 220**, but (Σx)² = 34² = 1,156. Do not confuse them. And for 4, 7, 9, 12 (mean 8), deviations are −4, −1, 1, 4, which sum to 0.

### Lesson 3.3: Geometric and harmonic means

**Must know**

- **Geometric mean (GM):** the **nth root of the product** of n positive values. GM = (x1 × x2 × ... × xn)^(1/n). With frequencies: GM = (x1^f1 × x2^f2 × ... × xn^fn)^(1/Σf). Log form: log GM = Σ(f log x) ÷ Σf.
- **Use the GM** to average percentage changes, growth rates, indexes, inflation and compound interest: whenever each value is calculated from a **different base** (multiplicative change).
- **Average growth rate** = (product of growth factors)^(1/n) − 1, where growth factor = 1 + rate.
- **Harmonic mean (HM):** the **reciprocal of the arithmetic mean of the reciprocals**. HM = n ÷ Σ(1/x). With frequencies: HM = Σf ÷ Σ(f/x).
- **Use the HM** to average rates such as speeds over the **same distance**. For two speeds a and b: average speed = 2ab ÷ (a + b).
- **Order:** for positive values not all equal, **AM > GM > HM**. If all values are equal, **AM = GM = HM**.
- **For two positive numbers:** GM² = AM × HM, so GM = √(AM × HM) and HM = GM² ÷ AM.

**Explain more**

*GM ungrouped:* 2, 4, 5, 8, 5, 10, 5, 4. Product = 320,000; GM = 320,000^(1/8) = 4.88 (2 d.p.). Small checks: GM of 4 and 25 = √100 = 10; GM of 1, 4, 54 = (216)^(1/3) = 6.

*GM with frequencies:* x = 2, 6, 18 with f = 2, 1, 1. Product = 2² × 6 × 18 = 432; Σf = 4; GM = 432^(1/4) = 4.56 (2 d.p.). The data in Lesson 3.2 (x = 2, 4, 5, 8, 10; f = 1, 2, 3, 1, 1) give GM = 4.88.

*Growth rates:* the price of a bag of rice in a Lagos market rises 10%, 20% and 30% in three years. Growth factors 1.10, 1.20, 1.30; product = 1.716; GM = 1.716^(1/3) = 1.1972; average annual increase = 19.72% (2 d.p.). The simple average (10 + 20 + 30) ÷ 3 = 20% overstates it. Use the factors, never the bare percentages, and subtract 1 at the end.

*HM ungrouped:* 2, 4, 5, 8, 5, 10, 5, 4. Σ(1/x) = 0.5 + 0.25 + 0.2 + 0.125 + 0.2 + 0.1 + 0.2 + 0.25 = 1.825; HM = 8 ÷ 1.825 = 4.38 (2 d.p.). Check: HM of 3, 6, 12 = 3 ÷ (1/3 + 1/6 + 1/12) = 3 ÷ 0.5833 = 5.14.

*HM with frequencies:* x = 2, 4, 5 with f = 2, 3, 5. Σf/x = 1 + 0.75 + 1 = 2.75; HM = 10 ÷ 2.75 = 3.64 (2 d.p.).

*Average speed:* a bus goes from Lagos to Abeokuta at 30 km/h and returns over the same road at 60 km/h. Average speed = 2 × 30 × 60 ÷ (30 + 60) = 3,600 ÷ 90 = 40 km/h, not 45 km/h.

*The three means together:* for 2 and 8, AM = 5, GM = √16 = 4, HM = 2 × 2 × 8 ÷ 10 = 3.2. AM > GM > HM, and GM² = 16 = 5 × 3.2. So if AM = 25 and HM = 15, GM = √375 = 19.36; if AM = 10 and GM = 8, HM = 64 ÷ 10 = 6.4.

### Lesson 3.4: The median and the mode

**Must know**

- **Median (Me):** the **middle value** of data arranged in order of size; 50% of values lie below it and 50% above. It equals Q2 = D5 = P50.
- **Median, ungrouped:** arrange in ascending order; position = **(n + 1) ÷ 2**. n odd: the value at that position. n even: average the values at positions n ÷ 2 and (n ÷ 2) + 1.
- **Median, with frequencies:** find (n + 1) ÷ 2 with n = Σf, then read the x whose cumulative frequency first reaches that position.
- **Median, grouped:** Median = L + ((N/2 − F) ÷ f) × h. L = lower class **boundary** of the median class; N = Σf; F = **cumulative frequency before** the median class; f = frequency of the median class; h = class width. The median class is where N/2 first falls in the cumulative frequency.
- **Steps (grouped):** (1) compute N/2; (2) form cumulative frequencies; (3) find the median class; (4) **convert class limits to class boundaries**; (5) apply the formula.
- **Median: advantage** not affected by outliers; **drawback** cannot be used for categorical data.
- **Mode (Mo):** the value that occurs **most frequently**. No repeated value: **no mode**. One mode: **unimodal**; two: **bimodal**; three: **trimodal**; more than two: **multimodal**.
- **Mode, grouped:** Mode = L + ((f1 − f0) ÷ (2f1 − f0 − f2)) × h, equivalently Mode = L + (d1 ÷ (d1 + d2)) × h, where L = lower boundary of the modal class (highest frequency), f1 = modal class frequency, f0 = pre-modal frequency, f2 = post-modal frequency, **d1 = f1 − f0**, **d2 = f1 − f2**.
- **Mode: advantages** valid for all data types (categorical and numeric); not influenced by outliers. **Main disadvantage:** representative only when the distribution has a **single peak** (unimodal). It may not exist and may not be unique.
- **Empirical relation (Karl Pearson), for moderately skewed data:** Mode = 3 Median − 2 Mean. Rearranged: Median = (Mode + 2 Mean) ÷ 3.

**Explain more**

*Ungrouped median:* 8, 3, 5, 12, 10 ordered: 3, 5, 8, 10, 12; n = 5, position 3, median = 8. For 2, 4, 5, 8, 5, 10, 5, 4 ordered: 2, 4, 4, 5, 5, 5, 8, 10; n = 8; the 4th and 5th values are 5 and 5, median = 5. For 12 monthly salaries (₦'000) 29, 35, 38, 41, 43, 47, 50, 54, 58, 62, 66, 71, median = (47 + 50) ÷ 2 = 48.5. Always sort first: the trap option is the middle of the unsorted list or the mean.

*Median with frequencies:* ages 3, 5, 7, 9, 10 of 2, 3, 4, 5, 6 children. Cumulative frequencies 2, 5, 9, 14, 20; n = 20; position (20 + 1) ÷ 2 = 10.5. Positions 10 and 11 both fall in cf 14, so median age = **9**.

*Grouped median:* use the 54-observation table of Lesson 3.2. Cumulative frequencies: 3, 8, 16, 26, 38, 45, 51, 53, 54. N/2 = 27, first reached in class 25-30. L = 25, F = 26, f = 12, h = 5. Median = 25 + ((27 − 26) ÷ 12) × 5 = 25 + 0.42 = 25.42 (2 d.p.). If classes are given as limits (10-19, 20-29, ...), the boundaries are 9.5-19.5, 19.5-29.5, ..., so L for 30-39 is 29.5 and h = 10. Note: the grouped formula uses N/2, not (N + 1)/2.

*Ungrouped mode:* 2, 2, 5, 7, 9, 9, 9, 10, 10, 11, 12, 18 has mode 9. 3, 5, 8, 10, 12, 15, 16 has **no mode**. 2, 3, 4, 4, 4, 5, 5, 7, 7, 7, 9 has modes 4 and 7: **bimodal**. With frequencies, the mode is the x with the largest f (for x = 28, 30, 32, 33, 35, 38, 40 with f = 1, 2, 2, 6, 2, 1, 1, the mode is **33**, not 6). The mode answers "which shoe size, brand or category sells most".

*Grouped mode:* same 54-observation table. Modal class 25-30 (f = 12). L = 25, f1 = 12, f0 = 10, f2 = 7, h = 5. Mode = 25 + ((12 − 10) ÷ (24 − 10 − 7)) × 5 = 25 + (2 ÷ 7) × 5 = 26.43 (2 d.p.). With d1 = 2 and d2 = 5: 25 + (2 ÷ 7) × 5, the same. Trap: d1 uses the class **before** the modal class.

*Empirical relation:* mean = 60, median = 55: mode = 3 × 55 − 2 × 60 = 165 − 120 = 45. Mode = 30, mean = 36: median = (30 + 72) ÷ 3 = 34. The relation is only approximate and is for moderately skewed distributions.

- The median is **not** always less than or equal to the mean; that depends on skewness. The mid-range (average of the largest and smallest values) is a different measure and is badly affected by outliers.

### Lesson 3.5: Quartiles, deciles and percentiles

**Must know**

- **Quartiles** divide ordered data into **4** equal parts (Q1, Q2, Q3). **Deciles** into **10** (D1 to D9). **Percentiles** into **100** (P1 to P99). (Quintiles would be 5 parts.) They are **non-central location measures** (fractiles, partition values).
- **Q1 (lower quartile):** separates the bottom 25% from the top 75%. **Q2** = median. **Q3 (upper quartile):** separates the top 25% from the bottom 75%.

| Partition value | Equals | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| Q1 (lower quartile) | P25 | 25% of values below it |
| Q2 = median | D5 = P50 | 50% below, 50% above |
| Q3 (upper quartile) | P75 | 75% of values below it |
| D1, D9 | P10, P90 | 10% and 90% below |

- **Ungrouped, (n + 1) position method:** position of Qk = k(n + 1) ÷ 4; Dk = k(n + 1) ÷ 10; Pk = k(n + 1) ÷ 100. If the position is not whole, **interpolate**: value = lower value + (decimal part) × (next value − lower value).
- **Grouped:** Qk = L + ((kN/4 − F) ÷ f) × h; Dk = L + ((kN/10 − F) ÷ f) × h; Pk = L + ((kN/100 − F) ÷ f) × h. L, F, f, h are as in the median formula, but for the class where kN/4 (or kN/10, kN/100) first falls.

**Explain more**

*Ungrouped example:* 12 ordered values 3, 5, 7, 8, 10, 12, 15, 18, 20, 22, 25, 30.
- Q1: position 1 × 13 ÷ 4 = 3.25, between the 3rd (7) and 4th (8): 7 + 0.25 × (8 − 7) = 7.25.
- Q3: position 3 × 13 ÷ 4 = 9.75, between the 9th (20) and 10th (22): 20 + 0.75 × 2 = 21.5.
- D4: position 4 × 13 ÷ 10 = 5.2, between 10 and 12: 10 + 0.2 × 2 = 10.4.
- P85: position 85 × 13 ÷ 100 = 11.05, between 25 and 30: 25 + 0.05 × 5 = 25.25.
- A whole-number position needs no interpolation: for n = 11, Q1 is the 3rd value and Q3 the 9th. If both neighbouring values are equal, the answer is that value (no change from interpolating).

*With frequencies:* ages 3, 5, 7, 9, 10 with f = 2, 3, 4, 5, 6 (cf 2, 5, 9, 14, 20). Q1 position = 21 ÷ 4 = 5.25. The 5th value is 5 (cf 5) and the 6th is 7, so Q1 = 5 + 0.25 × (7 − 5) = 5.5.

*Grouped examples* (54-observation table, cf 3, 8, 16, 26, 38, 45, 51, 53, 54):
- Q1: N/4 = 13.5, class 15-20; L = 15, F = 8, f = 8. Q1 = 15 + ((13.5 − 8) ÷ 8) × 5 = 18.44.
- Q3: 3N/4 = 40.5, class 30-35; L = 30, F = 38, f = 7. Q3 = 30 + (2.5 ÷ 7) × 5 = 31.79.
- D7: 7N/10 = 37.8, class 25-30 (cf reaches 38 there); L = 25, F = 26, f = 12. D7 = 25 + (11.8 ÷ 12) × 5 = 29.92.
- P20: 20N/100 = 10.8, class 15-20; L = 15, F = 8, f = 8. P20 = 15 + (2.8 ÷ 8) × 5 = 16.75.
- All rounded to 2 d.p. The commonest slip is choosing the wrong class: the class is the first one whose cumulative frequency is **at least** the target.

*Conventions:* the (n + 1) method above is the one to use in this course. Some texts and spreadsheets use other positions (such as n ÷ 4 or (n − 1)p + 1) and get slightly different ungrouped quartiles; when a question says "(n + 1) position method", use (n + 1). For grouped data always use kN/4, kN/10 and kN/100 with no "+1".

### One-minute recap

- Location = where data concentrate; with dispersion and skewness it describes a data profile.
- Mean = Σx ÷ n or Σfx ÷ Σf (midpoints for grouped); coding: A + h × Σfu ÷ Σf; Σ(x − mean) = 0.
- Weighted mean = Σwx ÷ Σw; combined mean = (n1 mean1 + n2 mean2) ÷ (n1 + n2); Σx = n × mean.
- GM = nth root of the product (growth rates, different bases); HM = n ÷ Σ(1/x) (speeds); AM > GM > HM; GM² = AM × HM for two numbers.
- Median: position (n + 1) ÷ 2; grouped L + ((N/2 − F) ÷ f) × h with class boundaries.
- Mode: most frequent; may not exist or be bimodal; grouped L + (d1 ÷ (d1 + d2)) × h; Mode = 3 Median − 2 Mean.
- Q2 = D5 = P50 = median; Q1 = P25; Q3 = P75; D9 = P90.
- Outliers distort the mean, not the median or mode; categorical data: mode only.

### Theory practice

**Question:** For the distribution Class 0-10, 10-20, 20-30, 30-40 with frequencies 5, 10, 15, 10, find the median and the mode.

*Solution:* N = 40, cumulative frequencies 5, 15, 30, 40. N/2 = 20 falls in class 20-30: L = 20, F = 15, f = 15, h = 10. Median = 20 + ((20 − 15) ÷ 15) × 10 = 20 + 3.33 = 23.33 (2 d.p.). Modal class 20-30: f1 = 15, f0 = 10, f2 = 10, so d1 = 5, d2 = 5. Mode = 20 + (5 ÷ 10) × 10 = 25.
