## Topic 1: The IASB Conceptual Framework

**What you must know:** standards and the IASB; the Framework's purpose, status, scope and benefits; the objective of financial reporting and its users; qualitative characteristics, cost constraint and going concern; the five elements, recognition and derecognition; measurement bases; capital maintenance.

### Lesson 1.1: Accounting standards, the IASB and the IFRS Foundation

**Must know**

- **GAAP (generally accepted accounting principles):** the concepts and rules behind financial statements, found partly in law and partly in standards. GAAP **vary from country to country** because **legal and regulatory systems are not the same**. Many countries now use one uniform set: **IFRS** and the older **IAS**.
- **Accounting standards:** common sets of principles and procedures that define the basis of financial accounting policies and practices. They apply to the **whole spectrum** of an entity's transactions and other events: assets, liabilities, revenue, expenses and equity.
- **Legal status:** accounting standards **do not have the force of law**, but they become effective when a **country adopts** them. Nigeria began adopting IFRS in **2012**, starting with companies listed on what is now the **Nigerian Exchange Group**. The **Financial Reporting Council of Nigeria (FRC of Nigeria)** is the national body that oversees and enforces compliance with IFRS in Nigeria.
- **IASB (International Accounting Standards Board, "the Board"):** the independent body of highly skilled accounting experts responsible for **issuing new IFRSs**. It has full responsibility for all technical matters.

**Outputs of the IASB (3):**

1. **Exposure drafts** of standards (proposals published for public comment).
2. **Revised IASs and new IFRSs.**
3. **Final interpretations** of the **IFRS Interpretations Committee** (IFRICs).

**Responsibilities of the IASB:**

1. Issues the **Conceptual Framework**, which sets out the concepts underlying the preparation and presentation of financial statements.
2. Issues **accounting standards** (IFRSs) and Interpretations (IFRICs), used worldwide for consistency and transparency.
3. Works with **national standard setters** such as the FRC of Nigeria, through which **compliance** with IFRS is promoted.

**Explain more**

- **Structure:** the not-for-profit **IFRS Foundation** sits at the top. Its **Trustees** appoint, fund and oversee the boards and answer to a **Monitoring Board** of public capital-market authorities. Under it sit the **IASB** (IFRS Accounting Standards), the **ISSB** (International Sustainability Standards Board, sustainability disclosure standards), the **IFRS Interpretations Committee** (IFRICs) and the **IFRS Advisory Council** (advice).
- **IAS vs IFRS:** the IASB replaced the **International Accounting Standards Committee (IASC)** in **2001**. IASC standards are called **IAS** (e.g. IAS 16); IASB standards are **IFRS** (e.g. IFRS 15). Both remain in force unless withdrawn.
- **Trap:** audited financial statements, tax rates and audits are **not** IASB outputs or duties. Companies prepare accounts, government sets tax, auditors audit.

### Lesson 1.2: What the Conceptual Framework is, its purpose, status and scope

**Must know**

- **Conceptual Framework:** a **coherent system of interrelated objectives and fundamental concepts** that prescribes the nature, function and limits of financial accounting and reporting, and is expected to lead to consistent guidance. The current version is the **Conceptual Framework for Financial Reporting 2018** (published March 2018).

**Purpose (3):** the Framework exists to

1. **assist the IASB** in developing and revising IFRS Accounting Standards (its **primary** purpose: guidance for developing standards);
2. **assist entities (preparers)** in developing consistent accounting policies **when no Standard applies** to a transaction or other event, **or when a Standard allows a choice** of accounting policy;
3. **assist all stakeholders** to understand and interpret the Standards.

- **Status:** the Framework is **not an IFRS Standard**. Nothing in it **overrides** any Standard or requirement in a Standard. Where a Standard conflicts with the Framework, the Standard wins.

**Scope of the 2018 Framework (8 chapters):**

1. The **objective** of general purpose financial reporting.
2. The **qualitative characteristics** of useful financial information.
3. A description of the **reporting entity** and its **boundary**.
4. Definitions of the **elements** (asset, liability, equity, income, expenses).
5. **Recognition** (criteria for including assets and liabilities) and **derecognition** (when to remove them).
6. **Measurement** bases and when to use them.
7. **Presentation and disclosure**.
8. **Capital and capital maintenance**.

**Benefits (4):**

| Benefit | What it gives |
|---|---|
| Consistency and comparability | A common set of principles, so statements are easier to compare across companies and periods |
| Guidance for standard setters | Helps the IASB and others develop consistent, conceptually sound standards |
| Assistance to preparers | Helps companies develop accounting policies where no standard exists or a choice is allowed |
| Enhanced understanding | Helps investors, lenders and analysts understand and interpret financial statements |

**Explain more**

- **History:** April 1989 IASC Board approves the *Framework for the Preparation and Presentation of Financial Statements* (published July 1989); April 2001 adopted by the IASB; September 2010 *Conceptual Framework for Financial Reporting 2010* approved; March 2018 revised *Conceptual Framework* published.
- **Applying it:** Ikeja Agro Ltd meets a new type of transaction that no IFRS covers. It should develop its policy by looking to the **Conceptual Framework** (through the IAS 8 hierarchy you meet in Topic 5), not to whatever gives the highest profit, stock exchange rules or its tax computation.
- **Trap:** assessing how efficiently management has used resources (stewardship) is what **users** do; it is **not** a purpose of the Framework. The Framework does **not** set tax rates, listing rules, audit procedures or directors' pay.
- **Reporting entity:** an entity that is **required, or chooses,** to prepare financial statements. It can be a **single entity**, a **portion** of an entity, or **more than one entity**, and it is **not necessarily a legal entity**. When Delta Holdings Plc and its three subsidiaries prepare one set of consolidated statements, that group of four companies is one reporting entity.

### Lesson 1.3: Objective of general purpose financial reporting and its users

**Must know**

- **Objective:** to provide financial information about the reporting entity that is **useful to existing and potential investors, lenders and other creditors** in making decisions about providing resources to the entity.
- **General purpose financial reports (GPFR):** financial statements meant to meet the information needs **common** to users who **cannot command** reports tailored to their own needs. They comply with accounting standards and GAAP.
- **Special-purpose financial statements:** prepared to meet the demands of **a particular user** or group (e.g. a report in a format a bank designs only for its loan assessment).
- GPFR **cannot provide all** the information users need; users must also consider information from **other sources**.

**Primary users (3):** **present and potential investors**, **lenders**, and **other creditors**. They decide about:

1. **buying, selling or holding** equity or debt instruments;
2. **providing or settling loans** and other forms of credit;
3. **exercising rights to vote** on, or otherwise influence, management's actions that affect the use of the entity's resources.

To decide, they need information to assess:

1. the entity's **prospects for future net cash inflows**; and
2. how efficiently and effectively management has discharged its responsibility to use the entity's resources: **stewardship**.

**Other users** (may find GPFR useful but are **not** primary users; GPFR are not mainly directed at them): **prudential and market regulators** (e.g. the Central Bank of Nigeria), **management**, **analysts and brokerage firms**, and the **government** (deciding how much tax to collect).

**Explain more**

**Users and their information needs** (learn the table: exams swap the rows):

| User | Interested in |
|---|---|
| Owners / investors | Appraisal of the **efficiency of management performance** |
| Management | **Overall performance, growth and survival** |
| Trade creditors (payables) | **Liquidity** |
| Employees | **Liquidity, profitability and survival** (job and wage security) |
| Customers | **Quality** of products or services |
| Government | **Profitability for tax purposes** and policy formulation |
| Financial analysts | Appraisal of the **entire business activities** |
| Lenders | **Liquidity, profitability and reliability of the business assets** |

- A workers' union in a Lagos factory checking whether jobs and wages are safe is an employee group: liquidity, profitability and survival.
- **Trap:** judging product quality before buying is a **customer's** decision, not one of the three primary-user decisions. The government is **not** a primary user, even though it reads the accounts for tax.

### Lesson 1.4: Qualitative characteristics, the cost constraint and going concern

**Must know**

Information is **useful** if it is **relevant** and **faithfully represents** what it purports to represent. Usefulness is **enhanced** if it is comparable, verifiable, timely and understandable.

| Fundamental (2) | Enhancing (4) |
|---|---|
| Relevance (includes **materiality**) | Comparability |
| Faithful representation | Verifiability |
| | Timeliness |
| | Understandability |

- **Relevance:** information is relevant if it can **make a difference to users' decisions**: it has **predictive value**, **confirmatory value**, or both.
- **Materiality:** an **entity-specific aspect of relevance**: information is material if omitting, misstating or obscuring it could reasonably be expected to influence users' decisions. Judged on **nature or magnitude (or both)** in the context of the **individual entity**; there is **no uniform amount** for all entities.
- **Faithful representation:** representing the **substance** of an economic phenomenon instead of its **legal form only** (substance over form). A perfectly faithful depiction is:
  1. **Complete:** all information needed for understanding is included.
  2. **Neutral:** without bias or slanting, supported by **prudence** (caution when judging under uncertainty).
  3. **Free from error:** no errors or omissions in the description or in the process used.
- **Comparability:** lets users identify **similarities and differences**, e.g. trends over time and comparison with similar businesses. **Consistency** (using the **same methods** for the same items **from period to period**, and across entities) helps achieve comparability. Consistency is the means; comparability is the goal.
- **Verifiability:** different **knowledgeable and independent observers** could reach **consensus, although not necessarily complete agreement**, that a depiction is a faithful representation.
- **Timeliness:** available **early enough** to be capable of influencing decisions.
- **Understandability:** classified, characterised and presented clearly and concisely. It assumes users **have a reasonable knowledge of business and economic activities** and **review and analyse the information diligently**.
- **Cost constraint:** reporting has a cost, so the **benefits** of information must **justify the costs** of providing and using it. Cost is a pervasive constraint, not a qualitative characteristic.
- **Going concern:** the **underlying assumption** that the entity will **continue operating for the foreseeable future**, with no intention or need to **liquidate or curtail** its operations materially. If that is not true, statements are prepared on a different basis.

**Explain more**

- **Going concern and relevance (classic question):** Eko Foods Plc is a going concern with no plan to curtail operations. Is **depreciated cost** or **net realisable value (NRV)** more relevant for its machinery? **Depreciated cost**, because the business will keep using the machine, not sell it. If Eko Foods were **not** a going concern, **NRV** (what the machine would fetch on sale) would be more relevant. Scrap value and undepreciated cost are wrong answers.
- **Materiality is entity-specific:** a ₦2 million omission could seriously mislead users of a small Yaba printing business yet be trivial in the accounts of a large commercial bank.
- **Spot the missing characteristic:** Oshodi Foods Ltd's MD deliberately slants estimates to look good: **neutrality** is missing. Aba Plastics Ltd leaves out a large bank loan: **completeness** is missing. An investor comparing Ogun Cement Plc's five-year sales trend with two rival cement firms relies on **comparability**.
- **Traps:** materiality and relevance are **not** enhancing characteristics; timeliness is **not** part of faithful representation; "neutrality and prudence" is **not** the fundamental pair. If asked which characteristic means "no change in the basis of preparation from period to period", answer **consistency**.
- Enhancing characteristics improve useful information but **cannot make irrelevant or unfaithful information useful**.
- Faithful representation replaced the older term "reliability" in 2010; the 2018 version brought back **prudence** and made **stewardship** explicit.

### Lesson 1.5: The elements, recognition and derecognition

**Must know**

**Elements of financial statements:** the broad classes into which the financial effects of transactions and other events are grouped according to their economic characteristics.

| Financial position (statement of financial position) | Performance (statement of profit or loss) |
|---|---|
| Assets, Liabilities, Equity | Income, Expenses |

The **statement of cash flows** reflects **both** income-statement elements and **some changes** in statement-of-financial-position elements.

**Definitions** (the wording most exam options use; the 2018 wording follows in brackets):

- **Asset:** a **resource controlled by the entity as a result of past events** and from which **future economic benefits are expected** to flow to the entity. (2018: a **present economic resource** controlled by the entity as a result of past events; an economic resource is a **right that has the potential to produce economic benefits**.)
- **Liability:** a **present obligation** of the entity **arising from past events**, the settlement of which is expected to result in an **outflow** of resources embodying economic benefits. (2018: a present obligation of the entity to **transfer an economic resource** as a result of past events.)
- **Equity:** the **residual interest in the assets** of the entity **after deducting all its liabilities**.
- **Income:** **increases in economic benefits** during the period, as **inflows or enhancements of assets or decreases of liabilities**, that **increase equity**, **other than contributions from equity participants**. (2018 says "holders of equity claims"; the idea is the same.)
- **Expenses:** **decreases in economic benefits** during the period, as **outflows or depletions of assets or incurrences of liabilities**, that **decrease equity**, **other than distributions to equity participants**.

**Formulas (the accounting equation):**

- Assets = Equity + Liabilities
- Equity = Assets − Liabilities
- Liabilities = Assets − Equity
- Closing equity = Opening equity + Profit + New capital contributed − Dividends (distributions)
- Profit = Income − Expenses = Closing equity − Opening equity − New capital + Dividends

**Recognition:** capturing an item that meets the definition of an element for inclusion in the statement of financial position or performance. Under the 2018 Framework an asset or liability is recognised **only if** it meets the definition **and** recognition gives users **relevant** information and a **faithful representation**, with benefits justifying costs. (The older Framework used two tests: it is **probable** that future economic benefits will flow, and the item has a cost or value that can be **measured reliably**.)

**Derecognition:** removing all or part of a recognised asset or liability. An asset is derecognised when the entity **loses control** of it; a liability when the entity **no longer has a present obligation**.

**Explain more**

- **Control:** the **ability to obtain the economic benefits and to restrict the access of others** (e.g. being the sole user of a plant, or selling surplus plant). It is **not** physical possession or legal title.
- **Past event:** Lekki Logistics Ltd has only **discussed** a lease it will sign next March, so at 31 December there is **no liability**: the past event has not happened.
- **Constructive obligation:** arises from **expected practice or published policy**, not law or contract. Niger Delta Oil Ltd publishes a policy of cleaning up damage it causes, though no law requires it; its duty to clean up damage already caused is constructive.
- **Income = revenue + gains.** **Revenue** arises from ordinary activities: **sales, fees, interest, dividends, royalties, rent** (capital contributed is **not** revenue). **Gains** are **no different in nature from revenue**, so they are **not a separate element**.
- **Expenses include losses** and ordinary costs such as **cost of sales, wages, depreciation**. **Losses** are **no different in nature from other expenses**, so they are **not a separate element**.
- **Owner transactions:** Ibadan Bakeries Ltd's shareholders pay ₦5 million for new shares: equity rises but it is **not income** (contribution from equity participants). A ₦1.5 million dividend is **not an expense** (distribution to equity participants). Buying a van or repaying loan principal is not an expense either.

**Worked examples (₦):**

1. *Equity.* Assets ₦250m, liabilities ₦90m: equity = 250 − 90 = **₦160m**. Buying equipment for ₦30m on credit gives 280 − 120 = **₦160m**: unchanged.
2. *Assets.* Equity ₦40m, liabilities ₦25m: assets = 40 + 25 = **₦65m**. *Liabilities.* Assets ₦120m, equity ₦70m: liabilities = 120 − 70 = **₦50m**.
3. *Loan repayment.* Assets ₦60m, liabilities ₦25m; ₦4m of loan repaid in cash. Liabilities = 25 − 4 = **₦21m**; assets 56m; equity 56 − 21 = **₦35m** (unchanged).
4. *Profit from equity.* Opening equity ₦60m, closing ₦84m, new shares ₦15m, dividends ₦6m. Profit = 84 − 60 − 15 + 6 = **₦15m**. Check: 60 + 15 + 15 − 6 = 84.
5. *Classifying.* Enugu Prints Ltd's first month: capital received ₦8m, cash revenue ₦3m, wages ₦1.2m, dividend ₦0.5m. Income = **₦3m**; expenses = **₦1.2m**; profit = ₦1.8m. Closing equity (from zero) = 8 + 1.8 − 0.5 = **₦9.3m**.

### Lesson 1.6: Measurement bases, capital and capital maintenance

**Must know**

**Measurement:** quantifying, in money, the elements recognised. The 2018 Framework has two groups of bases:

1. **Historical cost:** based on the price of the transaction that created the item: for an asset, the consideration paid plus transaction costs, later reduced by depreciation and impairment.
2. **Current value** (updated to conditions at the measurement date):
   - **Fair value:** the price that would be received to **sell an asset**, or paid to **transfer a liability**, in an orderly transaction between market participants at the measurement date.
   - **Value in use** (assets) / **fulfilment value** (liabilities): the **present value of the cash flows** the entity expects from using and finally disposing of an asset, or expects to transfer to fulfil a liability. These are entity-specific.
   - **Current cost:** the cost of an **equivalent asset** at the measurement date (consideration that would be paid plus transaction costs), or the consideration that would be received for an equivalent liability minus transaction costs.

**Concepts of capital (2):**

1. **Financial capital:** capital is the **net assets or equity** of the entity, measured either in **nominal money** units or in units of **constant purchasing power**. Most entities use this.
2. **Physical capital:** capital is the entity's **productive capacity** or **operating capability** (e.g. units of output per day).

**Capital maintenance:** profit is earned only if capital at the end of the period exceeds capital at the start, **after excluding contributions from and distributions to owners**. Anything more than what is needed to maintain opening capital is profit.

- Profit (money financial capital) = Closing net assets − Opening net assets
- Profit (constant purchasing power) = Closing net assets − Opening net assets × (1 + general inflation rate)
- Profit (physical capital) = Closing net assets − Cost of the opening operating capability at closing prices
- (In each case first remove owner contributions and add back distributions.)

**Explain more**

- **Measurement example.** Eko Foods Plc's machine cost ₦20m; accumulated depreciation is ₦8m. Historical cost carrying amount = 20 − 8 = **₦12m**. It would sell today for ₦10m (**fair value**). The present value of cash from using and then disposing of it is ₦13m (**value in use**). A similar used machine would cost ₦14m today (**current cost**). One asset, four measures.
- **Trap:** "market value only", "replacement cost only" and "tax value" are **not** the Framework's list of bases; **historical cost** is.
- **Capital maintenance example (all figures ₦).** Adaeze starts a trading business with ₦10,000,000, buys 2,000 bags of rice at ₦5,000 each and sells them all by year end for ₦13,000,000 cash. General inflation is 10%. Replacing 2,000 bags at year end costs ₦6,000 each.

| Concept | Capital to maintain | Profit |
|---|---|---|
| Money financial capital | ₦10,000,000 | 13,000,000 − 10,000,000 = **₦3,000,000** |
| Constant purchasing power | 10,000,000 × 1.10 = ₦11,000,000 | 13,000,000 − 11,000,000 = **₦2,000,000** |
| Physical capital | 2,000 × 6,000 = ₦12,000,000 | 13,000,000 − 12,000,000 = **₦1,000,000** |

- Under the last two concepts, the part of the ₦3,000,000 gain needed to maintain capital (₦1,000,000 or ₦2,000,000) is a **capital maintenance adjustment**, taken to equity, not profit. Physical capital maintenance needs the **current cost** basis.

### One-minute recap

- Standards bind only once a country adopts them (Nigeria: 2012). The IASB issues IFRSs, exposure drafts and IFRICs.
- The Framework is not a Standard and never overrides one; it helps the IASB, preparers and all stakeholders.
- Primary users: present and potential investors, lenders and other creditors (future net cash inflows, stewardship).
- Fundamental: relevance (incl. materiality) and faithful representation (complete, neutral, free from error). Enhancing: comparability, verifiability, timeliness, understandability.
- Cost constraint; going concern is the underlying assumption.
- Elements: asset, liability, equity; income, expenses. Owner contributions and dividends are neither income nor expenses.
- Assets = Equity + Liabilities; Profit = Closing equity − Opening equity − New capital + Dividends.
- Measurement: historical cost; current value (fair value, value in use / fulfilment value, current cost).
- Capital: financial (money or purchasing power) or physical; profit only after capital is maintained.

### Theory practice

**Question:** Kano Textiles Ltd had net assets of ₦20,000,000 at the start of the year and ₦26,000,000 at the end. There were no capital contributions or dividends. General inflation was 12%, and restoring the company's opening operating capability at year-end prices would cost ₦25,000,000. Compute profit under (a) money financial capital, (b) constant purchasing power financial capital and (c) physical capital maintenance.

*Solution:*

- (a) Capital to maintain = ₦20,000,000. Profit = 26,000,000 − 20,000,000 = **₦6,000,000**.
- (b) Capital to maintain = 20,000,000 × 1.12 = ₦22,400,000. Profit = 26,000,000 − 22,400,000 = **₦3,600,000**.
- (c) Capital to maintain = ₦25,000,000. Profit = 26,000,000 − 25,000,000 = **₦1,000,000**.

The higher the capital that must be maintained, the lower the profit.
