## Topic 4: IAS 2 Inventories

**What you must know:** the definition and scope; lower of cost and NRV; what goes into cost and what is kept out; FIFO, weighted average and specific identification (LIFO banned); NRV write-downs and reversals; expense recognition; disclosures; the computations.

### Lesson 4.1: What inventories are, and the scope of IAS 2

**Must know**

- **IAS 2** deals with **inventories** (their cost and measurement); borrowing costs are IAS 23.
- **Inventories:** assets (a) **held for sale in the ordinary course of business**; (b) **in the process of production for such sale**; or (c) **in the form of materials or supplies to be consumed in the production process or in the rendering of services**.

These are the **3 categories** in the definition:

1. Held for sale in the ordinary course of business (finished goods, goods bought for resale).
2. In the process of production for such sale (work in progress).
3. Materials or supplies to be consumed in production or in rendering services (raw materials, spare parts and consumables used in production).

- Goods held for resale include a retailer's merchandise and **land and other property held for resale**.
- **Excluded from the scope of IAS 2 (2 items):**
  1. **Financial instruments** (IAS 32 and IFRS 9).
  2. **Biological assets related to agricultural activity and agricultural produce at the point of harvest** (IAS 41).
- IAS 2's **measurement** rules do not apply to producers of agricultural and forest products, agricultural produce after harvest and minerals measured at NRV under well-established industry practice, or to **commodity broker-traders** measuring at fair value less costs to sell.

**Explain more**

- Classification depends on **purpose**, not on what the item is. Ikeja Motors Ltd, a car dealer, holds 40 new cars in its showroom for sale (inventories, IAS 2) and 3 cars used by its sales staff (property, plant and equipment, IAS 16).
- A building is inventory only when it is **held for sale in the ordinary course of business**, for example by a property developer such as Lekki Homes Plc. A head office is PPE (IAS 16); a building held to earn rental income is investment property (IAS 40). Cost or size never decides it.
- Trap: "assets held for use in the supply of goods over more than one period" describes PPE, **not** one of the 3 inventory categories.
- Trap: work in progress, raw materials, a retailer's goods and production spares are all **inside** IAS 2.

### Lesson 4.2: Measurement and the cost of inventories

**Must know**

- **Basic rule:** inventories shall be measured at the **lower of cost and net realisable value (NRV)**. Never the higher; never fair value less costs to sell (except broker-traders, above); no depreciation.
- **Cost of inventories** = Costs of purchase + Costs of conversion + Other costs incurred in bringing the inventories to their **present location and condition**.
- **Costs of purchase** = Purchase price + Import duties and other taxes (other than those **subsequently recoverable** from the tax authorities) + Transport + Handling + Other costs directly attributable to acquiring the goods − Trade discounts, rebates and similar items.
- **Costs of conversion** = Direct labour (and other costs directly related to units produced) + Systematic allocation of **fixed** and **variable** production overheads.
  - **Fixed production overheads** (factory depreciation, maintenance, factory management) are allocated on the basis of **normal capacity**. Unallocated fixed overhead (from low production or idle plant) is an **expense** in the period.
  - Rate = Fixed production overheads ÷ Normal capacity (units). If output is below normal: Amount expensed = Fixed overheads − (Actual units × Rate). If output is **abnormally high**: Rate = Fixed overheads ÷ Actual units, so inventory is not measured above cost.
  - **Variable production overheads** (indirect materials, indirect labour) are allocated on the **actual use** of the production facilities.
- **Other costs** are included only to the extent they bring inventories to their present location and condition, for example non-production overheads or the cost of designing products for specific customers.

**Costs excluded from the cost of inventories (expense them when incurred), 4 items:**

1. **Abnormal amounts of wasted materials, labour or other production costs.**
2. **Storage costs**, unless those costs are **necessary in the production process before a further production stage**.
3. **Administrative overheads** that do not contribute to bringing inventories to their present location and condition.
4. **Selling costs.**

- **Borrowing costs** are included only in the limited circumstances set by IAS 23 (Topic 8). Where goods are bought on **deferred settlement terms**, the extra paid above normal credit price is **interest expense** over the financing period, not inventory cost.
- **Joint products:** shared costs are allocated on a rational, consistent basis (e.g. relative sales value); an immaterial **by-product** is measured at NRV and deducted from the main product's cost.

**Explain more**

- **Worked example (purchase cost).** Kano Agro Traders Ltd imports goods: list price ₦2,400,000; trade discount 10% (₦240,000); non-recoverable import duty ₦180,000; recoverable VAT ₦162,000; carriage inwards ₦60,000; insurance in transit ₦20,000; advertising ₦50,000.
  - Net purchase price: ₦2,400,000 − ₦240,000 = ₦2,160,000
  - Cost = ₦2,160,000 + duty ₦180,000 + carriage ₦60,000 + insurance ₦20,000 = **₦2,420,000**
  - Excluded: recoverable VAT (reclaimed, so not a cost) and advertising (a selling cost).
- **Worked example (conversion cost).** Aba Leather Works Ltd makes a batch of bags: materials ₦1,500,000 (including ₦100,000 abnormal waste); direct labour ₦600,000; variable production overheads ₦250,000; fixed production overheads allocated at normal capacity ₦400,000; general administration ₦200,000; selling costs ₦150,000; storage of finished bags awaiting sale ₦80,000.
  - Cost = (₦1,500,000 − ₦100,000) + ₦600,000 + ₦250,000 + ₦400,000 = **₦2,650,000**
  - Abnormal waste, administration, selling and finished-goods storage are expensed.
- **Worked example (normal capacity).** Ogun Paints Ltd has fixed production overheads of ₦3,000,000. Normal capacity is 50,000 units.
  - Rate = ₦3,000,000 ÷ 50,000 = ₦60 per unit.
  - Low year, 40,000 units made: absorbed into inventory 40,000 × ₦60 = ₦2,400,000; expensed ₦3,000,000 − ₦2,400,000 = **₦600,000**.
  - Abnormally high year, 60,000 units made: rate falls to ₦3,000,000 ÷ 60,000 = ₦50 per unit, so no more than the ₦3,000,000 actually incurred is put into inventory.
- Trap: direct labour and fixed overheads on normal capacity are **included**. Storage between two production stages (e.g. maturing a product before bottling) is **included**; storage of finished goods awaiting sale is **not**.

### Lesson 4.3: Cost formulas: specific identification, FIFO and weighted average

**Must know**

- **Specific identification:** specific costs attributed to identified items. It **must** be used for items **not ordinarily interchangeable** and goods or services **produced and segregated for specific projects** (e.g. custom jewellery made for a named customer).
- For all other items, use **FIFO** or **weighted average cost**:
  - **FIFO (first-in, first-out):** items bought or made first are sold first, so **closing inventory = the most recent purchases**.
  - **Weighted average cost:** cost of each item = weighted average of the cost of similar items at the start of the period and those bought or made during it. Calculated on a **periodic** basis or **as each additional shipment is received** (moving average).
  - Periodic weighted average cost per unit = (Cost of opening inventory + Cost of purchases) ÷ (Opening units + Units purchased).
- **LIFO (last-in, first-out) is NOT allowed** under IAS 2.
- **Consistency:** use the **same cost formula for all inventories having a similar nature and use** to the entity. A different nature or use may justify a different formula; a different geographical location alone does **not**.
- **Standard cost** and the **retail method** may be used for convenience if the results **approximate cost**. Retail method: Cost = Sales value of inventory − Gross margin at an appropriate percentage; it suits retailers with many fast-moving items with similar margins.
- Units in closing inventory = Opening units + Units purchased − Units sold (or issued).
- Cost of sales = Opening inventory + Purchases − Closing inventory.

**Explain more**

- **Worked example (FIFO and weighted average).** Ilorin Rice Mills Ltd: opening inventory 500 bags at ₦30,000; purchases 1,000 bags at ₦33,000, then 500 bags at ₦36,000. It sold 1,600 bags at ₦45,000 each.
  - Total available: 500 + 1,000 + 500 = 2,000 bags; cost ₦15,000,000 + ₦33,000,000 + ₦18,000,000 = ₦66,000,000.
  - Closing units: 2,000 − 1,600 = 400 bags.
  - **FIFO:** the 400 left are from the last purchase: 400 × ₦36,000 = **₦14,400,000**. Cost of sales = ₦66,000,000 − ₦14,400,000 = **₦51,600,000**.
  - **Weighted average (periodic):** ₦66,000,000 ÷ 2,000 = ₦33,000 per bag. Closing = 400 × ₦33,000 = **₦13,200,000**. Cost of sales = ₦66,000,000 − ₦13,200,000 = **₦52,800,000**.
  - Revenue 1,600 × ₦45,000 = ₦72,000,000. Gross profit: FIFO ₦20,400,000; weighted average ₦19,200,000. With rising prices, FIFO gives the higher closing inventory and profit.
- **Issues spread through the year.** FIFO gives the same closing inventory whether you track each issue or work at the year end: find closing units, then price them from the latest purchases backwards. If 1,000 units remain and the last purchase was 1,200 units at ₦3.50, closing cost = 1,000 × ₦3.50 = ₦3,500.
- **Moving average** (recalculated on each receipt) can differ from periodic. Opening 100 units at ₦200 (₦20,000); buy 100 at ₦260 (₦26,000); new average ₦46,000 ÷ 200 = ₦230. Sell 150; 50 remain at ₦230 = ₦11,500. Buy 150 at ₦300 (₦45,000); new average (₦11,500 + ₦45,000) ÷ 200 = ₦282.50. Closing 200 units = **₦56,500**. On a periodic basis: (₦20,000 + ₦26,000 + ₦45,000) ÷ 350 = ₦260; closing 200 × ₦260 = **₦52,000**.
- **Retail method.** Allen Avenue Mart Ltd counts closing inventory at selling price of ₦5,000,000; its gross margin is 20% of selling price. Cost = ₦5,000,000 × (100% − 20%) = **₦4,000,000**.
- Trap: a retailer may **not** use LIFO and need **not** use specific identification for every item; the retail method is fine if it approximates cost. No switching formula each year.

### Lesson 4.4: Net realisable value, write-downs and reversals

**Must know**

- **Net realisable value (NRV):** the estimated selling price in the ordinary course of business **less the estimated costs of completion** and **the estimated costs necessary to make the sale**.
- NRV = Estimated selling price − Estimated costs of completion − Estimated costs to sell.
- Write-down = Cost − NRV (only when NRV is below cost). Carrying amount = lower of cost and NRV.
- **Why cost may not be recoverable:** inventories are damaged; wholly or partly obsolete; selling prices have declined; or estimated costs of completion or of making the sale have increased.
- Write-downs are normally made **item by item**, or by **grouping similar or related items** where appropriate (same product line, similar end uses, produced and marketed in the same area, not practicable to evaluate separately). **Not** on a whole classification (e.g. all finished goods) or on total inventories.
- **Materials rule:** materials and supplies held for use in production are **not written down below cost** if the finished products they go into are expected to sell **at or above cost**. If a fall in the price of materials shows that the finished products' cost will exceed NRV, the materials are written down, and **replacement cost** may be the best available measure of their NRV.
- **Firm sales contracts:** NRV of inventory held to satisfy firm contracts is based on the **contract price**; any quantity above the contract is based on general selling prices.
- **Reversal:** NRV is reassessed each period. If the causes of a write-down no longer exist or NRV has clearly risen, the write-down is **reversed**, limited to the **original write-down**, so the new carrying amount is the **lower of cost and the revised NRV**. Never above cost.
- NRV is **entity-specific**; fair value is a market price.

**Explain more**

- **Simple case.** Cost ₦10,000, NRV ₦8,000: written-down value is **₦8,000** (write-down ₦2,000).
- **Worked example (completion costs).** Abeokuta Adire Ltd holds fabric costing ₦800,000. Expected selling price ₦900,000 after finishing costing ₦120,000; selling costs ₦60,000.
  - NRV = ₦900,000 − ₦120,000 − ₦60,000 = ₦720,000
  - Write-down = ₦800,000 − ₦720,000 = **₦80,000**; carrying amount ₦720,000.
  - Trap: comparing cost with selling price alone (₦900,000) wrongly gives no write-down.
- **Worked example (per unit).** 200 phone cases cost ₦3,000 each; selling price ₦3,200; packaging and delivery ₦400 per unit. NRV per unit = ₦3,200 − ₦400 = ₦2,800, below cost. Carrying amount = 200 × ₦2,800 = **₦560,000**.
- **Worked example (item by item).** Gbagada Stores Ltd:

| Line | Cost (₦) | NRV (₦) | Lower (₦) |
|---|---|---|---|
| A | 400,000 | 520,000 | 400,000 |
| B | 300,000 | 240,000 | 240,000 |
| C | 150,000 | 180,000 | 150,000 |
| Total | 850,000 | 940,000 | **790,000** |

Item by item gives ₦790,000 (write-down ₦60,000 on line B). Comparing totals (₦850,000 against ₦940,000) would wrongly hide B's loss.
- **Firm contract.** 1,000 cartons cost ₦4,500 each; 600 are under contract at ₦5,000; the general price is ₦4,200 (ignore selling costs). Carrying amount = 600 × ₦4,500 + 400 × ₦4,200 = **₦4,380,000**.
- **Materials rule in practice.** Agege Bakery Ltd's flour has fallen below cost on the market, but the bread made from it will still sell above total cost: the flour is **not written down**.
- **Worked example (reversal).** An item cost ₦500,000 and was written down to NRV of ₦420,000 last year. It is still held.
  - NRV now ₦540,000: reverse ₦80,000 only (back to cost ₦500,000), not ₦120,000.
  - NRV now ₦460,000: reverse ₦460,000 − ₦420,000 = ₦40,000; carrying amount ₦460,000.
- **Journal entries.** Write-down: Dr Cost of sales / Cr Inventory. Reversal: Dr Inventory / Cr Cost of sales.

### Lesson 4.5: Recognition as an expense, disclosures and the full computation

**Must know**

- When inventories are **sold**, their carrying amount is recognised as an **expense in the period in which the related revenue is recognised** (matching), not when bought, made or paid for.
- **Write-downs to NRV and all losses of inventories** (theft, fire, spoilage) are recognised as an **expense in the period the write-down or loss occurs**.
- **Reversals** of write-downs are recognised as a **reduction in the amount of inventories recognised as an expense** in the period the reversal occurs.
- Inventory used as a component of self-constructed PPE is expensed over that asset's life.

**Disclosures required by IAS 2:**

1. The accounting policies adopted, including the **cost formula** used.
2. The **total carrying amount** of inventories and the carrying amount in classifications suitable to the entity (e.g. merchandise, production supplies, materials, work in progress, finished goods).
3. The carrying amount of inventories carried at fair value less costs to sell.
4. The amount of inventories **recognised as an expense** during the period.
5. The amount of any **write-down** recognised as an expense.
6. The amount of any **reversal** of a write-down recognised as a reduction in expense.
7. The **circumstances or events** that led to the reversal.
8. The carrying amount of inventories **pledged as security** for liabilities.

**Explain more**

- **Full computation (cost formula, NRV and profit).** Take Ilorin Rice Mills Ltd from Lesson 4.3 (FIFO). The 400 bags left have an estimated selling price of ₦40,000 each and costs to sell of ₦5,000 each. Operating expenses are ₦6,000,000.
  1. FIFO cost of closing inventory = ₦14,400,000.
  2. NRV = 400 × (₦40,000 − ₦5,000) = ₦14,000,000.
  3. Closing inventory = lower = **₦14,000,000**; write-down ₦400,000.
  4. Cost of sales = Opening ₦15,000,000 + Purchases ₦51,000,000 − Closing ₦14,000,000 = ₦52,000,000 (that is ₦51,600,000 FIFO cost of bags sold + ₦400,000 write-down).
  5. Gross profit = ₦72,000,000 − ₦52,000,000 = ₦20,000,000.
  6. Profit before tax = ₦20,000,000 − ₦6,000,000 = **₦14,000,000**.
- If NRV had been **above** FIFO cost, closing inventory would stay at cost and there would be no write-down: always compare first, then put the lower figure into cost of sales.
- Trap: write-downs and theft losses are **both** expensed at once; they are never deferred, spread forward, taken to OCI or adjusted against opening retained earnings.

### One-minute recap

- Inventories: held for sale; in production for sale; materials or supplies for production or services.
- Outside IAS 2: financial instruments; biological assets and agricultural produce at harvest.
- Measure at the **lower of cost and NRV**, item by item.
- Cost = purchase + conversion + other costs to bring to present location and condition; deduct trade discounts; exclude recoverable taxes.
- Excluded: abnormal waste, most storage, non-contributing admin overheads, selling costs. Fixed overheads on normal capacity.
- Specific identification for non-interchangeable or project items; otherwise FIFO or weighted average; **LIFO banned**; same formula for similar inventories.
- NRV = selling price − costs of completion − costs to sell. Materials not written down if the finished product sells at or above cost.
- Reversal capped at the original write-down; never above cost.
- Expense on sale when revenue is recognised; write-downs and losses expensed at once.

### Theory practice

**Question:** Mushin Fabrics Ltd holds a batch of cloth costing ₦600,000. It is expected to sell for ₦650,000 after further dyeing costing ₦70,000, and selling costs of ₦30,000 will be incurred. At what amount should the cloth be measured, and what entry is needed?

*Solution:*

- NRV = ₦650,000 − ₦70,000 − ₦30,000 = ₦550,000.
- Lower of cost (₦600,000) and NRV (₦550,000) = **₦550,000**.
- Write-down = ₦600,000 − ₦550,000 = ₦50,000, expensed this period: Dr Cost of sales ₦50,000 / Cr Inventory ₦50,000.
- The ₦50,000 write-down is disclosed in the financial statements.
