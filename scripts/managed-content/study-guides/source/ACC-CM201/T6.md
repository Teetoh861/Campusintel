## Topic 6: IAS 16 Property, Plant and Equipment

**What you must know:** what PPE is and when it is recognised; what goes into (and stays out of) its cost; subsequent costs; the cost and revaluation models; depreciation, its causes and methods (with formulas); reviews of useful life and residual value; disposal gains and losses; disclosures.

### Lesson 6.1: Objective, definitions and recognition

**Must know**

- **Objective:** to prescribe the accounting treatment for PPE so that users can **discern information about an entity's investment in its PPE and the changes in that investment**. The principal issues are recognition of the assets, determining their carrying amounts, and the depreciation charges and impairment losses to recognise.
- **Property, plant and equipment:** **tangible** items that (a) are held for use in the production or supply of goods or services, for rental to others, or for administrative purposes; and (b) are expected to be used during **more than one period**.
- **Cost:** the amount of cash or cash equivalents paid, or the fair value of the other consideration given, to acquire an asset at the time of its acquisition or construction.
- **Depreciable amount:** the cost of an asset, or other amount substituted for cost, less its residual value.
- **Depreciation:** the systematic allocation of the depreciable amount of an asset over its useful life.
- **Accumulated depreciation:** total depreciation charged from the start to a given date.
- **Carrying amount:** the amount at which an asset is recognised after deducting any accumulated depreciation and accumulated impairment losses.
- **Fair value:** the price that would be received to sell an asset or paid to transfer a liability in an orderly transaction between market participants at the measurement date (IFRS 13).
- **Impairment loss:** the amount by which the carrying amount of an asset exceeds its recoverable amount.
- **Recoverable amount:** the **higher** of an asset's fair value less costs of disposal and its value in use.
- **Residual (scrap) value:** the estimated amount an entity would currently obtain from disposal of the asset, after deducting estimated costs of disposal, if the asset were already of the age and in the condition expected at the end of its useful life.
- **Useful life:** (a) the period over which an asset is expected to be available for use; or (b) the number of production or similar units expected to be obtained from it.

**Recognition:** the cost of an item of PPE is recognised as an asset if, and only if:

1. it is **probable** that future economic benefits associated with the item will flow to the entity; and
2. the cost of the item can be **measured reliably**.

**Explain more**

- Classification depends on **use**, not on what the item is. Ikeja Motors Ltd sells cars: its showroom building is PPE, but the cars on display for sale are **inventories** (IAS 2). Cash and receivables are financial assets, never PPE.
- Spare parts and stand-by equipment are PPE if used for more than one period; otherwise inventory.
- Trap: recoverable amount is the **higher** of the two figures, not the lower. An impairment loss arises when carrying amount is **above** recoverable amount, never the other way round. Impairment itself is measured under IAS 36.
- Trap: recognition needs no full payment, insurance or minimum value.

### Lesson 6.2: Initial measurement: elements of cost

**Must know**

PPE is measured at **cost** on initial recognition. Cost comprises:

1. **Purchase price**, including import duties and **non-refundable** purchase taxes, after deducting trade discounts and rebates.
2. **Directly attributable costs** of bringing the asset to the location and condition necessary for it to operate as management intends. These are costs that **would have been avoided if the asset had not been bought or built**:
    1. employee benefits arising directly from the construction or acquisition;
    2. site preparation;
    3. initial delivery and handling;
    4. installation and assembly;
    5. testing whether the asset is functioning properly;
    6. professional fees (e.g. legal fees, architects, engineers).
3. The **initial estimate of the costs of dismantling and removing** the item and restoring the site, **discounted to present value**.

**Excluded from cost (expense them):**

1. repairs, renewals, repainting and day-to-day servicing;
2. administration and general overheads;
3. staff training costs;
4. wastage and other abnormal costs (e.g. materials wasted in an accident);
5. costs of opening a new facility, introducing a new product (including advertising) or doing business in a new location or with a new class of customer;
6. costs incurred after the asset is ready for use (including while it is idle or running below capacity), initial operating losses, and relocation or reorganisation costs;
7. recoverable taxes (e.g. recoverable VAT).

**Capitalisation stops when the asset is ready for use** (capable of operating as intended), even if it is not brought into use until later.

**Explain more**

**Worked example 1 (motor vehicle and premises).** Motor car ₦4,500,000; registration number ₦150,000; road licence fees ₦25,000; delivery of the car ₦35,000; testing the car ₦100,000; purchase of office premises ₦8,000,000; legal fees on the office ₦800,000; driver's wages for the first year ₦1,200,000.

| Motor vehicle | ₦ |
|---|---|
| Motor car | 4,500,000 |
| Registration number (one-off, needed to put the car on the road) | 150,000 |
| Delivery charge | 35,000 |
| Testing | 100,000 |
| **Cost capitalised** | **4,785,000** |

Road licence (₦25,000, a recurring running cost) and driver's wages (₦1,200,000) are expensed. Office premises = 8,000,000 + legal fees 800,000 = **₦8,800,000** (legal fees are professional fees).

**Worked example 2 (imported machine).** Ilupeju Packaging Plc buys a machine: list price ₦8,000,000; trade discount ₦400,000; import duty ₦600,000; recoverable VAT ₦600,000; delivery ₦150,000; installation ₦250,000; testing ₦100,000; staff training ₦200,000; allocated administration overheads ₦120,000. It must remove the machine and restore the site in 8 years at an estimated ₦1,200,000, with a present value of ₦500,000.

Cost = 8,000,000 − 400,000 + 600,000 + 150,000 + 250,000 + 100,000 + 500,000 = **₦9,200,000**. Excluded: recoverable VAT, training, overheads, and the undiscounted ₦1,200,000 (only the present value goes in). Journal: Dr Machine 9,200,000; Cr Bank/Payables 8,700,000; Cr Provision for dismantling 500,000.

**Worked example 3 (self-constructed asset).** Ogun Cement Plc builds its own warehouse: materials used ₦6,000,000; wages of staff working directly on it ₦2,000,000; materials wasted in an abnormal accident ₦400,000; site preparation ₦700,000; architect's fees ₦300,000; general company overheads ₦500,000. Cost = 6,000,000 + 2,000,000 + 700,000 + 300,000 = **₦9,000,000**. Abnormal waste and general overheads are expensed.

- **Ready for use trap:** Lekki Bottling Ltd's line is capable of operating on 1 March, first used on 1 June, and runs below capacity for two months. Capitalisation stops on **1 March**; later costs are expensed. Low demand does not justify further capitalisation.
- Deferred payment beyond normal credit: cost is the **cash price equivalent** (the rest is interest). Asset received in exchange: cost is normally **fair value**. Sales of items produced while testing go to profit or loss.

### Lesson 6.3: Subsequent costs and the measurement models

**Must know**

- **Subsequent expenditure** is capitalised only when it meets the recognition criteria, that is, it **enhances the benefits of the asset** (more output, longer life, better quality, more revenue). Replacing a major part (a new engine, a new roof) is capitalised and the carrying amount of the replaced part is derecognised.
- **Day-to-day servicing** (repairs, maintenance, repainting) is **expensed**.
- After recognition an entity chooses, **for each entire class** of PPE:

| | Cost model | Revaluation model |
|---|---|---|
| Carried at | Cost less accumulated depreciation and accumulated impairment losses | Fair value at revaluation date less subsequent accumulated depreciation and impairment losses |
| Condition | None | Fair value can be measured reliably |
| Frequency | Not applicable | Regular enough that carrying amount does not differ materially from fair value |
| Scope | Class | **Entire class** to which the asset belongs |

**Revaluation rules:**

1. **Increase:** recognised in **other comprehensive income** and accumulated in equity as **revaluation surplus**, except that it goes to profit or loss to the extent it reverses a previous revaluation decrease of the same asset charged to profit or loss.
2. **Decrease:** recognised in **profit or loss**, except that it goes to OCI (reducing the surplus) to the extent of any credit balance in the revaluation surplus for **that same asset**.
3. The surplus may be transferred **directly to retained earnings** as the asset is used (excess depreciation) or on derecognition. It never passes through profit or loss.

Revaluation surplus = Fair value − Carrying amount at revaluation date.

**Explain more**

**Worked example 4 (subsequent costs).** A bus has a carrying amount of ₦3,000,000. Ibadan Transit Ltd spends ₦1,200,000 on a new engine that extends its life, ₦80,000 repainting it and ₦50,000 on routine servicing. New carrying amount = 3,000,000 + 1,200,000 = **₦4,200,000** (ignoring the year's depreciation). Expense = 80,000 + 50,000 = ₦130,000. Where a question says nothing about the old engine, simply add the capitalised cost.

**Worked example 5 (revaluation up, then down).** Owode Mills Plc bought a building for ₦20,000,000 with a 20-year life and nil residual value (depreciation ₦1,000,000 a year). After 4 years: accumulated depreciation ₦4,000,000, carrying amount ₦16,000,000. It is revalued to ₦24,000,000.

- Surplus = 24,000,000 − 16,000,000 = **₦8,000,000** (to OCI).
- Journal: Dr Accumulated depreciation 4,000,000; Dr Building (24,000,000 − 20,000,000) 4,000,000; Cr Revaluation surplus 8,000,000.
- New depreciation over the remaining 16 years = 24,000,000 ÷ 16 = ₦1,500,000. Excess over historical-cost depreciation = 1,500,000 − 1,000,000 = ₦500,000, transferred each year: Dr Revaluation surplus 500,000; Cr Retained earnings 500,000.
- Two years later: carrying amount = 24,000,000 − (2 × 1,500,000) = ₦21,000,000; surplus left = 8,000,000 − (2 × 500,000) = ₦7,000,000. It is revalued down to ₦12,000,000. Decrease = 21,000,000 − 12,000,000 = ₦9,000,000: **₦7,000,000** against the surplus (OCI) and **₦2,000,000** to profit or loss.

**Land:** land costing ₦15,000,000 revalued to ₦19,000,000 gives a ₦4,000,000 surplus in OCI; land can be revalued though not depreciated.

- Trap: revaluing one building means revaluing the **whole class** (e.g. all land and buildings), never one item, and never inventories.
- Trap: a revaluation gain is **not** revenue and not profit; it is OCI.

### Lesson 6.4: Depreciation: principles, causes and review

**Must know**

- **Purpose:** to spread cost over the periods of use, **matching the cost of using the asset with the revenue it generates**; not to build a replacement fund or show market value.
- **Causes of depreciation (6):**
    1. use;
    2. physical wear and tear;
    3. passage of time;
    4. obsolescence;
    5. change in technology or fashion;
    6. depletion (mineral resources such as oil, gas, quarries).
- **Rules:**
    1. Depreciable amount = Cost (or revalued amount) − Residual value.
    2. The **method** must reflect the **pattern in which the asset's future economic benefits are expected to be consumed**. Methods based on revenue are not appropriate.
    3. **Component accounting:** each part with a cost significant to the total is depreciated separately (e.g. an aircraft's engines and its body).
    4. Depreciation **begins** when the asset is **available for use**, and **ceases** at the earlier of classification as held for sale (IFRS 5) and derecognition. It does not stop because the asset is idle.
    5. **Land** normally has an unlimited life and is not depreciated; buildings are. Land and buildings are separated even when bought together.
    6. Useful life, residual value and method are **reviewed at least at each financial year-end**. A change is a **change in accounting estimate, applied prospectively** (IAS 8).
    7. The charge goes to profit or loss, unless it is included in the cost of another asset.
- **Factors in useful life:** expected usage, expected physical wear and tear, technical or commercial obsolescence, legal or similar limits (e.g. lease expiry).

**Explain more**

- A Niger Delta oil well losing value as crude is extracted shows **depletion**. A rise in market prices of similar assets is **not** a cause of depreciation.
- If residual value rises to equal or exceed carrying amount, the charge is zero until it falls back below.

**Worked example 6 (change in estimate).** A machine cost ₦6,000,000, nil residual value, 12-year life: ₦500,000 a year. After 4 years, carrying amount = 6,000,000 − 2,000,000 = ₦4,000,000. The remaining life is revised to 5 years. New charge = 4,000,000 ÷ 5 = **₦800,000** a year from year 5. No restatement of years 1 to 4.

### Lesson 6.5: Depreciation methods and computations

**Must know**

**Time-based methods (5):** straight line; reducing balance; sum of the digits; annuity; sinking fund. **Service-based (usage) methods:** units of production (output, machine hours).

Formulas:

- Straight line: Annual depreciation = (Cost − Residual value) ÷ Useful life
- Straight line at a %: Depreciation = Rate × Depreciable amount (when the question says the rate applies to the depreciable amount)
- Reducing balance: Year 1 depreciation = Rate × Cost; later years = Rate × Carrying amount at the start of the year
- Reducing balance rate: R = (1 − nth root of (S ÷ C)) × 100, where n = useful life, S = residual value, C = cost
- Carrying amount after n years (reducing balance): A = P(1 − r)^n, where P = cost, r = rate
- Sum of the digits: Depreciation = (Remaining life at start of year ÷ Sum of the years' digits) × Depreciable amount; sum of digits = n(n + 1) ÷ 2
- Units of production: Rate per unit = (Cost − Residual value) ÷ Total expected units; Depreciation = Rate per unit × Units in the year

| Method | Pattern of charge |
|---|---|
| Straight line ("fixed instalment") | Same every year |
| Reducing balance | Higher early, falling each year |
| Sum of the digits | Higher early, falling by a constant amount |
| Units of production | Varies with use |

The annuity method builds interest on the capital tied up in the asset into the charge; the sinking fund method charges equal amounts that are also invested in an outside fund to replace the asset.

**Explain more**

**Worked example 7 (straight line).** Motor car cost ₦2,500,000, residual value ₦250,000, life 5 years. Depreciation = (2,500,000 − 250,000) ÷ 5 = **₦450,000** a year.

| Year | Cost ₦ | Accumulated depreciation ₦ | Carrying amount ₦ |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 2,500,000 | 450,000 | 2,050,000 |
| 2 | 2,500,000 | 900,000 | 1,600,000 |
| 3 | 2,500,000 | 1,350,000 | 1,150,000 |
| 4 | 2,500,000 | 1,800,000 | 700,000 |
| 5 | 2,500,000 | 2,250,000 | 250,000 |

Carrying amount after n years = Cost − n × annual charge. If a rate is given "on depreciable amount": a van costing ₦1,500,000 with scrap value ₦300,000 at 25% gives 25% × 1,200,000 = ₦300,000 a year. For part years, time-apportion: an asset with a ₦450,000 annual charge bought on 1 April by a 31 December company bears 450,000 × 9/12 = ₦337,500 in year 1 (unless the question states a full-year policy).

**Worked example 8 (reducing balance, rate from the formula).** Kano Haulage Ltd buys a truck for ₦1,000,000; life 3 years; residual value ₦216,000.

R = (1 − cube root of (216,000 ÷ 1,000,000)) × 100 = (1 − cube root of 0.216) × 100 = (1 − 0.6) × 100 = **40%**.

| Year | Depreciation ₦ | Carrying amount ₦ |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 40% × 1,000,000 = 400,000 | 600,000 |
| 2 | 40% × 600,000 = 240,000 | 360,000 |
| 3 | 40% × 360,000 = 144,000 | 216,000 |

Check with A = P(1 − r)^n: 1,000,000 × 0.6³ = ₦216,000, the residual value. To find a carrying amount fast, use A = P(1 − r)^n; to find a year's charge, multiply the rate by the opening carrying amount (for year 3: 1,000,000 × 0.6² × 0.4). On a calculator, the nth root is (S ÷ C) to the power 1/n; if a rate is not exact, round to two decimal places and say so.

**Worked example 9 (sum of the digits).** Cost ₦1,600,000, residual value ₦100,000, life 5 years. Depreciable amount ₦1,500,000; sum of digits = 5 × 6 ÷ 2 = 15. Charges: 5/15 × 1,500,000 = ₦500,000; then ₦400,000, ₦300,000, ₦200,000, ₦100,000 (total ₦1,500,000).

**Worked example 10 (units of production).** Aba Bottlers Ltd's machine cost ₦8,000,000, residual value ₦800,000, expected output 120,000 machine hours. Rate = 7,200,000 ÷ 120,000 = ₦60 per hour. Year 1 used 18,000 hours: 18,000 × 60 = **₦1,080,000**.

- Trap: the reducing balance rate is applied to the **carrying amount**, not to cost each year, and residual value is **not** deducted first. Units of production is **not** a time-based method.

### Lesson 6.6: Derecognition, disposal and disclosures

**Must know**

- **Derecognise** an item of PPE (a) on **disposal**, or (b) when **no future economic benefits** are expected from its use or disposal. Being fully depreciated is not a reason to derecognise if it is still in use.
- Gain or loss on disposal = **Net disposal proceeds − Carrying amount**, where Net disposal proceeds = Selling price − Costs of disposal (including costs of putting the asset in saleable condition) and Carrying amount = Cost − Accumulated depreciation (to the date of disposal).
- The gain or loss goes to **profit or loss**; a gain is **not classified as revenue**.
- On disposal of a revalued asset, any remaining revaluation surplus is transferred **directly to retained earnings**, not through profit or loss.

**Disclosures**, for each class of PPE:

1. measurement bases used for gross carrying amount;
2. depreciation methods used;
3. useful lives or depreciation rates;
4. gross carrying amount and accumulated depreciation (with accumulated impairment losses) at the start and end of the period;
5. a reconciliation of carrying amount from start to end showing additions, disposals and assets held for sale, revaluation increases and decreases, impairment losses and reversals, depreciation and other movements.

Also: restrictions on title and assets pledged as security; spending on assets under construction; commitments to buy PPE; third-party compensation. Revalued classes: revaluation date, whether an independent valuer was used, carrying amount under the cost model, and the revaluation surplus, its movement and restrictions on distribution.

**Explain more**

**Worked example 11 (disposal).** Onitsha Freight Ltd bought a truck for ₦8,000,000, residual value ₦800,000, life 6 years, straight line: (8,000,000 − 800,000) ÷ 6 = ₦1,200,000 a year. It sold the truck at the end of year 4 for ₦3,000,000, paying ₦100,000 selling costs.

- Accumulated depreciation = 4 × 1,200,000 = ₦4,800,000
- Carrying amount = 8,000,000 − 4,800,000 = ₦3,200,000
- Net proceeds = 3,000,000 − 100,000 = ₦2,900,000
- Loss = 2,900,000 − 3,200,000 = **₦300,000 loss**

Disposal account: debit cost 8,000,000; credit accumulated depreciation 4,800,000 and net proceeds 2,900,000; the remaining debit balance of 300,000 is the loss, transferred to profit or loss.

- Trap: deduct costs of disposal, and costs of making the asset saleable, from the proceeds. Count the years carefully: bought January 2023 and sold December 2025 is **three** full years of depreciation.
- Trap: compare proceeds with **carrying amount**, never cost.

### One-minute recap

- PPE = tangible, used in production/supply, rental or administration, for more than one period; recognise if benefits probable and cost reliable.
- Initial cost = purchase price (with import duty, net of trade discount, without recoverable taxes) + directly attributable costs + discounted dismantling estimate.
- Expense training, overheads, repairs, abnormal waste, and costs after the asset is ready for use.
- Subsequent cost is capitalised only if it enhances the asset; servicing and repainting are expensed.
- Cost model vs revaluation model, chosen by class; surplus to OCI, decrease to profit or loss after using up that asset's surplus.
- Depreciable amount = Cost − Residual value; method follows the pattern of consumption; reviews are changes in estimate, applied prospectively.
- Straight line = (C − S) ÷ n; reducing balance rate = 1 − nth root of (S ÷ C); A = P(1 − r)^n.
- Gain or loss on disposal = net proceeds − carrying amount, in profit or loss, not revenue.

### Theory practice

**Question:** Yaba Print Ltd bought a machine for ₦2,000,000 in January 2023, with a scrap value of ₦200,000. It is depreciated at 25% a year on a straight line basis on its depreciable amount. It was sold in December 2025 for ₦900,000 after spending ₦40,000 to put it in sellable condition. Calculate the gain or loss on disposal.

*Solution:*

- Depreciable amount = 2,000,000 − 200,000 = ₦1,800,000; annual depreciation = 25% × 1,800,000 = ₦450,000.
- January 2023 to December 2025 = 3 years; accumulated depreciation = 3 × 450,000 = ₦1,350,000.
- Carrying amount = 2,000,000 − 1,350,000 = ₦650,000.
- Net proceeds = 900,000 − 40,000 = ₦860,000.
- Gain = 860,000 − 650,000 = **₦210,000 profit on disposal**, recognised in profit or loss (not revenue).
