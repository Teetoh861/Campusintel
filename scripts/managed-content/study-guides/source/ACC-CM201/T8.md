## Topic 8: IAS 23 Borrowing Costs

**What you must know:** the core principle; what borrowing costs are; qualifying assets; specific and general borrowings; the capitalisation rate and investment income; when capitalisation starts, is suspended and stops; disclosures; the computations.

### Lesson 8.1: Core principle, borrowing costs and qualifying assets

**Must know**

- **IAS 23** deals with **accounting for borrowing costs**. (Government grants are IAS 20, revenue IFRS 15, inventories IAS 2.)
- **Core principle:** borrowing costs that are **directly attributable to the acquisition, construction or production of a qualifying asset** form part of the cost of that asset and must be **capitalised**. **Other borrowing costs are recognised as an expense** in the period in which they are incurred.
- Capitalisation is **compulsory**, not a choice. (An older version of IAS 23 allowed all borrowing costs to be expensed; the revised standard removed that option.)
- **Borrowing costs:** interest and other costs that an entity incurs in connection with the borrowing of funds.
- **Qualifying asset:** an asset that **necessarily takes a substantial period of time** to get ready for its intended use or sale.

**Borrowing costs include** (current IAS 23):

1. **Interest expense calculated using the effective interest method** (IFRS 9).
2. **Interest in respect of lease liabilities** (IFRS 16).
3. **Exchange differences** arising from foreign currency borrowings **to the extent that they are regarded as an adjustment to interest costs**.

- Older wording you may meet in questions: interest on bank overdrafts and loans; amortisation of discounts or premiums on borrowings; **amortisation of ancillary costs** incurred in arranging borrowings (e.g. arrangement fees, **management fees and service charges** on a loan); and **finance charges on (finance) leases**. Today these all sit inside the effective interest method or lease interest above.
- **Not borrowing costs:** dividends on ordinary shares, share issue costs, depreciation, repairs, income tax, audit fees, routine bank charges on customer receipts.

**Qualifying assets (depending on circumstances):** inventories that take a substantial time to produce, manufacturing plants, **power generation facilities**, intangible assets, investment properties and bearer plants.

**Not qualifying assets:**

1. **Financial assets.**
2. **Inventories manufactured or produced over a short period of time**.
3. Assets that are **ready for their intended use or sale when acquired**.

- **Scope:** IAS 23 need not be applied to qualifying assets measured at fair value (e.g. biological assets) or to inventories manufactured in large quantities on a repetitive basis.

**Explain more**

- A power plant that takes two years to build, a toll bridge built over several years, an office complex under construction for two years and a refinery built for the entity's own use are all qualifying assets. A delivery van bought ready for use from a dealer, or machinery ready for use when bought, is **not**.
- Lagos Breweries Ltd borrows to fund routine drinks produced in large quantities within a few days. The drinks are **not** qualifying assets because they are produced over a short period. Trap: borrowing money does not make an asset qualifying, and it is wrong to say inventories are always, or never, qualifying assets. Whisky maturing in casks for years can qualify.
- Trap: "interest expense under the effective interest method **and** finance charges on leases" is the pair of borrowing-cost components; dividends and share issue costs relate to equity, not borrowing.

### Lesson 8.2: Directly attributable costs and the two types of borrowing

**Must know**

- **Directly attributable** borrowing costs are those that **would have been avoided if the expenditure on the qualifying asset had not been made**. Whether the interest was paid in cash, or is above market rate, does not decide it.
- For capitalisation, borrowings are divided into **2 types**:
  1. **Specific borrowings:** funds borrowed specifically to obtain a particular qualifying asset.
  2. **General borrowings:** funds borrowed generally and used to obtain a qualifying asset.

| | Specific borrowings | General borrowings |
|---|---|---|
| Amount capitalised | Actual borrowing costs incurred in the period **less** investment income on temporary investment of those borrowings | **Capitalisation rate × expenditures** on the asset |
| Rate used | The loan's own rate | **Weighted average** of costs of general borrowings outstanding |
| Limit | Actual cost of the loan (net of income) | Cannot exceed general borrowing costs incurred in the period |

- **Investment income** earned by temporarily investing specific borrowings before they are spent is **deducted**, never added.
- Capitalisation covers only the **capitalisation period** (from commencement to cessation, less any suspension); see Lesson 8.4.

**Explain more**

- Journal for capitalised interest: Dr Property, plant and equipment (asset under construction) / Cr Interest payable (or Bank). Interest outside the capitalisation period, or on loans not used for qualifying assets: Dr Finance costs (profit or loss) / Cr Interest payable.
- Investment income on the specific loan: Dr Bank / Cr Property, plant and equipment, so the asset carries only the net cost.
- Trap: borrowing costs not directly attributable to a qualifying asset are **expensed as incurred**. They are not charged to retained earnings, deferred, or added to PPE.

### Lesson 8.3: Computing the amount to capitalise

**Must know**

- **Specific borrowing:** Capitalised = Loan × Annual rate × (Months in capitalisation period ÷ 12) − Investment income on temporary investment.
- **Capitalisation rate (general borrowings)** = general borrowing costs for the period ÷ weighted average general borrowings outstanding in the period. It is a **weighted average**, never the simple average, highest or lowest rate.
  - Weighted average general borrowings = Σ (Loan × months outstanding ÷ 12). Exclude specific borrowings (until the asset they funded is ready, after which any amount still outstanding joins the general pool).
- **General borrowing:** Capitalised = Capitalisation rate × Σ (Expenditure × months in capitalisation period after it was incurred ÷ 12).
- **Cap:** amount capitalised from general borrowings ≤ total general borrowing costs actually incurred in the period.
- **Expenditures** count only when they result in payments of cash, transfer of other assets or assumption of interest-bearing liabilities. They are **reduced by progress payments received and grants received** in connection with the asset. Eligible expenditure = Amount spent − Progress payments received − Grants received.

**Explain more**

- **Worked example (specific, investment income).** Ota Cement Ltd borrowed ₦200 million at 15% on 1 January specifically to build a kiln; work ran all year. Unused funds were temporarily invested, earning ₦3.5 million.
  - Interest = ₦200m × 15% = ₦30m
  - Capitalised = ₦30m − ₦3.5m = **₦26.5m**
- **Worked example (specific, part year).** Ikot Ekpene Mills Ltd borrowed ₦90 million at 16% on 1 January for a qualifying asset; expenditure and work began on 1 May and ran to 31 December.
  - Capitalised = ₦90m × 16% × 8/12 = **₦9.6m**
  - Expensed (January to April) = ₦90m × 16% × 4/12 = ₦4.8m
- **Worked example (general borrowings).** Enugu Power Ltd (year ended 31 December) had these general borrowings all year: ₦100m at 12%, ₦150m at 16%, ₦50m at 9%. It spent ₦60m on a plant on 1 March and ₦80m on 1 September; work continued to the year end.
  1. Interest: ₦12m + ₦24m + ₦4.5m = ₦40.5m
  2. Capitalisation rate = ₦40.5m ÷ ₦300m = **13.5%**
  3. ₦60m × 13.5% × 10/12 = ₦6.75m
  4. ₦80m × 13.5% × 4/12 = ₦3.6m
  5. Capitalised = ₦6.75m + ₦3.6m = **₦10.35m** (below the ₦40.5m incurred, so the cap does not bite)
  6. The remaining ₦40.5m − ₦10.35m = ₦30.15m is a finance cost in profit or loss.
- **Worked example (loan outstanding part of year).** General loans: ₦200m at 10% all year, and ₦100m at 15% taken on 1 July. Interest = ₦20m + (₦100m × 15% × 6/12) ₦7.5m = ₦27.5m. Weighted borrowings = ₦200m + (₦100m × 6/12) ₦50m = ₦250m. Rate = ₦27.5m ÷ ₦250m = **11%**.
- **Worked example (the cap).** Benin Builders Ltd's general borrowings cost ₦65m in the year. Rate × weighted expenditure gives ₦78m. It may capitalise only **₦65m**.
- **Worked example (grant deducted).** On 1 April an entity spends ₦80m on a qualifying asset and receives a ₦20m grant for it the same day. Eligible expenditure = ₦80m − ₦20m = ₦60m, to which the rate is applied for the 9 months to 31 December.
- Trap: for a simple average of 10%, 5% and 20% you would get 11.67%; that is wrong. Weight each rate by the size of its loan.

### Lesson 8.4: Commencement, suspension, cessation and disclosure

**Must know**

**Commencement:** capitalisation begins on the date the entity **first meets all 3 conditions**:

1. It **incurs expenditures** for the asset.
2. It **incurs borrowing costs**.
3. It **undertakes activities necessary to prepare the asset** for its intended use or sale.

- "Activities" include **technical and administrative work before physical construction**, such as **obtaining permits**. They do **not** include merely **holding** an asset when no production or development that changes its condition is taking place (e.g. land held with no development).

**Suspension:** capitalisation is suspended **during extended periods in which active development is suspended**. It is **not** suspended:

1. While **substantial technical and administrative work** is being carried out.
2. When a **temporary delay is a necessary part of the process** (e.g. high water levels that are common in the region during construction season).

**Cessation:** capitalisation ceases when **substantially all the activities necessary to prepare the asset for its intended use or sale are complete**.

- An asset is normally ready when **physical construction is complete**, even if routine administrative work continues or **minor modifications** remain (e.g. decoration to a buyer's or user's specification).
- When construction is done **in parts** and each part can be used **while construction continues on other parts** (e.g. a business park with several buildings), capitalisation stops for **each part** when substantially all activities on that part are complete. If the whole must be finished before any part can be used (e.g. an industrial plant with sequential processes), it stops when the whole is complete.
- Capitalisation does **not** depend on when the loan is repaid, when the asset is first used or sold, or when a tenant signs.

**Disclosures (2 items):**

1. The **amount of borrowing costs capitalised** during the period.
2. The **capitalisation rate** used to determine the amount eligible for capitalisation.

**Explain more**

- **Worked example (commencement).** Port Harcourt Marine Ltd borrowed ₦100m at 12% on 1 January for a jetty; expenditure and work began on 1 April. Capitalised = ₦100m × 12% × 9/12 = **₦9m**; January to March interest ₦3m is expensed, because only 2 of the 3 conditions were met.
- Owerri Hotels Ltd holds land bought with a loan, waiting for a better market, with no development activity. Borrowing costs in this period are **not** capitalised, even though interest and expenditure are being incurred.
- **Worked example (suspension).** Jos Mining Ltd's specific ₦60m loan at 10% funds a plant built all year, except 3 months when it deliberately halted work over a contractor dispute (not a necessary part of the process). Capitalised = ₦60m × 10% × 9/12 = **₦4.5m**; ₦1.5m is expensed.
- **Necessary delay.** A Niger Delta bridge stops for some weeks each year because of seasonal high water common in the region: capitalisation **continues** through those weeks.
- **Worked example (cessation).** Calabar Ports Ltd borrowed ₦120m at 15% for a warehouse; everything began on 1 January and substantially all activities were complete at the end of month 10. The loan stayed outstanding all year. Capitalised = ₦120m × 15% × 10/12 = **₦15m**; the last 2 months' interest (₦3m) is expensed.
- Ajah Homes Ltd's apartment block is physically complete; only minor decoration to each buyer's taste remains. Capitalisation has **ceased**.
- Trap: "the asset has been approved by a regulator" is **not** one of the 3 commencement conditions.

### One-minute recap

- IAS 23 = borrowing costs. Directly attributable costs of a qualifying asset **must** be capitalised; all others expensed.
- Borrowing costs: effective-interest-method interest, lease interest, qualifying exchange differences (older lists: ancillary costs such as management fees and service charges, finance lease charges). Never dividends.
- Qualifying asset = necessarily takes a substantial period to get ready. Not financial assets, quick-production inventories or ready-to-use assets.
- Directly attributable = would have been avoided without the expenditure.
- 2 types of borrowing: specific (actual cost less investment income) and general (capitalisation rate × expenditure).
- Capitalisation rate = weighted average cost of general borrowings; amount capped at actual general borrowing costs.
- Expenditure is net of progress payments and grants received.
- Start when all 3 conditions are met; suspend in extended halts of active development; stop when substantially all activities are complete.
- Disclose the amount capitalised and the capitalisation rate.

### Theory practice

**Question:** Owode Steel Ltd (year ended 31 December) had general borrowings outstanding all year: ₦400m at 10% and ₦100m at 15%. It built a rolling mill (a qualifying asset) throughout the year, spending ₦120m on 1 January and ₦80m on 1 July. Compute the capitalisation rate and the borrowing costs to capitalise.

*Solution:*

- Interest = ₦400m × 10% + ₦100m × 15% = ₦40m + ₦15m = ₦55m.
- Capitalisation rate = ₦55m ÷ ₦500m = **11%**.
- ₦120m × 11% × 12/12 = ₦13.2m; ₦80m × 11% × 6/12 = ₦4.4m.
- Capitalised = ₦13.2m + ₦4.4m = **₦17.6m** (below the ₦55m incurred). The other ₦37.4m is a finance cost. Dr PPE ₦17.6m, Dr Finance costs ₦37.4m / Cr Interest payable (or Bank) ₦55m.
