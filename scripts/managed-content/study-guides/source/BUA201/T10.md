## Topic 10: Contemporary Management Issues in Nigeria

**What you must know:** how insecurity, infrastructure deficits, inflation and foreign exchange constraints affect businesses; leadership failures and governance weaknesses; innovation, digitisation and global competition; how Nigerian managers respond, with real company cases.

### Lesson 10.1: Insecurity and infrastructure deficits

**Must know**

- **Insecurity:** threats to lives and property (banditry, kidnapping, terrorism, farmer-herder clashes, militancy, armed robbery, pipeline vandalism) that make normal business activity risky.

**Effects of insecurity on business**

1. **Higher operating costs:** private security guards, escorted convoys, higher insurance premiums.
2. **Disrupted supply of raw inputs:** farmers abandon farms, so agro-processors cannot get enough maize, sorghum or cassava.
3. **Restricted movement** of staff and goods.
4. **Smaller reachable markets** in affected areas.
5. **Difficulty attracting staff** to postings in affected areas.
6. **Reduced foreign direct investment (FDI):** foreign investors are **discouraged because of higher risk**.
7. Loss of assets, lives and investor confidence.

- **Infrastructure deficit:** inadequate or poor-quality public facilities: electricity, roads, rail, seaports, water, broadband.

**Effects of infrastructure deficits**

1. **Erratic power:** heavy spending on diesel and generators, which **raises production costs and reduces competitiveness**.
2. **Bad roads:** **longer distribution time and higher transport and vehicle-maintenance costs**; rural customers harder to reach.
3. **Congested seaports:** goods stuck for weeks, demurrage and extra charges.
4. Overall, infrastructure deficiencies **increase logistics and operating costs**.

**Common responses:** alternative power (generators, **solar systems**, gas-fired captive plants), own logistics fleets, regional warehouses, security partnerships, insurance, and locating plants close to raw materials or markets.

**Explain more**

- High diesel bills caused by unreliable public power are an **external infrastructure challenge**, not an ethical lapse, a governance issue or internal inefficiency: the cause lies outside the firm.
- An importer whose containers sit for weeks at a congested port and pays extra charges faces an **infrastructure and logistics constraint**, not a marketing, recruitment or dividend problem.
- Trap: insecurity never makes movement **easier**. "Easier movement of staff and goods across all regions" is the option that is **not** an effect.
- Telecoms operators such as MTN Nigeria and Airtel Nigeria power many base stations with diesel and suffer fibre cuts and vandalism; costs end up in tariffs and reduce profit.
- Under the **Petroleum Industry Act (PIA) 2021**, oil companies must fund **Host Communities Development Trusts** (3% of their actual annual operating expenditure), partly to reduce conflict and vandalism in producing areas.

### Lesson 10.2: Inflation, foreign exchange and the cost of finance

**Must know**

- **Inflation:** a **sustained rise in the general price level** over time. Not a one-time rise in one product's price; a fall in the general price level is **deflation**.
- Measured in Nigeria by the **Consumer Price Index (CPI)** published by the **National Bureau of Statistics (NBS)**.
- **Inflation rate = (CPI this period - CPI last period) / CPI last period x 100.**
- **Types:** **demand-pull** (too much money chasing too few goods) and **cost-push** (rising input costs such as fuel, power, imports and wages).

**Effects of high inflation on business**

1. **Reduces consumers' purchasing power** and **raises input costs**.
2. **Erodes the real value of fixed incomes** and savings, so workers demand pay rises.
3. Makes **forecasting costs and prices**, budgeting and long-term planning difficult (frequent price-list and menu changes).
4. Pushes up interest rates as the central bank tightens money supply.

- **Exchange rate:** the price of one currency in terms of another, e.g. naira per US dollar. **Depreciation** of the naira means **more naira are needed for each dollar**.
- **Naira cost of an import = Dollar price x Exchange rate.**

**Effects of naira depreciation and forex scarcity**

1. **Raises the naira cost of imported inputs**, so firms **dependent on imported raw materials** suffer most.
2. **Exporters paid in dollars receive more naira for each dollar earned.**
3. **Forex scarcity** makes it hard for multinationals to obtain dollars to **pay foreign suppliers or send profits (dividends) to parent companies**.
4. Firms with dollar loans see the naira value of their debt rise.

- **High lending interest rates** make bank loans **expensive** and **limit SMEs' ability to expand**.

**Explain more**

- **Worked example 1 (inflation rate):** CPI rises from 200 to 250. Inflation = (250 - 200) / 200 x 100 = **25%**.
- **Worked example 2 (real income):** a worker earns ₦100,000 a month and prices rise 25%. Real value = 100,000 / 1.25 = **₦80,000** in old prices. To keep the same standard of living the salary must rise to 100,000 x 1.25 = ₦125,000. This is why textile workers demand a pay rise after rapid price increases.
- **Worked example 3 (depreciation and an importer):** a firm imports 20,000 US dollars of raw materials. At ₦800 per dollar they cost 20,000 x 800 = ₦16,000,000. If the naira falls to ₦1,500 per dollar, they cost 20,000 x 1,500 = ₦30,000,000, an increase of (1,500 - 800) / 800 x 100 = **87.5%**.
- **Worked example 4 (exporter):** a cashew exporter earns 10,000 US dollars. At ₦800 per dollar it receives ₦8,000,000; at ₦1,500 per dollar it receives ₦15,000,000. Depreciation helps exporters, other things being equal.
- **Recent context:** petrol subsidy removal (announced 29 May 2023) and the Central Bank of Nigeria's unification of exchange-rate windows (June 2023) sent the naira from under ₦500 per dollar to well above ₦1,000 per dollar and pushed inflation sharply up. The CBN raised its Monetary Policy Rate repeatedly in 2023 and 2024, making loans dearer.
- Several multinationals cited forex difficulties when scaling down: **GSK Consumer Nigeria** (2023) announced it would stop operating in Nigeria and use third-party distributors, and **Procter & Gamble** (December 2023) moved from local manufacturing to an import model.
- **Import substitution** (producing locally what you used to import) mainly reduces **exposure to foreign exchange risk**. **Backward integration** into local raw materials does the same: a brewery that sets up its own farms to grow grains it once imported is integrating **backward**, not forward.

### Lesson 10.3: Leadership failures and governance weaknesses

**Must know**

- **Corruption:** the **abuse of entrusted power for private gain** (Transparency International's definition).
- **Effects:** **raises the cost of doing business and creates unfair competition**; contracts go to the connected, not the efficient; public trust falls; investors stay away.
- **Ethical response to a bribe request:** **refuse, document the request and report it through the proper channels.** Paying from personal funds or hiding it as "miscellaneous" is still bribery.
- Nigeria's main anti-corruption bodies: the **Independent Corrupt Practices and Other Related Offences Commission (ICPC)** and the **Economic and Financial Crimes Commission (EFCC)**.

**Governance weaknesses that hurt business**

1. **Weak corporate governance and insider abuse:** boards that fail to check the chief executive; insider loans.
2. **Poor succession planning:** no one prepared to take over when the founder or leader leaves.
3. **Policy inconsistency:** sudden import bans or tariff changes **increase uncertainty and make long-term planning difficult**.
4. **Multiple taxation:** several overlapping levies and taxes from different levels of government on the same activity.
5. **Slow and uncertain contract enforcement:** court cases take years, so firms avoid giving credit.
6. **Large informal sector:** many unregistered businesses make **reliable data and regulation harder** and narrow the tax base.
7. **Weak regulation and enforcement** generally (e.g. against counterfeiting and pollution).

**Explain more**

- **Bank case:** in August 2009 the CBN removed the chief executives of five banks (including Oceanic Bank and Intercontinental Bank) over huge non-performing and insider loans. Oceanic's former chief executive, Cecilia Ibru, later pleaded guilty and forfeited assets. The lesson: when a board fails to check a chief executive who grants unsecured loans to firms he secretly controls, the cause of failure is **weak corporate governance and insider abuse**.
- In June 2024 the CBN revoked **Heritage Bank's** licence, saying its financial condition had kept deteriorating and its board and management had failed to improve its performance: a reminder that weak oversight can destroy even established firms.
- **Succession:** many Nigerian family businesses collapse after the founder's death because heirs were not prepared and ownership quarrels follow. Good practice: groom successors early, separate ownership from management, and appoint professional managers and independent directors.
- **Multiple taxation** is not a tax incentive or a tax holiday (those reduce tax to attract investment). The 2025 tax reform laws, in force from January 2026, aim partly to harmonise taxes and reduce this burden.
- **Contract enforcement:** a supplier that refuses credit to new customers because debt recovery through the courts takes years is reacting to slow and uncertain enforcement of contracts.
- **Host communities:** when a pipeline leak damages farmland and fishing waters, the best long-term response is to **clean up, compensate fairly and engage the community through social responsibility programmes**, not to deny the damage or pay only community leaders privately.

### Lesson 10.4: Innovation, digitisation and global competition

**Must know**

- **Innovation:** turning new ideas into new or better products, processes or business models. An **innovation strategy** supports **competitive advantage**.
- **Digitisation:** using digital technology (mobile apps, cloud, online platforms, data analytics) to run business activities.
- Managers rely on innovation and digitisation to **improve efficiency, reach new customers and stay competitive**.

**Benefits of digital payments and mobile banking for small businesses:** faster, easier transactions; access to customers without relying only on cash; records of sales; access to credit based on transaction history. They do **not** guarantee protection from all fraud or remove the need for records.

**Challenges of digitisation**

1. **Cybersecurity risk:** phishing, hacking, data breaches.
2. **Shortage of staff with the needed digital skills.**
3. Cost of technology and unreliable power and internet.
4. Data protection compliance (**Nigeria Data Protection Act 2023**).

- **Globalisation:** the **increasing integration of national economies through trade, investment, technology and information flows**.
    - **Challenge:** **stronger competition from imported goods and foreign firms**; pressure to meet international standards.
    - **Opportunity:** **access to wider international markets and foreign investment**, technology and partnerships.
- **Brain drain:** skilled professionals emigrating for better pay and conditions (popularly "japa").
- **HR response to brain drain:** improve **retention through better pay, training and career development**, plus succession and replacement planning.
- **Counterfeiting:** fake copies bearing a firm's brand name, causing lost sales and **unfair competition**.

**Explain more**

- An employee who clicks a fake email link and lets hackers into the customer database illustrates **cybersecurity risk**, not an infrastructure, tax or forex problem. Defences: staff training, strong passwords and multi-factor authentication, backups.
- Nigerian fintechs such as **Paystack** (acquired by Stripe in 2020), **Flutterwave**, **Moniepoint** and **OPay** have spread point-of-sale terminals, transfers and mobile wallets to traders across the country.
- Under the **African Continental Free Trade Area (AfCFTA)**, which Nigeria signed in July 2019 and under which trading began in January 2021, Nigerian firms gain a wider African market but also face more competition.
- A Lagos hospital that cannot fill specialist posts because doctors have moved abroad is experiencing **brain drain**. Cutting training or salaries makes it worse.
- **Counterfeiting** is fought with regulators such as **NAFDAC** (food, drugs, cosmetics) and the **Standards Organisation of Nigeria (SON)**, plus anti-fake packaging and consumer education.

### Lesson 10.5: Managing in Nigeria: responses and company cases

**Must know**

**Contemporary challenges facing managers in Nigeria (summary):** insecurity; infrastructure deficits; inflation; foreign exchange constraints; high interest rates; corruption and weak governance; policy inconsistency; multiple taxation; brain drain; counterfeiting; cybersecurity threats; global competition. **Uninterrupted, cheap public power is not one of them.**

**Managerial responses**

1. **Scenario planning:** preparing responses to **several possible future situations** (it does not predict the future exactly or replace budgets).
2. **Backward integration and local sourcing** to cut forex exposure.
3. **Import substitution.**
4. **Alternative power** (generators, solar, gas).
5. **Innovation and digitisation.**
6. **Cost control, hedging and pricing reviews** under inflation.
7. **Strong governance, ethics and succession planning.**
8. **Talent retention** strategies.
9. **Corporate social responsibility** and community engagement.

**Correct challenge-effect pairs**

| Challenge | Most direct business effect |
|---|---|
| Inflation | Lower consumer purchasing power; higher input costs |
| Naira depreciation | Higher naira cost of imported inputs |
| Insecurity | Higher security and insurance costs; disrupted supplies |
| Poor roads | Slower deliveries; higher transport costs |
| Erratic power | Higher production costs (diesel) |
| Corruption | Higher cost of doing business; unfair competition |

**Explain more**

- **Dangote Group:** backward integration in cement using local limestone helped turn Nigeria from a cement importer into a self-sufficient producer; the **Dangote Petroleum Refinery** at Lekki (designed for 650,000 barrels a day) aims to reduce the country's reliance on imported fuel and the forex it consumes.
- **Nigerian Breweries and other brewers** replaced much imported barley with **locally sourced sorghum** through farmer outgrower schemes: import substitution that cuts forex risk.
- **GSK Consumer Nigeria and Procter & Gamble** (2023) show the opposite outcome: when forex scarcity, high costs and inflation make local manufacturing unviable, multinationals scale down or exit.
- **Moniepoint and OPay** show innovation turning infrastructure gaps (few bank branches, cash dependence) into business opportunities.
- **Oceanic and Intercontinental Banks** (2009) show the cost of weak boards and insider lending.
- Exam pattern: when a question pairs a challenge with an effect, choose the pair that makes economic sense (depreciation raises import costs); reject pairs that reverse it (inflation raising purchasing power, insecurity lowering insurance, bad roads speeding deliveries).

### One-minute recap

- Insecurity raises operating costs, disrupts raw inputs, restricts movement and discourages FDI.
- Infrastructure deficits (power, roads, ports) raise logistics and operating costs and are external challenges.
- Inflation = sustained rise in the general price level; it cuts purchasing power, raises costs and erodes fixed incomes.
- Naira depreciation raises importers' costs and gives exporters more naira per dollar; forex scarcity blocks payments and profit repatriation.
- Corruption = abuse of entrusted power for private gain; refuse, document and report bribe requests.
- Governance weaknesses: insider abuse, poor succession, policy inconsistency, multiple taxation, slow contract enforcement, informality.
- Digitisation brings speed and reach but also cybersecurity risk and skills gaps.
- Globalisation = integration of economies; threat of imports, opportunity of wider markets.
- Responses: scenario planning, backward integration, import substitution, alternative power, retention, CSR.

### Theory practice

**Question:** A Nigerian paint maker imports pigments worth 12,000 US dollars a month. The exchange rate moves from ₦900 per dollar to ₦1,350 per dollar. (a) What is the change in the naira cost of the pigments, and by what percentage does it rise? (b) Name two strategies the firm could use to reduce this exposure.

*Solution:*

1. Old cost = 12,000 x 900 = ₦10,800,000.
2. New cost = 12,000 x 1,350 = ₦16,200,000.
3. Increase = 16,200,000 - 10,800,000 = **₦5,400,000**; percentage = 5,400,000 / 10,800,000 x 100 = **50%**.
4. (b) **Backward integration or local sourcing** of pigments (import substitution), and passing costs through careful price reviews or hedging; scenario planning helps prepare for further moves.
