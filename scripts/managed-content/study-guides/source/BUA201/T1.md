## Topic 1: Introduction to Business and Management

**What you must know:** what business is, its features, how it evolved and why it exists; stakeholders, sectors and the resources managers combine; what management is (efficiency, effectiveness, art and science); and the evolution of management thought from Taylor, Fayol and Weber through Mayo, Maslow, Herzberg and McGregor to quantitative, systems, contingency and modern approaches.

### Lesson 1.1: Meaning and nature of business

**Must know**

- **Business:** any organised activity that provides goods or services to customers on a regular basis with the aim of earning a profit. It is carried on under risk and uncertainty.
- **Profit:** the amount by which total revenue exceeds total expenses. Profit = Total revenue − Total expenses. If expenses are higher, the result is a **loss**.
- **Characteristics of business:**

1. Exchange (dealings) in goods or services for value.
2. Regularity of dealings (a one-off sale is not business).
3. Profit motive.
4. Risk and uncertainty (profit is never guaranteed).
5. Buyer and seller.
6. Satisfaction of human wants (creation of utility).
7. An economic activity (not done for love, pleasure or as a gift).

- **Factors of production and their rewards:**

| Factor | Reward |
| --- | --- |
| Land (all natural resources) | Rent |
| Labour (human effort) | Wages (salaries) |
| Capital (man-made aids such as machines, buildings) | Interest |
| Entrepreneur (organiser and risk-bearer) | Profit |

- Profit is a **reward**, not a factor of production.
- **Entrepreneur:** a person who brings together land, labour and capital to start a business and bears the risk of losing his money.

- **Evolution of business:** subsistence (producing only for your own use) → **barter** (goods for goods) → **money** → trade and commerce → **Industrial Revolution** (Britain, late 18th century) → the modern company.
- **Main problem of barter:** the need for a **double coincidence of wants** (you must find someone who has what you want and wants what you have). Money solved it.
- **Separation of ownership from control** came most directly from the **Industrial Revolution**: factories needed huge capital, so many shareholders put in money (owners) while hired professional managers ran the firm (control).

**Explain more**

- A trader in Mile 12 Market who regularly buys rice in bulk and resells at a profit is in business. A mother cooking for her family, a birthday gift, or Aisha selling her own used laptop once for ₦150,000 are not; the laptop sale fails mainly on **regularity**, even though it had a buyer, a seller, a money price and a gain.
- Chidi uses his savings, rents a shop in Yaba, hires two bakers and buys ovens, knowing he could lose his money. He is acting as an **entrepreneur**: he organises the other factors and bears the risk.
- **Worked example:** Chidi's bakery earns total revenue of ₦2,500,000 in a month and spends ₦1,900,000 on flour, wages, rent and diesel. Profit = ₦2,500,000 − ₦1,900,000 = **₦600,000**. The ₦2,500,000 is revenue, not profit, and the savings he started with are capital, not profit.
- Other barter problems: no common measure of value, goods cannot be divided easily, no store of value.
- Traps: business does **not** guarantee profit; it covers **services** as well as goods.

### Lesson 1.2: Purpose of business, stakeholders and key operating elements

**Must know**

- **Purposes of business in society:** providing goods and services people need; creating jobs and incomes; paying taxes (government revenue); earning profit for owners; creating wealth and innovation; contributing to community development (social responsibility). Making the laws of a country is **not** a purpose of business; that is government's job.
- **Stakeholder:** any individual or group that can affect or is affected by the business's activities.
  - **Internal:** employees, managers, owners.
  - **External:** customers, suppliers, government (tax authority, regulators), creditors, host community.
- **Sectors of the economy:**

| Sector | Activity | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Primary | Extracting natural resources | Farming (cocoa), mining (tin), fishing, forestry |
| Secondary | Turning raw materials into goods | Cement, textile, food factories; construction |
| Tertiary | Providing services | Banking, insurance, transport, retail |

- **Commerce** = trade (buying and selling: home and foreign) + **aids to trade** (banking, insurance, transport, communication, warehousing, advertising).
- **Non-profit organisation:** uses any surplus to pursue its mission instead of distributing it to owners. It still needs management and may employ paid staff.
- **Organisation:** a group of people working together in a structured way to achieve common goals (goals, people, structure).
- **Four resources managers combine (key operating elements):**

1. **Human:** managers and staff (e.g. the sales force).
2. **Financial:** money (e.g. a bank loan of ₦5,000,000).
3. **Physical:** buildings, machines, vehicles, raw materials (e.g. delivery vans).
4. **Information:** data managers use (e.g. market research on customer preferences).

**Explain more**

- A firm in Ogun State that turns limestone into bags of cement is in the **secondary** sector; the quarry stage alone is primary. A commercial bank is **tertiary**.
- Customer satisfaction is a **goal**, not a resource.
- Every business also works through functional areas (production or operations, marketing, finance and accounting, human resources) inside an **environment** of customers, competitors, government and technology. Goals, people, structure, resources, technology and management are the operating elements that make it run.

### Lesson 1.3: What management is

**Must know**

- **Management** (Mary Parker Follett): "the art of getting things done through people."
- **Henri Fayol:** to manage is to forecast and plan, to organise, to command, to coordinate and to control.
- **Working definition:** the process of planning, organising, leading and controlling an organisation's human, financial, physical and information resources to achieve its goals efficiently and effectively.

| | Efficiency | Effectiveness |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Meaning | Using the fewest resources (time, money, labour) for a given output | Achieving the goals the organisation set |
| Phrase | Doing things right | Doing the right things |
| Concerned with | Means (inputs) | Ends (results) |

- Good management needs **both**.
- **Management is both an art and a science:** it rests on a body of organised principles (science), but applying them well requires personal skill, experience, creativity and judgement (art).

**Explain more**

- A Lagos factory makes plastic chairs at the lowest unit cost in its industry, but customers no longer want the design and most remain unsold: **efficient but not effective**.
- Why not a "pure" science: management deals with people, so its principles do **not** give exactly the same result in every situation. Why not a "pure" art: it does have principles that can be taught, so it is not learnt only from experience.
- A manager who relies on experience, creativity and judgement while applying management principles is practising management as **both** an art and a science.

### Lesson 1.4: The classical school

**Must know**

The classical approach has **three** branches: **scientific management**, **administrative management**, **bureaucratic management**. The human relations movement is **not** classical.

**Scientific management: Frederick W. Taylor** (father of scientific management; *The Principles of Scientific Management*, 1911). Main focus: raise **worker efficiency** through systematic study of tasks and methods.

1. Develop a science for each part of a job (replace rule of thumb).
2. Scientifically select, train and develop workers.
3. Cooperate with workers so work follows the scientific method.
4. Divide work and responsibility between management and workers.

- **Time and motion study:** find the **one best way** of doing a job and set a standard time for it.
- **Differential piece-rate:** a worker who produces **more** than the standard is paid a **higher rate per unit**; below standard, a lower rate.
- **Frank and Lillian Gilbreth:** motion study (bricklaying; **therbligs** = basic hand motions).
- **Henry Gantt:** the **Gantt chart**, a bar chart showing planned and actual progress of tasks against time.

**Administrative management: Henri Fayol** (French industrialist; father of modern management theory). **5 elements (functions):** planning, organising, commanding, coordinating, controlling. **Staffing is not one of Fayol's five.**

**Fayol's 14 principles:**

1. **Division of work:** specialisation.
2. **Authority and responsibility:** right to give orders, matched by responsibility.
3. **Discipline:** obedience and respect for agreements.
4. **Unity of command:** each employee receives orders from **only one superior**.
5. **Unity of direction:** **one head and one plan** for a group of activities with the same objective.
6. **Subordination of individual interest to the general interest.**
7. **Remuneration:** fair pay.
8. **Centralisation:** degree to which authority is concentrated at the top.
9. **Scalar chain:** the unbroken line of authority from highest to lowest rank, through which communication passes.
10. **Order:** "a place for everything and everything in its place", for people and things.
11. **Equity:** kindness and justice.
12. **Stability of tenure of personnel.**
13. **Initiative:** allowing staff to think up and carry out plans.
14. **Esprit de corps:** harmony and team spirit.

**Bureaucratic management: Max Weber** (German sociologist). Ideal bureaucracy features: division of labour; clear **hierarchy** of authority; formal written **rules** and procedures; **impersonality**; selection and promotion on **technical qualification**; career orientation. Authority is **rational-legal** (based on rules and the position held), not traditional (custom) or charismatic (personal appeal).

**Explain more**

- Taylor worked at Midvale and Bethlehem Steel. In the pig-iron study, a loader's output rose from 12.5 to 47.5 tons a day: increase = (47.5 − 12.5) ÷ 12.5 × 100 = **280%**.
- **Criticism of Taylor:** treated workers like **economic machines** (paid only for output), ignored social needs, made work monotonous.
- Applying Fayol: Bola gets orders from both the sales and finance managers = breach of **unity of command**. A Kano mill splitting spinning, weaving and dyeing = **division of work**. A manager refusing to lend company vans for a private event = **subordination of individual interest**. A Lagos supermarket letting cashiers test new queue ideas = **initiative**.
- Unity of command is about **one boss per person**; unity of direction is about **one plan per objective**. Exams swap them.
- Promotion based on personal loyalty to the boss is the **opposite** of Weber's bureaucracy.

### Lesson 1.5: Behavioural (human relations) and quantitative schools

**Must know**

- **Behavioural school** emerged to address the **human and social side of work** (informal groups, worker attitudes) that classical theory largely ignored.
- **Hawthorne studies** (Western Electric, near Chicago, 1924 to 1932; **Elton Mayo**): the Human Relations School grew mainly out of them. Key finding that challenged classical theory: **informal groups influence productivity**; social needs and attention matter, not just wages.
- **Hawthorne effect:** workers change their behaviour or productivity because they know they are being **observed and given attention**.
- **Human relations approach:** emphasis on social needs of workers and informal groups.
- **Chester Barnard:** **acceptance theory of authority** (authority depends on whether subordinates **accept** orders).
- **Abraham Maslow's hierarchy (bottom to top):** physiological, safety, social (belonging), esteem, **self-actualisation** (top).
- **Frederick Herzberg's two-factor theory:**
  - **Hygiene factors** (prevent dissatisfaction): **salary**, working conditions, company policy, supervision, job security.
  - **Motivators** (create satisfaction): achievement, recognition, the work itself, responsibility, advancement.
- **Douglas McGregor:**

| Theory X | Theory Y |
| --- | --- |
| Workers dislike work, avoid responsibility | Work is natural; people seek responsibility |
| Must be closely supervised, controlled, threatened | Exercise self-direction and self-control when committed to objectives |
| Tight supervision, centralisation, punishment | Job enrichment, empowerment, participation |

- **Quantitative (management science) approach:** mathematical and statistical tools such as **linear programming** and **operations research** to improve decisions (from World War II).

**Explain more**

- Hawthorne began as **lighting** tests: output rose in both test and control groups, even when light was dimmed, so lighting was not the cause.
- A firm applying Theory Y involves employees in setting their own goals and gives them responsibility for results. Clock-in checks and penalties are Theory X.
- A manager who uses strict controls in a crisis but participative methods in stable periods is applying **Theory X and Theory Y situationally**. McGregor himself favoured Theory Y.
- Herzberg trap: salary does not motivate on its own; it is hygiene.

### Lesson 1.6: Systems, contingency and modern approaches

**Must know**

- **Systems approach:** the organisation is a set of interrelated parts (subsystems) that takes **inputs**, **transforms** them and returns **outputs**, with **feedback**.
  - **Open system:** interacts with its environment. **Closed system:** does not.
  - **Synergy:** parts working together achieve more than they would separately.
  - **Entropy:** a system runs down without new inputs.
- **Contingency (situational) approach:** the best management practice **depends on the situation**; there is no one best way.
- **Management by objectives (MBO), Peter Drucker:** managers and subordinates **jointly** set specific goals and later review performance against them.
- **Modern thinking** stresses flexibility because organisations operate in **dynamic and complex environments**: total quality management (Deming, Juran), learning organisations, digitisation, globalisation.

**Historical order:**

1. Scientific management (about 1880s to 1910s)
2. Human relations (1920s to 1930s)
3. Quantitative (1940s)
4. Systems (1950s to 1960s)
5. Contingency (1960s to 1970s)
6. Modern approaches

**Explain more**

- Systems view of a bakery: inputs = flour, sugar, bakers' labour; transformation = mixing and baking; outputs = bread; feedback = customer complaints.
- Contingency contradicts Taylor's "one best way".
- Under MBO, targets are not imposed from the top; that is its whole point.

### One-minute recap

- Business = regular, organised provision of goods or services for profit, under risk.
- Profit = total revenue − total expenses; profit is the entrepreneur's reward, not a factor.
- Barter needed a double coincidence of wants; the Industrial Revolution separated ownership from control.
- Sectors: primary (extract), secondary (make), tertiary (serve). Insurance is an aid to trade.
- Four resources: human, financial, physical, information.
- Efficiency = doing things right; effectiveness = doing the right things. Management is both art and science.
- Classical: Taylor (scientific), Fayol (5 elements, 14 principles), Weber (bureaucracy, rational-legal).
- Behavioural: Hawthorne and Mayo, Barnard, Maslow, Herzberg (salary = hygiene), McGregor X and Y.
- Then quantitative, systems (synergy, open system), contingency ("it depends"), modern (MBO, Drucker).

### Theory practice

**Question:** A Surulere bakery aims to sell 2,000 loaves a week at ₦700 each. In Week 1 it bakes 2,000 loaves at a total cost of ₦1,000,000 and sells all of them. In Week 2 it cuts total cost to ₦800,000 for 2,000 loaves but sells only 1,200. Find the cost per loaf and profit each week, and say which week was more efficient and which more effective.

**Solution:**

- Week 1: cost per loaf = ₦1,000,000 ÷ 2,000 = ₦500. Revenue = 2,000 × ₦700 = ₦1,400,000. Profit = ₦1,400,000 − ₦1,000,000 = **₦400,000**.
- Week 2: cost per loaf = ₦800,000 ÷ 2,000 = ₦400. Revenue = 1,200 × ₦700 = ₦840,000. Profit = ₦840,000 − ₦800,000 = **₦40,000**.
- Week 2 is **more efficient** (₦400 per loaf against ₦500: fewer resources per unit). Week 1 is **more effective** (it met the 2,000-loaf sales goal). Efficiency alone is not enough: Week 2's lower unit cost earned a tenth of Week 1's profit because it missed the goal.
