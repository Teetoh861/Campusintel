## Topic 9: Functional Areas II: Accounting, Finance and Production

**What you must know:** what accounting is, its users and concepts; the accounting equation; the three financial statements and the main ratios; the finance function, its three decisions and sources of finance; budgets, cash budgets and break-even; production systems, quality, productivity and the supply chain; operations challenges in Nigeria; how marketing, finance, HR and operations work together.

### Lesson 9.1: Accounting, its users, concepts and the accounting equation

**Must know**

- **Accounting:** the process of **identifying, measuring, recording and communicating financial information** to users for decision-making.
- **Main purpose:** to provide financial information that helps users make **economic decisions**. It does not replace planning, set every price or guarantee profit.
- **Internal users:** managers, directors, employees. **External users:** shareholders and potential investors, lenders (banks), suppliers, customers, government and tax authorities (e.g. the Nigeria Revenue Service, formerly FIRS), regulators, the public.

| Point | Financial accounting | Management accounting |
|---|---|---|
| Main users | External (shareholders, lenders, tax authority) | Internal (managers) |
| Time focus | Mainly past transactions | Past and future (forecasts, budgets) |
| Format | Fixed, published format set by law and standards | Flexible, as managers need |
| Detail | Summary for the whole business | Detailed, by product or department |

**Accounting concepts**

1. **Business entity:** the business is separate from its owner; the owner's private spending is not a business expense.
2. **Going concern:** the business will continue to operate for the foreseeable future.
3. **Accruals (matching):** income and expenses are recorded when earned or incurred, not when cash is received or paid; expenses are matched to the revenue they helped earn.
4. **Historical cost:** assets are recorded at what they originally cost.
5. **Consistency:** the same methods are used year after year.
6. **Prudence:** do not overstate assets or profit; provide for likely losses.
7. **Money measurement:** only items that can be measured in money are recorded.
8. **Duality (double entry):** every transaction has two equal effects.

**Accounting equation:** Assets = Liabilities + Owners' equity. Rearranged: Owners' equity = Assets - Liabilities; Liabilities = Assets - Owners' equity.

**Explain more**

- **Entity in action:** Mrs Ade pays her children's school fees from her shop's cash. It is recorded as **drawings** (the owner taking value out), not a shop expense: the business entity concept.
- **Accruals in action:** a printer finishes a ₦400,000 job in December; the customer pays in January. The ₦400,000 is December income. Recording it in January would be the **cash** basis, which is not the accruals concept.
- When FIRS (now the Nigeria Revenue Service) studies published accounts to assess tax, it is an **external** user, not the preparer or a shareholder.
- Trap: going concern is about continuing; "assets at current market value" is not going concern, and "business separate from owner" is entity.
- **Worked example 1 (equity):** assets ₦8,000,000, liabilities ₦2,500,000. Owners' equity = 8,000,000 - 2,500,000 = **₦5,500,000**.
- **Worked example 2 (assets):** liabilities ₦3,000,000, owners' equity ₦4,500,000. Assets = 3,000,000 + 4,500,000 = **₦7,500,000**.
- Common wrong options add when you should subtract (₦8,000,000 + ₦2,500,000) or just repeat one figure.

### Lesson 9.2: Financial statements and ratios

**Must know**

1. **Statement of financial position (balance sheet):** assets, liabilities and owners' equity **at a particular date**.
2. **Income statement (statement of profit or loss):** revenue and expenses **over a period** and the resulting profit or loss.
3. **Statement of cash flows:** the **movement of cash into and out of** a business over a period, under three headings:
    - **Operating:** cash from trading (receipts from customers, payments to suppliers and staff).
    - **Investing:** buying or selling long-term assets (machines, land, vehicles).
    - **Financing:** raising or repaying long-term funds (loans, share issues, dividends paid).

- **Current assets** (turn into cash within a year): inventory, trade receivables, cash at bank, cash in hand. **Non-current assets:** land and buildings, machinery, vehicles.
- **Current liabilities** (due within a year): bank overdraft, trade payables, tax due. **Non-current liabilities:** long-term loans, debentures (e.g. a ten-year debenture). Share capital is **equity**, not a liability.

**Formulas**

- Gross profit = Revenue - Cost of sales
- Net profit (before tax) = Gross profit - Operating expenses
- Gross profit margin = Gross profit / Revenue x 100
- Net profit margin = Net profit / Revenue x 100
- Working capital = Current assets - Current liabilities
- Current ratio = Current assets / Current liabilities
- Debt-equity ratio = Total debt / Owners' equity
- Straight-line depreciation per year = (Cost - Residual value) / Useful life in years

| Ratio group | Measures | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Liquidity | Ability to meet **short-term** obligations | Current ratio, working capital |
| Profitability | Ability to earn profit from sales | Gross and net profit margin |
| Gearing (leverage) | Reliance on debt; financial risk | Debt-equity ratio |

**Explain more**

- A ₦15,000,000 bottling machine paid for is **investing**; a ₦20,000,000 five-year bank loan received is **financing**.
- **Worked example 3 (profits and margins):** revenue ₦10,000,000, cost of sales ₦6,000,000, operating expenses ₦2,500,000.
    - Gross profit = 10,000,000 - 6,000,000 = ₦4,000,000.
    - Net profit = 4,000,000 - 2,500,000 = ₦1,500,000.
    - Gross profit margin = 4,000,000 / 10,000,000 x 100 = 40%. Net profit margin = 1,500,000 / 10,000,000 x 100 = 15%.
- Trap: margin divides by **revenue**, not by cost of sales. Dividing ₦5,000,000 gross profit by ₦7,000,000 cost would wrongly give 71.43%; by revenue of ₦12,000,000 it is 41.67% (rounded to 2 decimal places).
- A **high gross profit margin** means sales revenue is high relative to cost of goods sold. Net profit margin measures **profitability**.
- **Worked example 4 (liquidity):** current assets ₦6,000,000, current liabilities ₦2,400,000. Working capital = 6,000,000 - 2,400,000 = ₦3,600,000. Current ratio = 6,000,000 / 2,400,000 = **2.5 : 1**. Dividing the wrong way round (2.4 / 6) gives 0.4 : 1, a favourite wrong option.
- A **very high current ratio** may show strong liquidity **or idle assets** (too much cash or stock not being used productively).
- **Worked example 5 (gearing):** debt ₦3,000,000, equity ₦6,000,000. Debt-equity = 3,000,000 / 6,000,000 = 0.5. A **high** debt-equity ratio means **high financial leverage** and higher risk, not strong liquidity or low risk.
- **Worked example 6 (depreciation):** a machine costs ₦3,000,000, lasts 4 years, residual value ₦600,000. Annual charge = (3,000,000 - 600,000) / 4 = **₦600,000**. Forgetting to subtract residual value gives ₦750,000, a typical wrong option.

### Lesson 9.3: The finance function and sources of finance

**Must know**

- **Finance function:** obtaining funds and allocating them efficiently to achieve the firm's objectives.
- **Financial planning:** forecasting **cash flows** and funding needs for the future.

**Three finance decisions**

1. **Investment (capital budgeting) decision:** what long-term assets to spend on, e.g. an ₦8,000,000 oven expected to raise output for ten years.
2. **Financing decision:** how to raise funds, e.g. issuing new shares or taking a bank loan; the mix of debt and equity is the **capital structure**.
3. **Dividend decision:** how much profit to pay shareholders and how much to keep (retained earnings) for expansion.

**Sources of finance by time**

| Term | Usual period | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Short | Up to 1 year | Trade credit from suppliers, bank overdraft, short-term loans, factoring, accrued expenses |
| Medium | 1 to 5 years | Medium-term bank loans, hire purchase, leasing |
| Long | Over 5 years | Ordinary shares, preference shares, debentures, long-term loans, retained earnings, venture capital |

- **Internal sources:** retained earnings (ploughed-back profit), sale of unused assets, better control of working capital. **External:** bank loans, new shares, debentures, trade credit, venture capital, government grants.
- **Equity** = ownership money (shares, retained earnings). **Debt** = borrowed money (loans, debentures, overdraft).

| Ordinary shareholders | Debenture holders |
|---|---|
| Owners | Lenders |
| May receive **dividends** (only if declared) | Receive fixed **interest** whether or not profit is made |
| Vote at general meetings | No vote; often secured on assets |
| Paid last on winding up | Paid before shareholders |

- **Simple interest = Principal x Rate x Time.**

**Explain more**

- **Venture capital** suits **start-ups with high growth potential**; investors take equity and accept high risk for high reward. Mature firms with stable cash flows can use cheaper sources.
- A **rights issue** offers new shares to existing shareholders in proportion to their holdings; it increases **share capital** (equity), not debt or leverage.
- **Dividend policy** directly affects **retained earnings**: the more paid out, the less kept for growth.
- **Financial leverage:** using more debt mainly increases **financial risk**, because interest must be paid even in bad years.
- **Capital rationing:** when funds are limited, managers must **prioritise the projects with the highest returns**, not accept every project.
- **Diversification of risk** reduces the **variability of overall returns**. A diversified firm spreads its activities across a **portfolio of businesses**, so a loss in one is cushioned by others. **Conglomerate diversification** means moving into unrelated businesses (Dangote Group spans cement, sugar, salt, fertiliser and petroleum refining). Concentrating on one market, retrenchment or downsizing does not spread risk.
- **Worked example 7 (simple interest):** ₦1,500,000 borrowed from a microfinance bank at 20% a year for 3 years. Interest = 1,500,000 x 0.20 x 3 = **₦900,000**. Total repayable = 1,500,000 + 900,000 = ₦2,400,000. Do not confuse interest with total repayment.

### Lesson 9.4: Budgeting, cash flow and break-even

**Must know**

- **Budget:** a financial plan, expressed in figures, for a future period. It is a tool for **planning and control**.
- **Budgetary control:** compare actual results with the budget. **Variance = Actual - Budget**; it is **favourable** if it raises profit (higher revenue or lower cost) and **adverse** if it lowers profit.
- **Variance analysis** supports **corrective action** when actual results differ from planned results.
- **Cash budget (cash flow forecast):** expected receipts and payments month by month. It helps a business **avoid cash shortages that could lead to insolvency** and plan borrowing in advance.
- **Closing cash = Opening cash + Receipts - Payments.**
- **Profit is not cash:** a business can show a profit and still run out of cash because **profit is recorded when sales are made, but customers may not have paid yet** (also cash tied up in stock, loan repayments, asset purchases).
- **Cost-volume-profit (CVP) analysis** finds the **break-even point**: the output where total revenue equals total cost (no profit, no loss).
    - Contribution per unit = Selling price - Variable cost per unit
    - Break-even units = Fixed costs / Contribution per unit
    - Break-even revenue = Break-even units x Selling price

**Explain more**

- Small businesses often keep a simple cash book and monthly cash forecast; large organisations prepare a master budget built from sales, production, labour, overhead, capital expenditure and cash budgets.
- **Worked example 8 (cash budget):** opening cash ₦500,000, receipts ₦1,800,000, payments ₦2,100,000. Closing = 500,000 + 1,800,000 - 2,100,000 = **₦200,000**. If instead opening cash were ₦200,000, receipts ₦900,000 and payments ₦1,300,000, closing = **-₦200,000**: the forecast warns the owner to arrange an overdraft early.
- **Worked example 9 (variance):** budgeted transport cost ₦500,000, actual ₦560,000. Variance = 560,000 - 500,000 = ₦60,000 **adverse** (12% over budget), so the manager investigates and corrects.
- **Worked example 10 (break-even):** fixed costs ₦2,400,000 a month; price ₦2,000; variable cost ₦1,200.
    - Contribution = 2,000 - 1,200 = ₦800 per unit.
    - Break-even = 2,400,000 / 800 = **3,000 units**; break-even revenue = 3,000 x 2,000 = ₦6,000,000.
    - At 4,000 units, profit = (4,000 - 3,000) x 800 = ₦800,000.
- Trap: dividing fixed costs by the selling price (2,400,000 / 2,000 = 1,200) is wrong; always use contribution.

### Lesson 9.5: Production, quality, productivity and the supply chain

**Must know**

- **Production:** the process of transforming inputs into goods and services. **Operations management** plans and controls that process.
- **Input-transformation-output model:** **inputs** (labour, raw materials, machines, capital, energy, information) → **transformation** (manufacturing, assembling, serving) → **outputs** (finished goods and services, customer satisfaction), with **feedback** for control.

**Production systems**

1. **Job (unit) production:** one item made to a customer's exact order, e.g. a Surulere tailor's single wedding gown.
2. **Batch production:** a set quantity of one product, then the line switches to another, e.g. 500 wheat loaves, then 300 coconut loaves, then 400 meat pies.
3. **Mass (flow) production:** identical products in very large volumes along an assembly line, e.g. a soft drinks bottling line.
4. **Continuous process production:** non-stop processing, often 24 hours, e.g. cement, refining, flour milling.
5. **Project production:** a large one-off undertaking, e.g. a bridge or a refinery.

- **Efficiency:** achieving output with **minimum use of resources**. **Effectiveness:** achieving the **intended goals**.
- **Productivity = Output / Input.** Labour productivity = Output / Labour hours.
- **Percentage change = (New - Old) / Old x 100.**
- **Capacity utilisation = Actual output / Maximum possible output x 100.**
- **Quality control (QC):** inspecting output to **detect** defects. **Quality assurance (QA):** building quality into the process to **prevent** defects.
- **TQM:** organisation-wide commitment to quality and customer satisfaction through **continuous improvement**. **Kaizen** (Japanese): continuous, small, step-by-step improvement.
- **Benchmarking:** comparing with leading firms to identify and adopt **best practices**.
- **Supply chain:** the network of organisations and activities that moves a product from raw material suppliers to the final customer (suppliers → manufacturer → distributors → retailers → customers).
- **Just-in-time (JIT):** minimise inventory by receiving inputs only when they are needed for production.

**Explain more**

- **Worked example 11 (labour productivity):** 4,800 units from 600 labour hours = 4,800 / 600 = **8 units per labour hour**. Dividing hours by units (600 / 4,800 = 0.125) is the reverse and wrong.
- **Worked example 12 (productivity change):** last month 5,000 units / 500 hours = 10 per hour; this month 6,600 units / 600 hours = 11 per hour. Change = (11 - 10) / 10 x 100 = **10% increase**. Note output rose 32%, but productivity rose only 10% because hours also rose; always compare output **per hour**.
- **Worked example 13 (capacity):** a plant able to make 40,000 tonnes produced 32,000. Utilisation = 32,000 / 40,000 x 100 = **80%**.
- To **raise** productivity: reduce waste and machine downtime, improve layout, train workers, use better technology. **Increasing inputs while output stays the same lowers productivity.**
- **Economies of scale:** as output grows, **unit cost** falls (bulk buying, specialised machines, cheaper finance).
- TQM stresses continuous improvement, not inspection only at the end or downsizing; its pioneers include **Deming** and **Juran**.
- **JIT's weakness:** it is especially vulnerable to **supply chain disruptions**, since there is little buffer stock. In Nigeria, port delays, bad roads and forex shortages make pure JIT risky.
- **Vertical integration** (control of more stages of the supply chain):
    - **Backward:** a manufacturer takes over or sets up its **suppliers**, producing its own raw materials (Dangote Cement owns limestone quarries; Nigerian brewers source local sorghum through farmer outgrower schemes).
    - **Forward:** a manufacturer acquires or sets up its own **distributors or retail outlets**.
    - **Horizontal integration** is buying a competitor at the same stage; **market penetration** is selling more of current products in current markets.
- **Outsourcing** improves efficiency when the firm's **core competence is protected** (keep what you do best, outsource the rest), not when everything is handed out.
- **Operations strategy** must align with **corporate strategy**: a low-cost firm needs efficient mass production; a premium firm needs quality and flexibility.

### Lesson 9.6: Operations challenges in Nigeria and how the functions work together

**Must know**

**Operations challenges in Nigeria**

1. Erratic power supply and heavy reliance on diesel generators.
2. Bad roads and congested seaports (longer delivery time, higher transport and maintenance costs).
3. Foreign exchange shortages and naira depreciation raising the cost of imported inputs and machinery.
4. Insecurity disrupting supply of raw materials and movement of goods.
5. Inadequate skilled labour and weak maintenance culture.
6. High cost of finance and multiple taxation.
7. Counterfeiting and inconsistent government policy.

**How the functions work together**

- **Marketing** forecasts demand and sets the price and promotion.
- **Operations** plans capacity, materials and output to meet that forecast.
- **Finance** budgets and raises the money for capacity, stock and staff.
- **HR** recruits and trains the people needed.
- **Accounting** records results and reports variances for control.

**Explain more**

- A Kano manufacturer whose deliveries to Lagos take far longer than planned because of poor roads faces an **infrastructure-related operations challenge**, not a segmentation, dividend or going concern problem.
- **Good integration:** marketing forecasts 30% higher demand; **operations plans extra capacity, finance budgets for it, and HR plans the extra staff.** Cutting the budget, freezing recruitment or waiting for "proof" are poor coordination.
- **Poor integration:** if marketing **overestimates** demand and production follows, operations ends up with **excess inventory** (money tied up, storage cost, possible write-off). Underestimating causes shortages and lost sales.
- Shared forecasts, regular cross-functional meetings, integrated software and joint KPIs keep the functions aligned.

### One-minute recap

- Accounting identifies, measures, records and communicates financial information for decisions; users are internal or external.
- Concepts: entity, going concern, accruals, historical cost, consistency, prudence, money measurement, duality.
- Assets = Liabilities + Owners' equity.
- Three statements: financial position (a date), income statement (a period), cash flows (operating, investing, financing).
- Margins divide by revenue; working capital = CA - CL; current ratio = CA / CL; high debt-equity = high leverage and risk.
- Finance decisions: investment, financing, dividend. Sources: short, medium, long; internal or external; equity or debt.
- Budget = plan in figures for planning and control; closing cash = opening + receipts - payments; break-even = fixed costs / contribution.
- Production systems: job, batch, mass, continuous process, project. Productivity = output / input.
- QC detects, QA prevents; TQM and kaizen = continuous improvement; JIT is vulnerable to supply disruption.
- Marketing, operations, finance and HR must plan together.

### Theory practice

**Question:** A pure-water producer in Ibadan has fixed costs of ₦3,600,000 a month. Each bag sells for ₦500 and has a variable cost of ₦300. (a) How many bags must it sell each month to break even? (b) What profit does it make if it sells 25,000 bags?

*Solution:*

1. Contribution per bag = 500 - 300 = ₦200.
2. (a) Break-even = 3,600,000 / 200 = **18,000 bags** (break-even revenue = 18,000 x 500 = ₦9,000,000).
3. (b) Bags above break-even = 25,000 - 18,000 = 7,000. Profit = 7,000 x 200 = **₦1,400,000**.
4. Check: revenue 25,000 x 500 = ₦12,500,000; total cost = 3,600,000 + 25,000 x 300 = ₦11,100,000; profit = ₦1,400,000.
