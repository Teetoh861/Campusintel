## Topic 3: The Global Marketplace

**What you must know:** why nations trade; absolute and comparative advantage; balance of trade and balance of payments; exchange rates; free trade vs protectionism and the trade barriers; the WTO, IMF, World Bank and the trading blocs; cultural, legal, political and economic differences abroad; the forms of international business; multidomestic, global and transnational strategies.

### Lesson 3.1: What international trade is and why nations trade

**Must know**

- **International trade:** the exchange of goods and services across national borders. Trade inside one country is **domestic trade**.
- **Global (international) business:** a business that operates in several countries. A **domestic business** operates in one country only.
- **Multinational corporation (MNC):** a company with operations (offices, factories, sales units) in several national markets, e.g. MTN, Nestlé, Unilever.
- **Importing:** buying goods or services from another country. **Exporting:** producing in one country and selling to buyers in another. Both are forms of global business engagement.
- **Why nations and firms trade:**
  1. To focus on their **strengths** (what they produce best).
  2. To **expand markets**: broader market access and more customers.
  3. To pursue **economies of scale** (lower cost per unit from larger output).
  4. To **acquire** materials, goods and services they lack or cannot make cheaply.
  5. To **keep up with customers** who have gone international.
  6. To **keep up with competitors** already abroad.

| Term | Meaning | Credited to |
|---|---|---|
| **Absolute advantage** | A country can produce a good **more efficiently** (with fewer resources) than any other country | Adam Smith |
| **Comparative advantage** | A country produces the goods it makes **relatively** most efficiently (lowest opportunity cost) and trades for the rest, even if it is not the best at anything | David Ricardo |

**Explain more**

- International trade is **driven by comparative advantage**. Nigeria exports crude oil and imports machinery because each country gains by specialising where its opportunity cost is lowest.
- Example of comparative advantage: suppose Country A can make both cloth and cocoa faster than Country B, but its lead is much bigger in cloth. A should specialise in cloth and B in cocoa; both end up with more by trading.
- **Benefits of trade:** broader market access, lower costs through scale, wider choice of products, access to resources and technology. **Trap:** "higher production costs" and "narrower product range" are not benefits; trade tends to lower costs and widen choice.
- A franchise or a partnership is a form of ownership or arrangement; neither term by itself means a firm operates in several countries.

### Lesson 3.2: Measuring trade and exchange rates

**Must know**

- **Balance of trade:** the difference between the value of a country's **exports** and the value of its **imports** over a period (goods and services).
  - **Trade surplus (favourable balance):** exports > imports.
  - **Trade deficit (unfavourable balance):** imports > exports.
- **Balance of payments:** the **total** of all money flowing into and out of a country over a period. It includes the balance of trade **plus** investment flows, foreign aid, loans, interest, dividends and transfers (e.g. remittances). It is the broader measure.
- **Exchange rate:** the rate at which one country's currency can be exchanged for another's, e.g. ₦ per US dollar.
- **Floating exchange rate:** set mainly by demand and supply in the currency market. **Fixed (pegged) rate:** held by the government or central bank at a set level.

| If the naira | Nigerian exports become | Imports into Nigeria become |
|---|---|---|
| **Weakens** (more ₦ per $; depreciation or devaluation) | Cheaper to foreigners (helps exporters) | Dearer (hurts importers) |
| **Strengthens** (fewer ₦ per $) | Dearer to foreigners | Cheaper |

**Explain more**

- **Trap:** balance of trade is **not** GDP (total output), employment levels or inflation (price increases). It is only the export-import difference.
- **Trap:** balance of trade covers trade only; balance of payments covers trade **and** all other money flows. A country can have a trade deficit but still receive large inflows, e.g. foreign investment or diaspora remittances.
- In Nigeria the Central Bank of Nigeria (CBN) manages monetary and exchange-rate policy. Exchange-rate movements are part of the economic environment of global business: a firm that buys raw materials in dollars and sells in naira is hurt when the naira falls.

### Lesson 3.3: Conflicts in international trade

**Must know**

- **Free trade:** international trade with no government barriers. Supported because it raises efficiency, lowers prices and widens choice.
- **Protectionism:** government policies that restrict imports to protect domestic industries and jobs.
- **Trade conflicts** arise mainly from **barriers like import duties**, tariffs and quotas, which can trigger retaliation (a **trade war**), e.g. the US-China trade tensions in which each side raised tariffs on the other's goods.

**Main trade barriers and restrictions:**

1. **Tariff:** a tax or levy on **imported** (inbound) goods. Raises the price of foreign products and protects local producers; also earns government revenue.
2. **Import quota:** a limit on the **quantity** of a product that may be imported.
3. **Embargo:** a total **ban** on trade in a product or with a country (often for political reasons).
4. **Restrictive import standards:** strict rules on quality, health, safety or labelling that make importing harder.
5. **Export subsidy:** government payment or aid to domestic exporters so they can sell more cheaply abroad.
6. **Sanctions:** politically motivated restrictions on trade with a country.
7. **Anti-dumping measures:** actions against **dumping**, i.e. selling goods abroad below cost or below the price charged at home, often to drive out local competitors.

**Explain more**

- **Trap:** a tariff is **not** an export incentive payment (that is a subsidy), not a type of money (currency) and not a trade agreement.
- **Tariff vs quota:** a tariff works through **price**; a quota works through **quantity**. An embargo is the extreme case: quantity is zero.
- In Nigeria, import duties are collected by the Nigeria Customs Service. Restrictive standards are applied through bodies such as the Standards Organisation of Nigeria (SON).
- Arguments for protection: protect infant industries, save jobs, national security, fight unfair practices like dumping. Arguments against: higher prices for consumers, less choice, inefficient local firms and retaliation. Open policies, cooperative alliances and stable exchange rates tend to **reduce** conflict, not cause it.

### Lesson 3.4: International trade organisations and trading blocs

**Must know**

| Organisation | Main role |
|---|---|
| **WTO** (World Trade Organization), 1995, Geneva; replaced GATT | **Promotes free and fair trade globally**: sets trade rules, negotiates trade agreements, settles trade disputes |
| **IMF** (International Monetary Fund) | Promotes **financial and exchange-rate stability**; lends to countries with balance-of-payments problems |
| **World Bank** | Lends and gives aid for **long-term development** projects (roads, power, health, education) in developing countries |

- **Trading bloc:** a group of countries that reduce trade barriers among themselves and often act together on trade.
  1. **ECOWAS** (Economic Community of West African States): founded 1975 by the Treaty of Lagos; promotes economic integration and free movement of people and goods in West Africa. Nigeria is a member.
  2. **AU** (African Union): replaced the Organisation of African Unity (OAU) in 2002; HQ Addis Ababa. Its **AfCFTA** (African Continental Free Trade Area) aims at a single African market.
  3. **EU** (European Union): European states with a single market; many share one currency, the **euro**.
  4. **NAFTA / USMCA:** the North American Free Trade Agreement (USA, Canada, Mexico) was replaced in 2020 by the **United States-Mexico-Canada Agreement (USMCA)**.
  5. **APEC** (Asia-Pacific Economic Cooperation): Pacific Rim economies (including the USA, China, Japan and Australia) promoting freer trade in the region.
  6. **ASEAN** (Association of Southeast Asian Nations).

**Explain more**

- **Trap:** the question "which organisation promotes free trade globally?" is the **WTO**, not the IMF (finance), the UN (peace and security) or the WHO (health).
- **IMF vs World Bank:** IMF = short-term financial stability and currency problems; World Bank = long-term development projects. Both were created at Bretton Woods in 1944 and are based in Washington, DC.
- Blocs encourage trade **inside** the bloc but can make it harder for outsiders. ECOWAS, for instance, has a common external tariff applied to goods from outside the region.

### Lesson 3.5: The global business environment

**Must know**

- **Global business environment:** the many forces that affect how a firm operates abroad, especially **cultural, political (legal) and economic** differences between countries. It is never just one factor (only money, only technology or only regulation).

1. **Cultural differences:** language, religion, values, customs, social behaviour, attitudes to time, gift-giving, negotiation styles and body language. **Ethnocentrism** (judging others by your own culture) and **stereotyping** cause failure abroad.
2. **Legal and political differences:** each country has its own laws on contracts, tax, ownership, labour and product standards; political stability and government attitudes to foreign firms vary. **Bribery** is illegal in most countries, e.g. the US Foreign Corrupt Practices Act forbids US firms to bribe foreign officials.
3. **Economic differences:** level of development, income, infrastructure, inflation and **currency exchange rates**.

**Explain more**

- A firm entering a new country must research its culture: a product name, colour or advert that works in Lagos may offend or confuse buyers elsewhere.
- **Trap:** ignoring local customs is a common reason firms fail abroad; it is never a sound strategy.
- Political risk includes sudden policy changes, import bans, nationalisation of foreign assets and unrest.

### Lesson 3.6: Forms of international business and strategic approaches

**Must know**

**Forms of international business activity** (roughly from least to most commitment and risk):

1. **Importing and exporting:** buying from and selling to other countries; the simplest entry.
2. **International licensing:** a firm lets a foreign company use its patent, trademark, technology or brand for a fee (**royalty**).
3. **International franchising:** a franchisor lets a foreign franchisee run a business under its name and system for fees, e.g. fast-food chains.
4. **Contract manufacturing:** a firm pays a foreign producer to make its goods, which it then sells under its own name.
5. **Strategic alliance:** a long-term partnership between firms to share resources or knowledge, **without** creating a new company.
6. **Joint venture:** two or more firms (often a foreign and a local one) jointly **create and own** a new business, sharing costs, risks and profits.
7. **Foreign direct investment (FDI):** owning physical assets in another country, e.g. building a factory or buying a local firm, often run as a **subsidiary**. Firms that do this widely become **multinational corporations**.

**Strategic approaches to international markets:**

| Approach | Product and marketing | Also called |
|---|---|---|
| **Multidomestic** | Each country unit operates fairly independently; offerings **tailored** to each local market | Adaptation |
| **Global** | **Same** product and marketing worldwide, controlled from headquarters; lowest cost | Standardisation |
| **Transnational** | Mix: global efficiency where possible, local adaptation where needed ("think globally, act locally") | |

**Explain more**

- A sound international strategy usually involves **tailoring offerings to regions** (adaptation) to suit local tastes, culture and needs. Overlooking local customs, shunning rivals or cutting exports are not strategies.
- **Standardisation vs adaptation:** standardisation gives economies of scale and one brand image; adaptation fits local markets better but costs more.
- **Licensing vs franchising:** licensing transfers the right to use a patent, trademark or technology; franchising transfers a whole way of running the business.
- **Strategic alliance vs joint venture:** both are partnerships; only a joint venture creates a **new jointly owned entity**.
- Exporting, licensing and franchising need little investment and carry low risk, but give less control. FDI gives most control and profit but carries most risk.

### One-minute recap

- International trade = cross-border exchange of goods and services, driven by comparative advantage.
- Absolute advantage (Smith) = most efficient producer; comparative advantage (Ricardo) = lowest opportunity cost.
- Balance of trade = exports minus imports; balance of payments = all money flows in and out.
- A weaker naira makes exports cheaper and imports dearer.
- Barriers: tariffs (levy on imports), quotas, embargoes, standards, subsidies, sanctions, anti-dumping; barriers like import duties cause trade conflicts.
- WTO = free trade; IMF = financial stability; World Bank = development. Blocs: ECOWAS, AU, EU, USMCA, APEC, ASEAN.
- Global environment = cultural, political/legal and economic differences.
- Forms: import/export, licensing, franchising, contract manufacturing, alliances, joint ventures, FDI; an MNC operates in several national markets.
- Strategies: multidomestic (adapt), global (standardise), transnational (mix).
