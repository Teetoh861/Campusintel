## Topic 8: Production Systems and Operations

**What you must know:** the systems view of business (open systems, boundaries, input-process-output, feedback); value chains and value webs; supply chain management; production and operations management and the types of production; the distinctive challenges of service delivery; product quality and process quality.

### Lesson 8.1: The systems view of business

**Must know**

- **System:** a series of related parts that are dependent on one another; a group of interacting or interrelated elements that act according to a set of rules to form a **unified whole**.
- **Business system:** a defined set of principles, practices and procedures applied to specific activities to achieve a specific result.
- **Systems view of business:** sees a company as a complex, **interconnected** system of **interdependent parts** (departments or functions). Each part contributes to the organisation's overall goals, and a change in one area can affect the entire system. It views the business **as a whole rather than as isolated parts**, and takes account of its interactions with the external environment.
- **Organisational boundary:** the line that **separates the organisation (the system) from its environment**. Inputs cross it coming in; outputs cross it going out.

**4 basic points of the systems view:**

1. **Interdependence:** each component (marketing, finance, operations and so on) relies on the others, so a change in one area has ripple effects throughout the organisation.
2. **Open system:** most businesses constantly interact with, and are influenced by, factors outside the organisation: market trends, customer needs, economic conditions.
3. **Input-process-output:** inputs (resources, information) are transformed through processes into outputs (products, services).
4. **Feedback loop:** the system continuously monitors its performance and adjusts its processes based on feedback from the environment and from internal operations.

- **Open vs closed:** an open system interacts with its environment (inputs in, outputs out, feedback back); a closed system is cut off from it. Almost every real business is open.

**Explain more**

- A business transforms **lower-value inputs into higher-value outputs**, adding value at every stage. A bakery takes in flour, labour, ovens and electricity (inputs), bakes (transformation) and sells bread (output). Complaints about stale bread are **feedback**.
- Interdependence: if marketing runs a big promotion without telling production, stock runs out and finance loses revenue.
- **Trap:** the systems view focuses on **interconnected business parts**, not on isolated job functions, promotion alone or budgeting alone.
- **Trap:** an *organisational* boundary surrounds the whole organisation; a *process* boundary marks the limits of a single process. Exams ask for the first.

### Lesson 8.2: Value chains and value webs

**Must know**

- **Value chain:** a **linear sequence of activities within a company** that add value to a product or service at each stage, from raw materials to the final customer. In short, the steps that enhance a product's worth.
- **Value chain analysis:** a framework (Michael **Porter**) for analysing a company's activities and identifying areas of **competitive advantage**. It breaks operations into activities classified into **2 basic sets**: **primary** and **support** activities.

**5 primary activities** (directly make, sell and support the product):

1. **Inbound logistics:** receiving, storing and handling raw materials and inputs coming in.
2. **Operations:** turning inputs into the finished product.
3. **Outbound logistics:** storing and **distributing finished goods (inventory)** to customers.
4. **Marketing and sales:** persuading customers to buy; pricing, advertising, selling.
5. **Service:** after-sales support: installation, repairs, customer care.

**4 support activities** (help the primary activities work):

1. **Procurement:** buying inputs.
2. **Technology development:** research, design, process improvement, IT.
3. **Human resource management:** recruiting, training, rewarding staff.
4. **Firm infrastructure:** general management, planning, finance, legal, quality systems.

**3 steps in value chain analysis:**

1. Identify the primary activities.
2. Identify the support activities.
3. Analyse the linkages between these activities.

- **Value web:** a more complex **network of interconnected value chains across different companies**, often involving collaborations and partnerships, where value is created through multiple interactions between stakeholders in a **less linear** fashion. Essentially, a value web is a **network of value chains**.

| Value chain | Value web |
|---|---|
| Linear (straight-line) | Network, less linear |
| Within one company | Across many companies and partners |
| One firm does most steps itself | Firms specialise and link their chains together |

**Explain more**

- Count traps: **2** basic sets, **5** primary activities, **4** support activities, **3** steps of analysis. Read the question carefully to see which count it wants.
- **Trap:** technology development, procurement, HR management and firm infrastructure are **support**, never primary. Marketing and sales and service are **primary**.
- **Trap:** inbound logistics = materials coming **in**; outbound logistics = finished goods going **out** (distribution of inventory).
- Example: a bottling company receives sugar (inbound), bottles drinks (operations), trucks crates to distributors (outbound), advertises (marketing and sales) and fixes shop coolers (service); its HR and IT are support.
- Value webs arise when firms **outsource** and partner: a phone brand designs phones, buys chips from one firm, has another assemble them and sells bundles through MTN. Each firm runs its own chain; together they form a web, giving flexibility but less control.

### Lesson 8.3: Supply chain management

**Must know**

- **Supply chain:** the set of firms and activities that bring a product from its raw materials to the end consumer.
- **Supply chain management (SCM):** coordinating and **overseeing** the activities involved in producing, distributing and delivering goods or services from their **source to the end consumer**; everything from sourcing raw components to delivering the final result. In short, managing the **flow of goods**.
- **Key activities:** sourcing, design, production, warehousing, shipping, distribution, order management.
- **Goal:** to improve **efficiency, quality, productivity and customer satisfaction**.
- **Scope:** managing internal resources, operations management, and coordinating with external partners: suppliers, manufacturers, distributors, retailers and customers.
- **Importance:** ensures products are available **when and where needed**, minimising costs and maximising profits.

**5 components of SCM:**

1. **Procurement:** sourcing raw materials and other components.
2. **Production:** manufacturing the product.
3. **Logistics:** managing the movement and storage of goods.
4. **Distribution:** getting the finished product to the end customer.
5. **Order management:** handling customer orders and ensuring timely fulfilment.

**5 stages in the concept of SCM:**

1. **Planning the supply chain network:** plan each "link" so steps join smoothly and every stakeholder knows its responsibilities.
2. **Sourcing materials:** choose suppliers, decide how much to order, track supplies, pay suppliers.
3. **Organising manufacturers:** find the right manufacturer to turn raw materials into the end product.
4. **Delivery of the product:** reliable logistics; damaged goods, late delivery or wrong orders hurt reputation and lose customers.
5. **Managing return services:** convenient returns and exchanges; reduces waste, since returned items can go to other customers.

**Explain more**

- SCM involves the flow of **goods, information and money** among all the partners. It is about overseeing and coordinating flow, never halting production, ignoring vendors or restricting delivery.
- **Supply chain vs value chain:** the supply chain looks at how materials and products **move** from source to consumer; the value chain looks at how each activity **adds value**. They overlap heavily, and SCM is how a firm manages its part of the value web.
- Example: a Lagos supermarket chain orders from manufacturers, warehouses stock, trucks it to branches and handles returns; a break at any link (a port backlog, fuel scarcity) leaves empty shelves.
- **Inventory:** too much ties up money and space; too little loses sales. **Just-in-time (JIT)** delivers materials only as needed, cutting storage costs but exposing the firm to late suppliers.

### Lesson 8.4: Production and operations management

**Must know**

- **Production system:** a system used to produce goods or services, often with multiple steps that must be completed to produce the final product. In operations management, it is the **organised set of activities, processes and resources involved in transforming inputs into finished goods or services**.
- **Production and operations management (POM):** the process of using (turning) an organisation's resources to **create and deliver goods and services**. It involves **planning, scheduling and controlling** production so that products are made and delivered **on time and in line with customer demand**.
- **Production management** relates to the activities around **creating products**. **Operations management** concerns the **production and delivery** of products and services.
- **Aim of operations management:** produce efficiently, **boosting quality and output** while using resources well and cutting waste.

**Scope of production management** (planning, organising, directing and controlling production; it starts with input and ends with output, the finished good):

1. **Location of facilities:** a key decision because large investment goes into land, buildings and machinery.
2. **Plant layout and material handling:** layout = physical arrangement of facilities; material handling = moving materials from the storeroom to the machine and from one machine to the next.
3. **Product design:** converting ideas about the product into reality.
4. **Process design:** deciding the overall process route for converting raw materials into finished goods.

| Production management | Operations management |
|---|---|
| Manages all production activities | Manages complete overall business operations, including production and post-production stages |
| Limited scope: design, pricing, quality and quantity of goods | Broader scope: routine activities such as workforce and inventory management |
| High initial capital requirement | Lower capital requirement |
| Only where products are manufactured | All organisations, including banks and hospitals |

**Types of production:**

1. **Mass production:** large quantities of **identical** (standardised) goods, usually on assembly lines, at low unit cost. Example: bottled water, cement.
2. **Customised production:** goods or services made to **each customer's individual order** (also called job production when one-off). Example: a tailor sewing an agbada, a building contractor.
3. **Mass customisation:** a blend: standard parts mass-produced, then combined to suit individual customers. Example: choosing a laptop's memory and storage when ordering.
4. **Batch production:** a set quantity (batch) of one product is made, then the equipment is switched to the next batch. Example: a bakery baking batches of different breads.
5. **Continuous production:** operations run **non-stop**, often 24 hours a day, turning out a constant flow of standard output. Example: oil refining, cement and flour milling.

**Explain more**

- **Trap:** POM focuses on **streamlined (efficient) goods creation**, not advertising (marketing), recruitment (HR) or financial targets (finance).
- **Trap:** operations management is the **broader** term and applies to service firms too; production management applies only where goods are manufactured.
- Other POM decisions: **forecasting** demand, **capacity planning** (how much a plant, e.g. a Dangote cement plant, can produce), **scheduling** (what is done when) and **inventory control**.
- **Lean production:** maximum output with minimum waste; JIT is one lean tool.
- Higher volume and standardisation lower unit cost but reduce flexibility; customised production is flexible but costly per unit.

### Lesson 8.5: Service delivery and quality

**Must know**

**Distinctive challenges of service delivery:**

1. **Intangibility:** services cannot be seen, touched or stored before purchase, so quality is hard to judge.
2. **Perishability:** an unsold service is lost for ever (an empty seat on a flight, an unused hour of a barber's time); it cannot be stored as inventory.
3. **Customer participation (involvement):** many services are produced while the customer is present and taking part (salon, clinic, classroom), so the customer's own input affects the result.
4. **Variability (performance variability):** services are performed by people, so quality varies from one provider, customer or day to the next; **quality control is variable** and hard.
5. **Location constraints and scalability:** many services must be delivered where the customer is, and grow only by adding people, so expanding is harder than running a factory extra hours.
6. **Management difficulties:** service delivery needs planning and many management skills: **time management** (several jobs at once), **managing other people's work** (one of the biggest challenges) and **coordination**: **poor coordination between departments leads to poor service quality**.

**Product quality vs process quality:**

- **Product quality:** the inherent characteristics of the **final product**, judged by whether it **meets customer expectations (standards)** in functionality, durability and other attributes.
- **Process quality:** how well a production **process** adheres to established standards and procedures, ensuring **consistency and minimising defects** at each step; the efficiency of the production method.
- **Link:** good process quality directly contributes to high product quality.
- **Process vs product:** a process is a set of steps that guides the work to a good product; a product is the result.

| Product | Process |
|---|---|
| The final output | A sequence of steps to create the output |
| Focuses on the final result | Focuses on completing each step |
| Follows firm guidelines | Consistently follows guidelines |
| Tends to be short-term | Tends to be long-term |
| Goal: complete the work successfully | Goal: make the quality better |
| Created from customers' needs and expectations | A model for producing various goods in a similar way |
| **Product layout:** materials placed in a single line in the order of operations | **Process layout:** resources with similar processes or functions grouped together |
| Product patents give greater protection | Process patents give only limited protection |

**Quality tools:**

1. **Statistical process control (SPC):** sampling output and charting results to spot when a process drifts from its standard.
2. **Continuous improvement (kaizen):** constant small improvements by everyone.
3. **Total quality management (TQM):** an organisation-wide commitment to quality and customer satisfaction.
4. **Six Sigma:** a data-driven method aiming at no more than **3.4 defects per million** opportunities.
5. **ISO 9000 family:** international quality management standards; firms are certified under **ISO 9001**.

**Explain more**

- **Trap:** the challenges of services are **variable quality control** and **client participation**. "Tangible consistency", "uniform execution", "low variation" and "minimal client contact" describe physical goods, the opposite.
- **Trap:** product quality is about **meeting customer standards**, not production cost, promotion, or looks alone. Process quality is about **production method efficiency**, not costs, promotion or staff onboarding.
- In Nigeria, the **Standards Organisation of Nigeria (SON)** sets and enforces standards for products. ISO 9001 certification signals that a firm's processes meet an international standard.

### One-minute recap

- A system is interdependent parts forming a unified whole; the systems view sees a business as interconnected parts, not isolated functions.
- Systems view: interdependence, open system, input-process-output, feedback loop. The organisational boundary separates the organisation from its environment.
- Value chain = linear steps adding value inside one firm (Porter): 2 sets, 5 primary, 4 support activities, 3 steps of analysis.
- Primary: inbound logistics, operations, outbound logistics (distribution of inventory), marketing and sales, service. Support: procurement, technology development, HRM, firm infrastructure.
- Value web = a network of linked value chains across firms.
- SCM oversees the flow of goods from source to end consumer: procurement, production, logistics, distribution, order management.
- POM = planning, scheduling and controlling the turning of resources into goods and services; operations management is broader than production management.
- Types of production: mass, customised, mass customisation, batch, continuous.
- Service challenges: intangibility, perishability, customer participation, variability, coordination.
- Product quality = meeting customer expectations; process quality = consistent, efficient production method.
