## Topic 10: E-business and Business Communication

**What you must know:** e-commerce vs e-business and the perspectives on e-commerce; the EC framework; click-only, click-and-mortar and brick-and-mortar firms; B2B, B2C, C2C, B2E and the other transaction types, including m-commerce; business models and online auctions (forward and reverse); benefits and limitations; the communication process model, ways of receiving information and channels; formal communication, the grapevine and barriers.

### Lesson 10.1: E-commerce and e-business

**Must know**

- **Electronic commerce (EC or e-commerce):** the online exchange of goods, services and money between firms, and between firms and their customers. In short, buying, selling, transferring or exchanging products, services and information over computer networks, mostly the internet.
- **E-business (electronic business):** the conducting of business on the internet. It is a broader term: the use of electronic communications for **all business processes**, not just buying and selling. It covers sales, purchasing and services on the internet, plus serving customers, collaborating with business partners, e-learning and transactions inside the organisation.
- E-commerce goes beyond the sale itself: it can involve the events **leading to a purchase** (searching, comparing, advertising) as well as **customer service after the sale**.

**Perspectives on e-commerce:** e-commerce is often seen simply as buying and selling on the internet, but all these perspectives apply:

1. **Communications:** delivering information, products, services or payments over telephone lines, computer networks or other electronic means.
2. **Business process:** applying technology to automate business transactions and workflows.
3. **Service:** a tool that helps firms, consumers and management cut service costs while improving the quality and speed of service.
4. **Online:** buying and selling products and information on the internet and other online services.

**The EC framework:** EC **applications** (online shopping, auctions, banking, e-government and so on) rest on **infrastructure** (hardware, software, networks) and on **five support areas**:

1. **People** (buyers, sellers, intermediaries, IT staff)
2. **Public policy** (laws, regulation, taxation, privacy)
3. **Marketing and advertisement**
4. **Support services** (payments, logistics/delivery, security)
5. **Business partnerships** (joint ventures, partners along the supply chain)

**Explain more**

- **Trap:** "Competitors" is **not** a support area of EC. If asked which is the exception, pick competitors.
- **Trap:** when e-commerce and e-business appear together, e-business is the **wider** term. "An organisation selling direct to its customers online" or "buying from its suppliers online" describes only part of e-business. "Electronic communications for all business processes" is the full definition.
- When a question asks what is exchanged in EC and offers money, goods, services and "all of the above", choose **all of the above**. The same goes for sales, purchasing and services as examples of e-business.
- The internet and Web technologies together created a **global platform** where firms across the world compete for customers and gain access to **new markets**. A Lagos fashion brand can sell to buyers in Accra or London through its website and Instagram page.

### Lesson 10.2: Types of e-commerce organisations and transactions

**Must know**

| Type of organisation | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Brick-and-mortar** | Old-economy firm doing business only offline, through physical premises | A neighbourhood provision shop |
| **Click-and-mortar** (click-and-brick) | Keeps its physical locations but has added an **EC (electronic commerce)** component | A supermarket chain with shops plus an online store |
| **Click-only** (virtual, pure-play) | Does business entirely online; has **no physical storefronts** | Jiji, Jumia |

**Transaction types (by who trades with whom):**

1. **B2B (business-to-business):** transactions between businesses, e.g. a manufacturer buying raw materials from a supplier online. The largest type of EC by value.
2. **B2C (business-to-consumer):** a business selling to individual shoppers (e-tailing), e.g. Jumia and Konga selling phones and household goods.
3. **C2C (consumer-to-consumer):** individuals selling to individuals, e.g. a student listing a used laptop on **Jiji** and another person buying it.
4. **B2E (business-to-employee):** an organisation delivering information or services to its own employees, usually through its **intranet**, e.g. HR posting a Sales Department vacancy on the company intranet.
5. **C2B (consumer-to-business):** individuals sell products or services to firms, or name what they will pay and firms compete to supply.
6. **B2B2C:** a business sells to another business, which delivers to consumers.
7. **Intrabusiness EC:** EC activities inside one organisation (B2E is part of it).
8. **E-government:** government using EC to deal with citizens (G2C) and businesses (G2B), e.g. online tax filing or paying fees and levies online.
9. **Collaborative commerce (c-commerce):** business partners working together electronically, e.g. **Boeing designing a product together with its business partners**.
10. **Nonbusiness EC:** non-profit bodies (universities, religious groups, NGOs, government agencies) using EC to cut costs or improve service.
11. **M-commerce (mobile commerce):** EC carried out through **mobile** devices (smartphones, tablets) over wireless networks. The "m" means **mobile**.

**Explain more**

- **Trap:** click-only firms still have employees and supply chains, and many keep warehouses to store goods. What they lack is a **storefront** customers walk into.
- **Trap:** posting a job on the intranet is **B2E**, not B2B. "B2S" and "E2C" are not standard categories.
- Jiji's typical transaction is **C2C**: Jiji provides the website, but buyer and seller are both individuals. Jumia also runs a marketplace where independent sellers list goods, but its typical transaction (a shop selling to you) is classed as **B2C**.
- Nigeria is a mobile-first market, so m-commerce is huge here: ordering on a shopping app, buying airtime and data, and **mobile money** (sending, receiving and paying money from a phone through mobile money operators and payment apps).

### Lesson 10.3: EC business models and online auctions

**Must know**

- **Business model:** the method by which a firm generates revenue to sustain itself.

**Common EC business models:**

1. **Online direct marketing:** manufacturers or retailers selling straight to customers online, cutting out middlemen.
2. **Electronic tendering systems:** large buyers invite sellers to bid (reverse auctions; see below).
3. **Name-your-own-price:** the buyer states the price he is willing to pay and the system looks for a seller willing to accept it.
4. **Find-the-best-price:** the customer states a need and a search engine compares sellers to find the lowest price.
5. **Affiliate marketing:** a firm pays partners a commission for customers they refer from their own websites or pages.
6. **Viral marketing:** people pass a marketing message on to their friends and contacts (word of mouth online).
7. **Group purchasing:** pooling many small buyers' orders to get a volume discount.
8. **Online auctions.**
9. **Product and service customisation:** the buyer configures the product online to suit himself.
10. **Electronic marketplaces and exchanges:** online meeting places where many buyers and many sellers trade.

**Online auctions:**

| | Forward auction | Reverse auction |
|---|---|---|
| Who sells, who bids | **One seller**, many buyers bid | **One buyer**, many sellers bid |
| Price movement | Goes **up** | Goes **down** |
| Status | The **most common, traditional** form of auction | Also called **electronic tendering** |
| Typical user | A seller clearing goods to the highest bidder | Large private organisational buyers and **government agencies** making large-volume or large-value purchases |

**Explain more**

- **Trap:** "Reverse auction" and "electronic tendering system" mean the same thing. Online direct marketing, name-your-own-price and viral marketing are other models, not reverse auctions.
- A tendering system is buyer-led, so it can never be the answer for "one seller, many buyers". That is a **forward** auction.
- A ministry inviting online bids for a supply contract, lowest acceptable bid winning, is a reverse auction.

### Lesson 10.4: Benefits and limitations of e-commerce

**Must know**

**Benefits to organisations:** wider (national and global) market reach at low cost · lower costs of creating, processing and storing information · lower inventory and overheads · 24-hour trading · customisation of products · faster communication with customers and partners · new business models.

**Benefits to consumers:** shopping any time, anywhere · more choice of products and sellers · price comparison and often lower prices · quick delivery of digital products · participation in auctions · interaction with other consumers (reviews).

**Benefits to society:** people can work and shop from home, reducing travel and traffic · lower prices help the less well-off · access to services (education, health information, government services) in remote areas.

| Limitations | Examples |
|---|---|
| **Technological** | Poor or expensive internet access in some areas · limited bandwidth · weak security standards · difficulty integrating EC with existing systems |
| **Non-technological** | Fear of **fraud** and doubts about **security and privacy** · lack of **trust** in unseen sellers · customers wanting to touch and inspect goods · unclear legal issues · delivery (logistics) problems · not enough buyers and sellers for some markets |

**Explain more**

- Many Nigerian shoppers prefer to pay on delivery because they are wary of paying in advance to a seller they cannot see. That is a **trust** limitation, not a technological one.
- Personal data collected online is protected under the **Nigeria Data Protection Act 2023**, an example of public policy as a support area.
- Physical delivery in congested cities and to rural areas is a logistics limitation, solved partly by pick-up stations.

### Lesson 10.5: The communication process and channels

**Must know**

- **Communication:** the process of transferring **information and understanding** from one person (or group) to another. It has happened only when the receiver **understands** the message as the sender intended.

**The basic communication model:** **sender, the message, channel, receiver and feedback.** In full, the process runs:

1. **Sender** has an idea and **encodes** it (puts it into words, symbols or gestures).
2. **Message:** the encoded idea.
3. **Channel (medium):** the means used to carry it (face-to-face, phone, email, letter).
4. **Receiver** **decodes** (interprets) it.
5. **Feedback:** the receiver's response, which tells the sender whether the message was understood. It makes communication **two-way**.

- **Noise:** anything that distorts or interrupts the message at any stage (a loud generator, a bad network, jargon, distraction).

**Ways of receiving information:** **visual** (seeing), **auditory** (hearing) and **kinesthetic** (doing, touching, feeling).

**Channels of communication:**

1. **Oral (verbal):** face-to-face talk, meetings, phone calls. Fast, with instant feedback, but leaves no permanent record.
2. **Written:** letters, memos, reports, emails, notices. A permanent record, but slower and with delayed feedback.
3. **Non-verbal:** body language, facial expression, gestures, tone of voice, posture.
4. **Electronic/digital:** email, video calls, intranet, instant messaging, social media.

**Explain more**

- **Trap:** noise and barriers are **not** elements of the basic model; they are what disturb it. Any option listing "noise", "barriers", "data" or "para-verbal" as core elements is wrong.
- **Trap:** "shouting" is not a way of receiving information, and "auditory and visual only" leaves out **kinesthetic**.
- **Channel richness:** face-to-face is the **richest** channel (voice, body language and instant feedback together); a printed notice is among the leanest. Use rich channels for sensitive or complex messages, such as telling a staff member he is being transferred.

### Lesson 10.6: Formal and informal communication; barriers

**Must know**

- **Formal communication:** flows through the **official** lines of authority laid down by the organisation's structure.

**Directions of formal communication:**

1. **Downward:** superior to subordinate (instructions, policies, feedback on performance).
2. **Upward:** subordinate to superior (reports, suggestions, complaints).
3. **Horizontal (lateral):** between people at the **same level**, often across departments, for coordination.
4. **Diagonal (cross-wise):** between people at **different levels in different departments**, e.g. a sales officer contacting the finance manager directly.

- **Informal communication (the grapevine):** the unofficial network that grows from social relationships among staff and does not follow the chain of command. It is fast and can carry rumours.

**Uses of the grapevine:**

1. Acts as a **safety valve**: employees let off steam and express feelings.
2. Helps employees enjoy **friendly relationships** and builds group unity.
3. Helps the organisation **discover gaps**: problems and attitudes management did not know about.
4. Spreads information **quickly**.

**Barriers to effective communication:**

1. **Physical:** noise, distance, poor network or equipment.
2. **Semantic (language):** jargon, words with different meanings, poor translation.
3. **Perceptual:** people interpret the same message differently.
4. **Emotional/psychological:** anger, fear, distrust, prejudice.
5. **Cultural:** different customs, values and gestures.
6. **Organisational:** too many levels, rigid rules, status differences between boss and staff.
7. **Information overload:** more messages than the receiver can handle.
8. **Poor listening** and lack of feedback.

**Explain more**

- **Trap:** the grapevine is **informal**. Horizontal, diagonal, upward and downward are all **formal** directions. The question about a "safety valve" that "helps organisations discover gaps" always points to the grapevine.
- Drawbacks of the grapevine: rumours, distortion, half-truths and leaks. Managers cannot abolish it, so they should feed it accurate information early.
- **Overcoming barriers:** use simple language, choose the right channel, listen actively, seek feedback, be aware of cultural differences, keep messages concise and factual, and cut unnecessary levels.
- Good professional communication means: listen actively, give practical information, give facts rather than vague impressions, do not present opinions as facts, be concise, clarify expectations and responsibilities, and make persuasive, well-supported recommendations.

### One-minute recap

- E-commerce = online exchange of goods, services and money; it includes pre-purchase events and after-sale customer service.
- E-business = conducting business on the internet; electronic communications for **all** business processes (wider than e-commerce).
- Perspectives on EC: communications, business process, service, online.
- EC framework: infrastructure + 5 support areas (people, public policy, marketing and advertisement, support services, business partnerships). Competitors are not one.
- Click-only = no storefronts; click-and-mortar = physical shops plus an EC component.
- B2B, B2C, C2C (Jiji), B2E (intranet job posting), c-commerce (Boeing), m-commerce (m = mobile).
- Forward auction = one seller, many buyers (most common); reverse auction = electronic tendering, one buyer, many sellers.
- Communication model: sender, message, channel, receiver, feedback; noise disturbs it.
- Receiving information: visual, auditory, kinesthetic.
- Grapevine = informal; safety valve, friendly relationships, discovers gaps.
