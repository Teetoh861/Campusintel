## Topic 2: Understanding Basic Economics

**What you must know:** what an economy is and its levels; scarcity and the factors of production; the economic systems (market, command, mixed, with socialism and communism); demand, supply and equilibrium; the macro view (competition, business cycles, unemployment, inflation); government's role in a free market; the economic measures and monitors (GDP, GNP, CPI, PPI and others).

### Lesson 2.1: The economy and the factors of production

**Must know**

- **Economics:** the study of how a society uses scarce resources to produce and distribute goods and services.
- **Economy:** the system (framework) through which a society produces, distributes, exchanges and consumes goods and services. More fully: the management of national, regional or household resources in terms of production capacity, monetary system, distribution of resources, and the principles or ideology that govern how resources are shared. It sums up a society's economic activities as they affect individuals and businesses.
- **5 levels at which we speak of "an economy":**

1. **Nation:** e.g. the Nigerian, Ghanaian, US or Cuban economy.
2. **State or region:** e.g. the Lagos State economy, the Northern region economy.
3. **Industry:** e.g. the oil and gas economy, the agricultural economy of Nigeria.
4. **Household or family:** e.g. a family's income and spending.
5. **Institution:** e.g. the Unilag economy, the volume and size of all business activity around the campus.

- **Microeconomics:** the study of individual units: a consumer, a household, a firm, a single market (a firm's earnings, a worker's salary, a company's overheads).
- **Macroeconomics:** the study of the economy as a whole: total output (GDP), inflation, unemployment, growth.
- **4 traditional factors of production:**

1. **Land** (natural resources): land, water, minerals, forests, crude oil.
2. **Labour** (human resources): the physical and mental effort of people.
3. **Capital:** man-made aids to production: machines, tools, buildings, and the money used to buy them.
4. **Entrepreneurship:** the willingness to take risks to combine the other factors into a business.

**Explain more**

- **Scarcity** is the basic economic problem: unlimited wants, limited resources. Every choice is a **trade-off**; the best alternative given up is the **opportunity cost** (spend ₦20,000 on a phone accessory and the textbooks you could have bought are the opportunity cost).
- **Trap:** technology, information and knowledge improve how the factors work, and **knowledge** is sometimes added as a fifth factor. But when a question asks for the **four traditional** factors, the answer is land, labour, capital and entrepreneurship. "Technology", "money supply" or "government" are not among the four.
- **Trap:** "Economy" is not a tax mechanism, an advertising system or a staff-control tool. It is the whole framework for producing and exchanging goods and services.

### Lesson 2.2: Economic systems

**Must know**

- **Economic system:** the policies that define how a society distributes its resources to satisfy its people's needs. Every system must answer: **what** to produce, **how** to produce it, and **for whom**.
- **3 broad systems:** market, command (planned), mixed.

| Feature | Market (free-market, capitalism) | Command (planned) | Mixed |
|---|---|---|---|
| Who owns resources | Private individuals and firms | Government owns key businesses | Private, public and joint sectors co-exist |
| Who decides what to produce | Demand and supply (price mechanism) | Central government planning | Both market forces and government |
| Competition | Strong | Lacking | Present, but regulated |
| Consumer choice | Freedom of choice | Limited | Economic freedom with social welfare |
| Government role | Limited intervention | Controls wages, means of production, what and how to produce | Regulates, redistributes; regulatory bodies prominent |

- **Market economy characteristics:** competition, private ownership of resources, freedom of choice, price mechanism, limited government intervention, efficient allocation of resources (at the equilibrium point).
- **Command economy characteristics:** government ownership of key businesses, government intervention, government control of wages and means of production, centralised economic planning, lack of competition, limited consumer choice, government decides what and how to produce. Resources are allocated by **central government authority**.
- **Mixed economy characteristics:** social welfare, mixed ownership of property, economic freedom, co-existence of private, public and joint sectors, resources allocated by both government and market forces, prominent government-owned regulatory bodies, redistribution of resources to achieve economic and social goals. Also called a **welfarist state**.

**Explain more**

- **Capitalism** is the market system: private owners and the profit motive drive the economy. **Pure capitalism** (no government role at all) does not exist anywhere.
- Planned systems come in two forms:
    - **Communism:** the government owns and operates all productive resources; private property is largely eliminated. The state decides all production and prices. Cuba and North Korea are the usual examples.
    - **Socialism:** the government owns and runs **selected major industries** (e.g. power, transport, banking), while private businesses operate in the rest. It stresses social welfare and equal distribution.
- **Mixed economy** = market freedom **plus** state control. Nigeria is a mixed economy: private firms such as Dangote, MTN and the banks operate alongside government agencies and regulators such as the Central Bank of Nigeria (CBN), SEC and SON. Most countries today are mixed.
- **Privatisation** moves businesses from government to private ownership; **nationalisation** is the reverse. **Deregulation** removes or relaxes government rules on an industry (e.g. Nigeria's telecoms deregulation, which let private mobile operators in).
- **Trap:** a mixed economy combines market and command features. It is not a mix of "tradition and customs" (that is a **traditional economy**, based on custom), and it is not "all economic systems".
- **Trap:** state production quotas, absence of competition and uniform allocation describe a command economy, never a free market.

### Lesson 2.3: Demand, supply and equilibrium

**Must know**

- **Demand:** the quantity of a good or service buyers are willing and able to buy at various prices.
- **Law of demand:** other things being equal, as price **falls**, quantity demanded **rises**; as price rises, quantity demanded falls. Lower prices spur demand. The demand curve slopes **downward**.
- **Supply:** the quantity sellers are willing and able to offer for sale at various prices.
- **Law of supply:** other things being equal, as price **rises**, quantity supplied **rises**; rising prices lift supply. The supply curve slopes **upward**.
- **Equilibrium price (market price):** the price at which the demand curve and supply curve intersect, where quantity demanded equals quantity supplied. It is the optimal price for allocating resources or goods.
- **Relevance of demand and supply (price mechanism) in a market economy:**

1. It determines the distribution of goods and the allocation of resources.
2. It reduces, if not eliminates, government intervention in resource allocation in many sectors.
3. It determines the production of goods and their prices.
4. The equilibrium price stands as the optimal price to allocate resources or goods.

**Explain more**

- **Demand shifters:** income, prices of substitutes and complements, tastes, expectations, number of buyers. **Supply shifters:** input costs, technology, expectations, number of producers, prices of other goods the producer could make. Dearer diesel cuts the supply of bread at every price.
- A change in the good's **own price** moves you **along** the curve (change in quantity demanded or supplied). A change in any other factor **shifts** the curve.
- **Surplus:** price above equilibrium, so supply exceeds demand; sellers cut prices. **Shortage:** price below equilibrium, so demand exceeds supply; prices rise. Both push the market back to equilibrium.

### Lesson 2.4: The macro view: competition, business cycles, unemployment and inflation

**Must know**

- **Competition:** rivalry among businesses for the same customers. **4 market structures:**

| Structure | Number of sellers | Products | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Pure (perfect) competition** | Very many | Identical; no seller controls price | Farm produce such as maize or tomatoes in an open market |
| **Monopolistic competition** | Many | Slightly different (branding, quality) | Restaurants, fast food, hair salons, clothing |
| **Oligopoly** | Few large firms | Similar or differentiated; high entry barriers | Telecoms (MTN, Airtel, Glo, 9mobile), cement, airlines |
| **Monopoly** | One | No close substitute | Electricity transmission, water utilities |

- **Business cycle:** the rise and fall of economic activity over time. Phases: **expansion** (growth) → **peak** → **contraction** (recession) → **trough** → recovery.
- **Recession:** a period of falling economic activity; commonly, two consecutive quarters of falling GDP. **Depression:** a deep, prolonged recession.
- **Unemployment:** people actively seeking work who cannot find it. **4 types:**

1. **Frictional:** temporarily between jobs (e.g. a new graduate searching).
2. **Structural:** skills do not match available jobs, often from technology or industry change.
3. **Cyclical:** caused by a downturn in the business cycle.
4. **Seasonal:** tied to seasons (e.g. farm labour after harvest).

- **Inflation:** a steady, general rise in the prices of goods and services over time; it reduces the purchasing power of money. **Deflation:** a sustained fall in general prices. Inflation is a measure of the condition of the economy.

**Explain more**

- Monopolies often arise in utilities because building power or water networks is so costly that one supplier serving an area is more efficient. Electric utilities are the standard example; fast food, online retail and smartphones face many competitors.
- **Trap:** a **recession** is the contraction phase; the **trough** is its lowest point, not the start.

### Lesson 2.5: Government's role in a free-market system

**Must know**

**6 roles of government in a free-market system:**

1. **Provide the legal and social framework** and enforce the rules of fair play: protecting property rights, enforcing contracts, protecting consumers.
2. **Ensure economic stability** through different models and frameworks, including **monetary** decisions.
3. **Maintain competition** in the marketplace, with an **anti-trust** body to monitor abuse.
4. **Provide public goods and services**, e.g. postal system, water, education, health.
5. **Redistribute income** to cater for the weak and under-privileged and reduce the income gap.
6. **Deal with the international community and economic blocs** to determine trade and economic relationships.

- **Monetary policy:** the central bank's control of the **money supply and interest rates** to stabilise the economy. In Nigeria this is done by the **Central Bank of Nigeria (CBN)**.
- **Fiscal policy:** the government's use of **taxation and public spending** to influence the economy.
- **Taxes:** a major source of government revenue (income tax, companies income tax, value added tax (VAT), customs duties).

**Explain more**

- In a free market the government **sets and enforces the rules**; it does not fix all prices, direct all firms or cancel market forces (those describe a command economy).
- **Regulation** protects stakeholders. Nigerian examples: the **Federal Competition and Consumer Protection Commission (FCCPC)** fights unfair competition and protects consumers; **SON** (Standards Organisation of Nigeria) sets product standards; **NAFDAC** regulates food and drugs; **SEC** regulates the capital market; **CAC** (Corporate Affairs Commission) registers businesses under the Companies and Allied Matters Act (CAMA) 2020.
- **Monetary tools:** raising interest rates and reducing money supply to fight inflation (tight policy); lowering rates to boost borrowing and growth (loose policy).
- **Fiscal tools:** cutting taxes or raising spending to stimulate a slow economy; raising taxes or cutting spending to cool an overheating one.
- **Trap:** monetary = central bank, money and interest rates. Fiscal = government, taxes and spending. Exams swap them.

### Lesson 2.6: Economic measures and monitors

**Must know**

- **Gross Domestic Product (GDP):** the total value of all final goods and services produced **within a country's borders** in a given period (usually a year), whoever owns the producers. It is the main measure of **economic growth** and a key macroeconomic indicator.
- **Gross National Product (GNP):** the total value of goods and services produced by a country's **citizens and businesses**, wherever in the world they are located.
- **Per capita income:** national income divided by population; average income per person.
- **Consumer Price Index (CPI):** measures changes over time in the prices of a "basket" of goods and services typically bought by consumers; the main measure of **inflation**.
- **Producer Price Index (PPI):** measures changes over time in the prices producers receive for their goods (wholesale prices); it often signals future consumer price changes.
- **Unemployment rate:** the percentage of the labour force actively seeking work but without a job.

**Measures and monitors of an economy:**

1. GDP and per capita income.
2. Level of investment (e.g. foreign direct investment, FDI) and volume of international trade (export trade, and non-oil exports in Nigeria).
3. Inflation rate and CPI; consumption level and the purchasing power of the currency (e.g. the naira).
4. National employment figures and the unemployment level or rate.
5. Level of infrastructural development.
6. Non-economic metrics: life expectancy, poverty rate, literacy rate, political stability.

**Explain more**

- **GDP vs GNP:** profits made in Nigeria by a foreign-owned company count in Nigeria's **GDP** but in its home country's **GNP**. Income earned abroad by Nigerian firms counts in Nigeria's GNP, not its GDP.
- In Nigeria, GDP, CPI and unemployment figures are published by the **National Bureau of Statistics (NBS)**.
- **Trap:** GDP measures growth; CPI measures the price level (inflation); the unemployment rate measures joblessness; interest rates measure the cost of borrowing. Only GDP measures growth directly.
- **Trap:** a firm's quarterly earnings, a worker's salary or a company's overheads are **micro** figures, not macroeconomic indicators.

### One-minute recap

- Economy = framework for producing, distributing and exchanging goods and services; can be national, regional, industry, household or institutional.
- Scarcity forces trade-offs; the 4 traditional factors are land, labour, capital, entrepreneurship (not technology).
- Market = price mechanism and private ownership; command = central government allocates; mixed = market freedom plus state control.
- Communism: state owns all productive resources; socialism: state owns selected major industries.
- Law of demand: lower price, more demanded. Law of supply: higher price, more supplied. Equilibrium where the curves meet.
- Market structures: pure competition, monopolistic competition, oligopoly, monopoly (utilities).
- Business cycle: expansion, peak, contraction, trough. Unemployment: frictional, structural, cyclical, seasonal. Inflation = general rise in prices.
- Government in a free market enforces fair-play rules, keeps competition, provides public goods, redistributes income, stabilises via monetary (CBN) and fiscal (tax and spending) policy.
- GDP measures growth (within borders); GNP counts citizens' output anywhere; CPI tracks consumer prices; PPI tracks producer prices.
