## Topic 5: Forms of Business Ownership

**What you must know:** sole proprietorship, partnership (types of agreement and partner), companies (private vs public, memorandum and articles, corporation pros and cons), cooperatives, public enterprises and privatisation; corporate governance (principles, actors, board duties, three models); mergers and acquisitions, strategic alliances and joint ventures.

### Lesson 5.1: Forms of ownership and the sole proprietorship

**Must know**

- **Form of business ownership:** the category a business is registered under with government. It decides how much ownership and managerial control the owner has, how the business pays tax and how far the owner is personally liable if the business fails.
- **Liability:** **unlimited** = the owner's personal property (house, car, savings) can be taken to pay business debts, so he can lose everything; **limited** = owners lose at most what they invested.
- **Sole proprietorship:** a business in which a **single person owns, finances, manages and controls** all its activities, with all the authority, responsibility and risk. It is the **most common (numerous)** form of business.

**Features:** no separation of ownership and management · easy set-up (minimal formalities) · owner keeps all profit · unlimited liability · single ownership · profit taxed **once**, as the owner's personal income.

| Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|
| Independence and full control (the single most important advantage) | Unlimited liability |
| Quick decisions (no hierarchy) | Limited capital |
| Possible tax benefits (losses offset personal income) | Limited managerial expertise |
| Flexibility; easy to form and wind up | Uncertain continuity (depends on owner's life and health) |
| Owner keeps all profit | Not suitable for large-scale operations |

**Explain more**

- A sole proprietor **cannot issue or sell stock (shares)**; only companies can. "Profits taxed twice" is a **company** feature, never a sole proprietorship one.
- A sole trader who registers a business name with the **Corporate Affairs Commission (CAC)** still has **unlimited** liability: a business name is not a company.
- Examples: a provision shop, a tailor, a barber or a phone-repair stand on campus.

### Lesson 5.2: Partnerships

**Must know**

- **Partnership:** a business relation between **two or more persons** who join hands, finance and skills to form a business with the objective of a profitable return. Partners **divide the profits** among themselves, usually by their agreement.
- In a **general partnership** liability is **unlimited** and **each partner** can be held liable for the business's **entire** debt. Partners have **joint authority**.

**Features (5):** agreement between partners · shared ownership · sharing of profit (and, implicitly, loss) · mutual business (each partner is owner **and agent**; one partner's act binds all, the test of partnership) · unlimited liability.

| Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|
| Shared responsibility | Unlimited (full personal) liability in general partnerships |
| Complementary skills | Conflict between partners |
| Easier capital access than a sole trader | Shared profits can cause disputes |
| Profits taxed as personal income | Limited life span (withdrawal, death or bankruptcy of a partner) |
| Limited government regulation; low start-up costs | |

**Partnership deed:** the legal document founding the firm: names of partners, capital contributions, profit-sharing ratios, decision-making, admission and retirement of partners, dispute resolution, duration, commencement date.
**Partnership agreement (Articles of Partnership):** internal contract on management, ownership, investment, profits and losses.

**3 types:**
1. **General:** all partners share liabilities, profits and assets equally and can manage.
2. **Limited:** at least one **general partner** (unlimited liability, manages) plus **limited partners** (liability limited to their investment, no day-to-day role; they invest and take a profit share).
3. **Limited liability partnership (LLP):** works like a general partnership but protects each partner from **malpractice or negligence claims** arising from other partners; common in professions such as law and accounting.

**Types of partner:**
1. **Active:** manages; unlimited liability.
2. **Sleeping (dormant):** contributes capital and shares profit, does not manage; still unlimited liability.
3. **Secret:** connection hidden from outsiders; contributes capital, manages; unlimited liability.
4. **Nominal:** lends only name and reputation; no capital, no management, no profit share; liable to outsiders who lent believing he was a partner. Two kinds: by **estoppel** (he says or acts as if he is a partner) and by **holding out** (he does not deny it when others call him one).

**Explain more**

- **Sole proprietorship vs partnership:** both have low start-up costs and unlimited liability, and profits pass through to the owners' personal tax. One owner vs two or more; one keeps all profit vs shared profit; one decides vs shared decisions.
- Trap: a **sleeping** partner is not a **limited** partner. Only a limited partner's liability stops at his investment. Limited partnerships and LLPs are now recognised in Nigeria under the **Companies and Allied Matters Act (CAMA) 2020**.
- Elements of an agreement: business name and purpose, partners' details, capital contributed, ownership percentage, profit and loss distribution, management and voting, adding or removing partners.

### Lesson 5.3: Companies and corporations

**Must know**

- **Corporation (company):** a business recognised by the state as a **legal entity separate from its owners (shareholders)**. It can own property, sue and be sued in its own name. It is "an artificial being, invisible, intangible, existing only in contemplation of law". **Limited liability results from this separate legal existence.**
- **Ownership is by shareholders**; it is transferable by buying and selling shares.
- **Board of directors:** elected by shareholders to represent them, set the company's direction and select top executives. **Management** (CEO and officers) runs day-to-day operations.
- In Nigeria a company is registered with the CAC and needs **both a Memorandum and Articles of Association**.
  - **Memorandum:** regulates the company's **external** relationship (name, objects).
  - **Articles:** regulate **internal** relationships between the company and its members.

**Private vs public limited company**

| Private (Ltd, "unquoted") | Public (Plc, "quoted", joint stock company) |
|---|---|
| Articles restrict share transfer | Public may subscribe; shares freely transferable |
| 2 to 50 shareholders | Minimum 7, no maximum |
| Small, limited capital | Large capital |
| Accounts not published | Annual accounts must be published |
| Does not issue debentures to the public | Issues debentures |
| Cannot be quoted on the stock exchange | Can be quoted on the stock exchange |
| Name ends "Limited"/"Ltd" (e.g. AGRO Nigeria Limited) | Name ends "Plc" (e.g. GTBank Plc, Vitafoam Nigeria Plc) |

Owned by shareholders but **controlled by the board** (public).

**Corporation: advantages (6)**
1. Limited liability protection
2. Credibility and trust
3. Access to investment (issue shares; easier loans)
4. Tax benefits and incentives
5. **Perpetual life** (continues across generations of owners)
6. Easy ownership transfer

**Disadvantages (5)**
1. Administrative responsibilities (records, annual returns, company law)
2. Set-up and maintenance costs
3. Public disclosure requirements
4. Additional tax burden, including **double taxation**: profit is taxed as company income, then dividends are taxed again as shareholders' personal income
5. Lack of control for shareholders (ownership separated from management)

**Explain more**

- Managers' power is **not** complete: shareholders can vote out directors, and the board can remove managers.
- Plc means **public limited company**; "public liability company" is a wrong expansion. Nigeria also allows companies limited by **guarantee** (often non-profits) and **unlimited** companies. Under CAMA 2020, a **single person** may now form a private company; for this course, keep 2 to 50 as the private range and a minimum of 7 for a public company.
- **Limited liability company (LLC)** in the American sense is a hybrid: members get **limited liability** plus **flexibility in management and tax treatment** (taxed like a sole proprietorship, partnership or corporation; pass-through avoids double taxation). Drawbacks: more paperwork than a partnership, differing state rules, harder to attract investors, uncertain continuity when members change. An LLC can have one or many members and is not identical to a corporation. In Nigerian usage "limited liability company" usually just means a private limited company.
- Other company words: **parent/holding company** controls a **subsidiary**; a **mutual company** is owned by its customers or policyholders (a rare, specialised form).

### Lesson 5.4: Cooperatives, public enterprises and privatisation

**Must know**

- **Cooperative society:** a voluntary association of persons united by a common bond who venture into business **with the sole purpose of providing service to members in the most efficient and affordable manner**. It is a registered body corporate (owns property, contracts, sues).
- **Features:** owned and democratically run by members · voluntary membership · **one member, one vote** · profits shared among members or reinvested · separate legal identity.
- **5 types:** producer, consumer, credit unions (thrift and credit), marketing, housing.

| Advantages (7) | Disadvantages (6) |
|---|---|
| Shared benefits | Slow, conflict-prone decisions |
| Democratic structure | Limited capital |
| Risk sharing | Limited growth |
| Stability | Inequality in contribution |
| Economy in operations (no middlemen) | Inefficient management (unpaid, inexperienced) |
| Government support (cheap loans, subsidies, lower tax) | Excessive government control |
| Social utility (stops concentration of economic power) | |

- **Public enterprise:** a business **owned (wholly or mainly) and controlled by government** to provide essential services in the public interest. Also called **government parastatals, public corporations, state-owned enterprises** and government-owned companies.
- **Objectives:** provide essential services (power, water, transport), control strategic sectors, prevent private monopoly exploitation, create jobs, earn revenue, develop neglected areas.
- **2 sources of finance:** **internal** (revenue from operations, retained surplus) and **external** (government grants and subventions, loans, bonds).
- **Privatisation:** transfer of ownership of a public enterprise, **partly or wholly**, to private investors. It may be **partial or full**.
- **Commercialisation:** government keeps ownership but the enterprise must run on commercial, profit-making lines (also partial or full).

**Explain more**

- Trap: "government sectors" is **not** a name for public enterprises.
- Nigerian public enterprises have included the Nigerian Ports Authority and the Nigerian Railway Corporation. Problems: political interference, overstaffing, inefficiency, corruption and dependence on subsidies, which led to privatisation.
- Nigeria's programme is run by the **Bureau of Public Enterprises (BPE)** under the **National Council on Privatisation**, guided by the Public Enterprises (Privatisation and Commercialisation) Act 1999. The best-known example is the 2013 sale of the power generation and distribution companies created from PHCN.
- **For privatisation:** efficiency, less burden on the budget, revenue for government, new investment, wider share ownership. **Against:** job losses, higher prices, private monopoly, foreign domination.

### Lesson 5.5: Corporate governance

**Must know**

- **Corporate governance:** the system of rules, practices and processes by which a company is **directed and controlled**, ensuring accountability to stakeholders, transparency and ethical behaviour (oversight and management systems). The **board of directors** is the primary force in it.
- **5 principles:** accountability, transparency, fairness, responsibility, risk management.
- **4 key actors:** board of directors, management, shareholders, audit committee (within the board; checks financial reports, compliance and audits).
- **Stakeholders considered (7):** shareholders, management, board, employees, customers, suppliers, community.
- **6 board responsibilities:** set corporate strategy · oversee management (appoint, evaluate, remove the CEO) · ensure compliance · risk management · financial reporting (with the audit committee) · succession planning.

**3 governance models**

| Model | Who controls | Key marks |
|---|---|---|
| **Anglo-American** | Board and shareholders | Shareholder Model is principal (others: Stewardship, Political). Management maximises shareholder interest; insiders plus independent directors; chairperson and CEO ideally different people; big decisions put to shareholder vote |
| **Continental** | **Two-tier:** supervisory board (outsiders: shareholders, unions, banks) and management board (insiders) | Boards entirely separate; supervisory board size set by national law; companies align with government objectives; values stakeholders |
| **Japanese** | Banks, affiliated entities (**keiretsu**), management, government | Board mostly insiders; keiretsu can remove directors if profits fall; small shareholders have no voice; low transparency |

**Explain more**

- **Benefits of good governance:** trust, clear direction, long-term viability, investor confidence and capital raising, rising share prices, better reputation and customer retention, less loss, waste, risk and corruption. Bad governance does the opposite and can collapse profitability.
- **Assessing governance (9 areas):** disclosure practices, executive pay structure, risk management, conflict-of-interest policies, board members, contractual and social obligations, vendor relationships, complaints, audits.
- Institutional investors (pension and mutual funds) are especially influential shareholders. In Nigeria the **Nigerian Code of Corporate Governance 2018** is issued by the Financial Reporting Council of Nigeria, and the **Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC)** regulates public companies.
- Trap: the board does **not** run day-to-day operations; management does.

### Lesson 5.6: Mergers, acquisitions, strategic alliances and joint ventures

**Must know**

| Merger | Acquisition |
|---|---|
| Two or more firms **unite into one** new entity | One firm buys part or all of another |
| Mutual; usually equals | Larger, stronger firm buys a smaller one; need not be mutual |
| New shares issued to both sets of shareholders; usually no cash | No new shares; buyer needs at least **51%** for control |
| New name and joint management usually | Target operates under the buyer's name |

- **3 types:** **horizontal** (competitors at the same stage in the same industry) · **vertical** (firms at different stages of one supply chain, e.g. a manufacturer and its supplier) · **conglomerate** (unrelated industries).
- **Acquisition forms:** **stock sale** (buy the whole entity, assets and liabilities) and **asset sale** (buy particular assets). Selling a whole segment = **divestiture (carve-out)**.
- **3 reasons for acquisition:** expansion (Apple buying Beats, 2014) · defensive play (Facebook buying WhatsApp, 2014) · capability acquisition (technology or talent).
- **Friendly** acquisition: target's board and shareholders approve. **Hostile takeover:** one company buys a **controlling interest in the voting stock** of another **against the wishes of its management**.
- **Strategic alliance:** a cooperative, often **long-term** agreement between separate businesses to share resources for mutually beneficial projects (production, R&D, marketing, distribution) while **each keeps its independence**. Main advantage: **less risk and work than permanently integrating two companies**.
- **4 Cs:** complementarities, congruence of goals, compatibility of organisations, change.
- **3 types:** joint venture, **equity** alliance (one buys an equity stake in the other, e.g. 40%), **non-equity** alliance (each brings core competencies; **most common**).
- **Joint venture (JV):** two or more parents create a **new, separate child company** for a specific goal or project. **50-50 JV** or **majority-owned venture** (e.g. 70/30). Types: **equity** and **contractual**. Example: Sony Ericsson.

**Explain more**

- **Leveraged buyout:** buying a company mostly with borrowed money. Not the same as a hostile takeover.
- Merger motives: market share, new markets, lower operating costs, more revenue, wider margins. In Nigeria, Access Bank's 2019 merger with Diamond Bank grew its customer base. Oil joint ventures between NNPC and international oil companies are well-known JVs.
- **Product life cycles and alliances:** slow cycle (medicine: access restricted markets, keep stability), standard cycle (pool resources, scale, market share), fast cycle (software: speed innovation, share R&D cost).
- **How alliances create value (5):** short-term finances, removing entry barriers, business insight, shared financial risk, innovating beyond current capabilities.
- **7 steps to a small-business alliance:** put your house in order, set goals, identify partners, due diligence, get to know each other, make it win-win, keep clear communication.
- **Alliance advantages:** shared resources and expertise, innovation, competitive advantage, new-market penetration, economies of scale, revenue growth, risk mitigation, brand awareness. **Cons:** more coordination work, one side may gain more, conflict over strategy, possible damage to image.
- **JV steps (7):** purpose and goals → structure and ownership → resources → governance (board, management) → finances and profit sharing → risks and exit → monitoring. **Benefits:** new markets, shared costs and risks, combined expertise. **Challenges:** conflict of interest, cultural difference, shared control.
- Traps: alliance = cooperative pact, **not** a permanent consolidation; JV = a new entity for a defined purpose, often limited in time; merger = one entity; acquisition = controlling interest.

### One-minute recap

- Sole proprietorship: one owner, full control, independence, unlimited liability, most common, cannot sell shares.
- Partnership: 2+ owners, shared profits, joint authority, unlimited liability (general); general, limited, LLP; active, sleeping, secret, nominal partners.
- Company: separate legal entity, shareholders, limited liability, perpetual life, double taxation; memorandum (external) and articles (internal).
- Private Ltd 2-50, no public shares; Plc 7+, quoted, publishes accounts.
- Cooperative: serves members, one member one vote; 5 types.
- Public enterprises (parastatals, public corporations, SOEs) finance internally and externally; privatisation is partial or full.
- Governance: 5 principles, 4 actors, 6 board duties; Anglo-American, Continental (two-tier), Japanese (keiretsu).
- M&A: horizontal, vertical, conglomerate; hostile takeover against management's wishes; alliance keeps independence; JV creates a new child company.

### Theory practice

**Question:** Distinguish between a private limited company and a public limited company, and state two advantages and two disadvantages of the corporate form.

*Answer outline:* Define a company as a separate legal entity owned by shareholders. Give four differences: membership (2-50 vs 7+), share transfer and public subscription, publication of accounts, stock-exchange quotation (or debentures, Ltd vs Plc). Advantages: limited liability, perpetual life, access to capital. Disadvantages: double taxation, cost and disclosure requirements.
