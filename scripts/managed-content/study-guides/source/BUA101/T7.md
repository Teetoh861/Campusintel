## Topic 7: Management Roles, Functions and Skills

**What you must know:** what management is and the manager's main job; the three groups of managerial roles; the three levels of management; the four functions (planning with vision, mission, goals, objectives and SWOT; organising with structure and teams; leading with leadership styles; controlling); and the four essential skills (technical, interpersonal, conceptual, decision-making).

### Lesson 7.1: What managers do: roles and levels

**Must know**

- **Management:** the process of planning, organising, leading and controlling an organisation's resources (people, money, materials, information) to reach its goals effectively and efficiently.
- **Primary role of a manager:** to **drive organisational objectives**, that is, to get the organisation's goals achieved through the work of others. It is not doing routine technical work, chasing promotion or avoiding choices.
- **Efficiency** = doing things right (least waste of resources). **Effectiveness** = doing the right things (reaching the goal).

**The three groups of managerial roles (10 roles in all):**

| Group | Meaning | Roles inside it |
|---|---|---|
| **Interpersonal** (relationship-building) | dealing with people inside and outside the firm | figurehead, **leader**, liaison |
| **Informational** (information-sharing) | receiving, gathering and passing on information | monitor, disseminator, **spokesperson** |
| **Decisional** (decision-making) | making choices and solving problems | entrepreneur, **disturbance handler**, resource allocator, negotiator |

**The three levels of management (the management pyramid):**

1. **Top managers:** the highest level (CEO, managing director, chief financial officer). Set long-term direction and strategic plans, and represent the firm to outsiders.
2. **Middle managers:** heads of departments, divisions or plants. Turn top management's strategy into departmental plans and link top and first-line managers.
3. **First-line (supervisory) managers:** supervisors, team leaders, shift managers. Oversee the people who do the work and handle day-to-day operations.

**Explain more**

- **Interpersonal:** the **figurehead** performs ceremonial duties (cutting the tape at a new branch); the **leader** guides and motivates staff; the **liaison** builds networks with people outside his unit.
- **Informational:** the **monitor** scans for information (reading business news, market reports); the **disseminator** passes information to staff; the **spokesperson** speaks for the organisation to outsiders such as the press, regulators or investors. When MTN Nigeria's CEO addresses journalists about a policy, he is a spokesperson, an **informational** role.
- **Decisional:** the **entrepreneur** starts improvements and new projects; the **disturbance handler** resolves conflicts and crises, acting as a **mediator**; the **resource allocator** decides who gets money, people and time; the **negotiator** bargains with unions, suppliers or partners.
- **Trap:** "spokesperson" sounds like talking to people, so students pick interpersonal. It is **informational**. Resolving a conflict is decisional (disturbance handler), not interpersonal. "Skill-based role" or "technical role" is not one of the groups.
- All managers perform all roles, but the mix changes with level: top managers spend more time as figurehead, spokesperson and negotiator; the leader role matters most for first-line managers on the shop floor.

### Lesson 7.2: The planning function

**Must know**

- **Planning:** the function of **defining objectives (goals) and the strategies and tactics** to reach them. It is the first function; the others depend on it.
- **Vision statement:** a brief, inspiring statement of what the organisation wants to become in the future.
- **Mission statement:** a statement of the organisation's purpose: what it does, whom it serves and its basic values.
- **Goal:** a broad, long-range target. **Objective:** a specific, measurable, shorter-range target that helps reach a goal.
- **SWOT analysis:** **Strengths, Weaknesses, Opportunities and Threats**. Strengths and weaknesses are **internal**; opportunities and threats are **external**.

**Types of plans:**

| Plan | Made by | Time frame | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Strategic** | top managers | long term (several years) | enter West African markets under ECOWAS |
| **Tactical** | middle managers | medium term | a region's sales plan for the year |
| **Operational** | first-line managers | short term (days, weeks, months) | this week's production schedule |

**Explain more**

- **Strategic planning steps:** (1) define the mission, vision and values; (2) assess the organisation's strengths, weaknesses, opportunities and threats (SWOT); (3) develop forecasts; (4) analyse the competition; (5) establish goals and objectives; (6) develop action plans (strategies and tactics) to reach them.
- **SWOT example (a Nigerian cement maker):** strength = large plant and strong brand; weakness = high diesel and power costs; opportunity = growing housing demand; threat = cheap imports or a sharp fall in the naira. Internal factors are within the firm's control; external ones are not.
- Good objectives are **SMART**: Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Relevant, Time-bound. "Increase sales" is a goal; "increase sales in Lagos by 10% by December" is an objective.
- **Crisis management (contingency planning):** planning ahead for emergencies such as fire, product recalls or system failure, so the firm can respond quickly and protect people and its reputation.
- **Trap:** budget goal-setting and fiscal forecasting are **planning**, not organising or controlling. Tracking staff output is **controlling**; recruiting is **staffing/HR**; mediating disputes is **leading**.

### Lesson 7.3: The organising function: structure and teams

**Must know**

- **Organising:** arranging resources, **assigning tasks, duties and roles**, grouping people into departments and **delegating** authority so that plans can be carried out.
- **Organisation structure:** the framework that defines roles, **who does what and who reports to whom**. An effective structure gives **clearly defined duties and roles**, smooth communication and good output.
- **Chain of command:** the line of authority from the top to the bottom. **Span of control:** the number of people who report directly to one manager. **Delegation:** giving a subordinate authority and responsibility for a task.

| Tall (hierarchical) structure | Flat structure |
|---|---|
| many layers (tiers) of management | **few (minimal)** layers of management |
| narrow span of control | wide span of control |
| slower decisions and communication | faster decisions, more staff freedom |

- Other structures: **functional** (departments by function: marketing, finance, HR), **divisional** (by product or region), **matrix** (staff report to both a functional manager and a project manager).
- **Unstructured (flexible) organisation:** few formal rules, loose roles, **adaptability and fluidity**; common in tech start-ups. Managing it requires **flexibility and ingenuity (creativity)**, not tight oversight.
- **Team:** a group of people working together toward a **common goal**, sharing responsibility for the result.

**Benefits of organising in teams:** higher output (enhanced productivity) · quicker problem-solving · more ideas and creativity · better coordination · greater commitment and morale.

**What makes teams productive (4):** clear (precise) goals · the tools and resources needed · open and clear communication · trust and unity among members.

**Explain more**

- **Types of teams:** **functional** (people from one department), **cross-functional** (people from different departments, such as product launch teams), **problem-solving** (formed to fix a specific issue), **self-managed** (manage themselves with little supervision), **virtual** (members in different locations linked by technology, e.g. on Zoom or WhatsApp).
- **Trap:** a flat structure still has authority levels; it simply has fewer of them. "No authority levels" is wrong.
- **Trap:** fixed chain of command and rigid rules describe a **highly structured** firm, not an unstructured one.
- Team problems such as distrust, unclear targets, persistent disputes, slow decisions and stifled ideas are the result of **poorly managed** teams, never benefits. To foster teamwork, a manager builds trust, keeps everyone focused on shared objectives and invites every member's input; setting members against each other destroys it.

### Lesson 7.4: The leading function

**Must know**

- **Leading:** guiding, **motivating and inspiring** people (raising team morale) so they work willingly toward the organisation's goals. It includes communicating, directing, coaching and resolving conflicts.
- **Leadership:** the ability to influence others to achieve goals. Managers plan and control; leaders inspire and set direction. Good managers do both.

**Leadership styles:**

1. **Autocratic:** the leader makes decisions alone and expects obedience. Fast; useful in emergencies; can lower morale.
2. **Democratic (participative):** the leader involves staff in decisions; final decision often by the leader or the group. Builds commitment; slower.
3. **Laissez-faire (free-rein):** the leader gives broad goals and leaves employees to decide how to work. Suits skilled, self-motivated staff.
4. **Situational (contingency) leadership:** no single best style; the leader adapts to the people, the task and the situation.

**Explain more**

- **Coaching** is helping employees build skills and confidence through guidance and feedback. **Mentoring** is an experienced manager or employee guiding a less experienced colleague's career.
- Leaders also **manage change** (helping staff accept new systems) and build **organisational culture**: the shared values, beliefs and norms of the firm.
- An autocratic style may suit a factory fire drill; a laissez-faire style suits a team of experienced software engineers.
- **Trap:** output oversight is controlling, not leading; fiscal forecasting is planning; product development is an operational activity, not a management function.

### Lesson 7.5: The controlling function

**Must know**

- **Controlling:** **monitoring performance, comparing it with targets and taking corrective action** so that goals are met. It is the last function and feeds back into planning.

**The control cycle (4 steps):**

1. **Set standards** (targets drawn from the plan's objectives).
2. **Measure** actual performance.
3. **Compare** performance with the standards.
4. **Take corrective action** where there is a gap (or reward good results).

**Explain more**

- Example: a bank branch sets a target of opening 500 new accounts in a quarter. At mid-quarter it has opened 150, so the manager investigates and adds staff to the account-opening desk. That is controlling.
- **Balanced scorecard:** a control tool that measures performance from four perspectives (financial, customer, internal business processes, and learning and growth), not money alone.
- Quality checks, budgets compared with actual spending, and performance reviews are all control tools. Performance reviews belong to **controlling**, not organising.
- **Trap:** "finances are overlooked" is the opposite of control. Onboarding staff is staffing (HR); advertising goods is marketing.

### Lesson 7.6: Essential management skills

**Must know**

1. **Technical skills:** the knowledge and ability to perform the specific tasks of a job (hands-on expertise), such as accounting, coding or operating machinery. Most needed by **first-line** managers.
2. **Interpersonal (human) skills:** the ability to communicate with, understand, motivate and work with people (teamwork aptitude). Includes **communication**. Needed at **all levels**.
3. **Conceptual skills:** the ability to see the organisation as a whole, think **strategically** and understand how its parts relate (big-picture or visionary capability). Most needed by **top** managers.
4. **Decision-making skills:** the ability to define problems, weigh options and choose the best course of action (problem-solving, issue resolution).

**Steps in decision-making:**

1. Recognise and define the problem or opportunity.
2. Identify and develop options.
3. Analyse the options.
4. Select the best option.
5. Implement the decision.
6. Monitor the results and adjust.

**Explain more**

- **Dialogue (communication) and issue resolution (problem-solving)** are the essential management skills an exam expects. Dismissing input, shunning collaboration and a profit-only focus are weaknesses.
- As a manager rises, technical skill matters less and conceptual skill matters more; interpersonal skill stays important throughout.
- **Programmed decisions** are routine and follow set rules (reordering stock when it falls to a set level). **Non-programmed decisions** are new, one-off and complex (entering a new country).
- **Time management** (prioritising and delegating) supports all four skills.
- **Trap:** strategic thinking is **conceptual** skill, not technical (hands-on) or human (teamwork) skill.

### One-minute recap

- A manager's primary role is to drive organisational objectives through planning, organising, leading and controlling.
- 10 roles in 3 groups: interpersonal (figurehead, leader, liaison), informational (monitor, disseminator, spokesperson), decisional (entrepreneur, disturbance handler, resource allocator, negotiator).
- Spokesperson = informational; resolving conflict = disturbance handler (mediator), decisional.
- Levels: top (strategic), middle (tactical), first-line (operational).
- Planning = objectives and tactics; vision, mission, goals, objectives; SWOT = Strengths, Weaknesses, Opportunities, Threats.
- Organising = assigning tasks and roles; flat structure = few management tiers; unstructured firm = adaptable and fluid, managed with flexibility and ingenuity.
- Teams raise output and fix issues faster; they need clear goals, tools, open communication and trust.
- Leading = inspiring and motivating; styles: autocratic, democratic, laissez-faire, situational.
- Controlling = set standards, measure, compare, correct.
- Skills: technical, interpersonal, conceptual (strategic thinking), decision-making.
