## Topic 9: Employee Motivation

**What you must know:** what motivation is and why it matters; what motivates employees (intrinsic vs extrinsic, financial vs non-financial); classical and human relations views (Taylor, Hawthorne); McGregor's Theory X and Y and Ouchi's Theory Z; Maslow's hierarchy and Herzberg's two-factor theory; equity, expectancy and goal-setting theories; redesigning jobs (enrichment, enlargement, rotation); reinforcing high-performance behaviour; motivational strategies.

### Lesson 9.1: What motivation is and why it matters

**Must know**

- **Motivation:** a process of stimulating people to accomplish desired goals. In business, it is what makes employees put effort, energy and care into their work and keep at it.
- **Importance of motivation (3 core points):**
  1. Improves employee involvement.
  2. Promotes job satisfaction.
  3. Promotes interpersonal cooperation.
- Motivation also **enhances job performance** and productivity, encourages creativity and **reduces staff turnover** (people leaving).

**Factors that motivate employees to improve performance:**
1. Meaningful work
2. Clear goals and expectations
3. Regular feedback, recognition and appreciation
4. Opportunities for growth and development
5. A positive work environment and strong leadership
6. A sense of belonging
7. Alignment with company values (feeling valued, challenged and empowered)
8. Autonomy (freedom to decide how to do the work)

| Intrinsic motivation | Extrinsic motivation |
|---|---|
| Comes from **inside** the person | Comes from **outside** the person |
| Satisfaction from the work itself: personal growth, interest, achievement | Rewards given by others: pay, bonuses, praise, promotion |

| Financial motivators | Non-financial motivators |
|---|---|
| Salary, wages, bonuses, overtime pay, commission, profit sharing | Recognition, praise, **promotion** (status and responsibility), training, job security, flexible hours, a pleasant work environment |

- **Employee choices:** the decisions employees make about their jobs and the benefits they receive, such as the design of welfare programmes, the benefits they take up and the development opportunities they pursue. Each choice is driven by intrinsic or extrinsic motivation.

**Explain more**

- Low motivation shows up as lateness, absenteeism, poor-quality work, low output and people resigning. Lower productivity, higher turnover and less creativity are **results of poor motivation**, never reasons why motivation is important.
- **Trap:** a bonus, salary and overtime pay are all money, so they are financial. A promotion is classed as **non-financial** because its key value is higher status, more responsibility and growth, even though it often comes with a pay rise.
- A bank officer in Lagos who stays late because she enjoys solving clients' problems is intrinsically motivated; one who stays only for overtime pay is extrinsically motivated. Most people are driven by a mix of both.

### Lesson 9.2: Early views: Taylor, Hawthorne, McGregor and Ouchi

**Must know**

- **Frederick W. Taylor (scientific management, early 1900s):** studied work scientifically (time-and-motion studies) to find the "one best way" to do each task. He believed workers are motivated mainly by **money**, so he used **piece-rate pay** (paying per unit produced).
- **The Hawthorne studies (human relations movement):** carried out from the 1920s at Western Electric's Hawthorne plant near Chicago; **Elton Mayo** is the name linked with them. Productivity rose whatever the researchers changed (for example, lighting), because workers felt **noticed and valued** and belonged to a group.
- **Hawthorne effect:** people change (usually improve) their behaviour when they know they are being observed or given attention.
- **Douglas McGregor (1960)** described two sets of managerial assumptions:

| Theory X | Theory Y |
|---|---|
| Workers dislike work and avoid it | Work is as natural as play or rest |
| Must be controlled, directed, threatened | Workers exercise self-direction and self-control |
| Avoid responsibility, have little ambition | Accept and seek responsibility |
| Want security above all | Are creative and committed to goals they value |
| Leads to an **authoritarian**, close-control style | Leads to a **participative**, trusting style |

- **William Ouchi's Theory Z (1981):** blends Japanese and American practices: long-term employment, **collective (consensus) decision-making**, individual responsibility, slow evaluation and promotion, and concern for the **whole person** and their family.

**Explain more**

- The shift in thinking: Taylor treated workers as "economic beings" moved by pay; Hawthorne showed that **social and psychological factors** (attention, group belonging, morale) also drive output. This gave birth to the human relations approach.
- **Trap:** Theory X and Y are assumptions **managers** hold about workers, not two types of worker. A manager who clocks staff in and out and threatens query letters for every mistake is acting on Theory X; one who sets goals and lets the team decide how to meet them is acting on Theory Y.
- Theory Z is by **Ouchi**, not McGregor.

### Lesson 9.3: Needs theories: Maslow and Herzberg

**Must know**

**Abraham Maslow's hierarchy of needs** (from the base upwards, 5 levels):
1. **Physiological:** survival essentials: food, water, shelter, sleep. The **most basic** need. At work: a wage that pays for them.
2. **Safety (security):** protection from harm, stable income. At work: job security, safe conditions, pension, health insurance.
3. **Social (belongingness, love):** friendship, acceptance, being part of a group. At work: teamwork, friendly colleagues.
4. **Esteem:** recognition, respect, status, self-respect. At work: titles, praise, awards, promotion.
5. **Self-actualisation:** becoming all one is capable of; personal fulfilment. The **highest** need. At work: challenging, creative work and growth.

- Lower needs must be largely met before higher needs motivate. A **satisfied need no longer motivates**.
- Physiological and safety = lower-order needs; social, esteem and self-actualisation = higher-order needs.

**Frederick Herzberg's two-factor theory** (also called motivation-hygiene theory):

| Motivators (satisfiers) | Hygiene factors (maintenance factors) |
|---|---|
| Linked to the **content** of the job | Linked to the **context** (surroundings) of the job |
| Achievement, recognition, the work itself, responsibility, advancement, growth | Salary, company policy, supervision, working conditions, relationships with colleagues, job security, status |
| Present: **motivate** and satisfy | Absent: cause **dissatisfaction**. Present: only prevent dissatisfaction, they do **not** motivate |

**Explain more**

- Herzberg's key point: the opposite of satisfaction is **no satisfaction**, and the opposite of dissatisfaction is **no dissatisfaction**. Raising pay may stop complaints, but lasting motivation comes from motivators such as responsibility and recognition.
- **Trap:** the two factors are "motivation and maintenance (hygiene)", never "financial and relational" or "intrinsic and short-term". Salary is a **hygiene** factor in Herzberg; recognition is a **motivator**.
- Linking the two: Herzberg's hygiene factors roughly match Maslow's lower needs; his motivators match the higher needs (esteem, self-actualisation).
- **Other needs-based ideas** you may meet: **David McClelland** (needs for achievement, affiliation and power) and **Lawrence and Nohria's four drives** (to acquire, to bond, to comprehend or learn, and to defend).

### Lesson 9.4: Process theories: equity, expectancy and goal-setting

**Must know**

- **Equity theory (J. Stacy Adams):** employees compare the ratio of their **inputs** (effort, skill, time, qualifications) to their **outcomes** (pay, recognition, promotion) with that of other people (a **referent**). They act based on whether the rewards seem **fair**.
- If they feel under-rewarded they may: reduce effort, ask for a raise, cut quality, change who they compare with, or quit.
- **Expectancy theory (Victor Vroom):** people are motivated when they believe effort will lead to good performance, performance will bring a reward, and the reward is one they value. Three links:
  1. **Expectancy:** effort → performance ("If I try, can I do it?")
  2. **Instrumentality:** performance → reward ("If I do it, will I be rewarded?")
  3. **Valence:** value of the reward ("Do I want that reward?")
- If any link is zero, motivation collapses (Motivation = Expectancy × Instrumentality × Valence).
- **Goal-setting theory (Edwin Locke):** **specific, challenging but attainable** goals produce higher performance than vague or easy goals ("do your best"), especially with **feedback** and when employees **accept** the goals.
- **Motivating with challenging goals:** setting goals that push people outside their comfort zone, so overcoming the difficulty brings a sense of accomplishment and personal growth, which raises motivation and engagement.
- **SMART goals:** **S**pecific, **M**easurable, **A**chievable, **R**elevant, **T**ime-bound.

**Explain more**

- **Management by objectives (MBO):** manager and employee agree goals together, the employee works towards them, and progress is reviewed and rewarded. Participation increases goal acceptance.
- A good challenging goal is **precise and trackable**: "raise sales in Ikeja by 10% by December" beats "sell more". Goals that are **beyond realistic reach** discourage people; vague, simple targets or lowered expectations fail to stretch them; goals must stay **linked to results**.
- Equity is about **perceived** fairness. If two graduates join an MTN office on the same grade and one learns the other earns ₦50,000 more, the first may feel inequity even if her own pay is good.
- Equity and expectancy are called **process** theories because they explain **how** people choose their behaviour; Maslow and Herzberg are **content (needs)** theories because they explain **what** motivates.

### Lesson 9.5: Redesigning jobs and reinforcing high-performance behaviour

**Must know**

- **Job redesign:** a strategic process of reshaping jobs to fit business needs and the employee's skills and interests, so performance and satisfaction rise and roles stay relevant and engaging.
- **3 common types:**
  1. **Job enrichment:** adds more tasks **and gives more authority** (responsibility, control, decision-making) to the job holder. Vertical expansion.
  2. **Job enlargement:** adds **more tasks at the same level** of difficulty and responsibility. Horizontal expansion; widens the range of tasks.
  3. **Job rotation:** moves employees **from one job to another** at intervals, giving variety and new skills (cross-training).
- **Benefits of job redesign:** more control over work and more meaningful tasks; reduced stress; improved job satisfaction; reduced turnover.
- **Job characteristics model (Hackman and Oldham), 5 core dimensions:** skill variety, task identity, task significance, autonomy, feedback.
- **High-performance behaviours:** actions and attitudes that consistently lead to exceptional results and go beyond the job requirements: excellence, initiative, commitment.
- **Reinforcement theory:** behaviour that is followed by a pleasant consequence tends to be **repeated**; behaviour followed by an unpleasant consequence or no consequence tends to fade.

| Method | What happens | Effect on behaviour |
|---|---|---|
| **Positive reinforcement** | A reward is given (praise, bonus, extra days off) | Increases it |
| **Negative reinforcement** | Something unpleasant is removed when the right behaviour occurs | Increases it |
| **Punishment** | Something unpleasant is applied (query, fine) | Decreases it |
| **Extinction** | No response at all; behaviour is ignored | Decreases it |

**Explain more**

- **Trap:** enrichment = **deeper** job (more authority); enlargement = **wider** job (more tasks, same level); rotation = **different** jobs in turn. Shrinking duties, narrowing tasks or limiting control is the opposite of good redesign.
- Example: a cashier at a supermarket who now also orders stock and handles customer complaints with power to issue refunds has an **enriched** job. One who also packs bags and arranges shelves has an **enlarged** job. A trainee at Dangote moved through production, sales and logistics over a year is on **job rotation**.
- **Negative reinforcement is not punishment.** It **increases** good behaviour by removing something unpleasant, for example excusing a team that meets its targets from a tedious daily reporting requirement, or removing obstacles in their way.
- Reinforcing high performance means **offering praise and rewards** promptly and specifically. Overlooking successes, cutting incentives or giving no feedback weakens the behaviour.

### Lesson 9.6: Motivational strategies

**Must know**

Motivational strategies help employees feel more productive and engaged at work. They include:

1. **Setting goals:** measurable and achievable, linked to the company's objectives.
2. **Providing feedback:** frequent, meaningful feedback that highlights progress.
3. **Rewarding accomplishments:** bonuses, gift cards or public recognition.
4. **Offering career development:** opportunities to learn and advance.
5. **Creating a positive work environment:** inclusive and welcoming, so employees feel a sense of belonging.
6. **Offering flexible work arrangements:** remote work or flexible hours, for work-life balance.

Also: a positive work culture, competitive pay, job security, recognising achievements and aligning personal and company goals build a motivated, high-performing team.

**Explain more**

- Good strategies **adapt to the individual** (a young graduate may value training; a parent may value flexible hours) and are **fair**, which links back to equity theory.
- Strategies that **lower** motivation: slashing perks, ignoring staff needs, piling on workloads, favouritism, delayed or no rewards.
- Each strategy rests on a theory: goals (goal-setting), feedback and rewards (reinforcement, expectancy), career development (Maslow's esteem and self-actualisation, Herzberg's motivators), positive environment (Hawthorne, Maslow's social needs), fairness (equity).

### One-minute recap

- Motivation = a process of stimulating people to accomplish desired goals; it improves involvement, job satisfaction, cooperation and performance.
- Intrinsic (from within) vs extrinsic (rewards); financial (pay, bonus, overtime) vs non-financial (recognition, promotion).
- Taylor: money and piece-rate pay. Hawthorne (Mayo): attention and social factors raise output.
- McGregor: Theory X (control) vs Theory Y (self-direction). Ouchi: Theory Z (Japanese-style, consensus, long-term).
- Maslow: physiological, safety, social, esteem, self-actualisation; physiological is the most basic.
- Herzberg: motivators (job content) vs hygiene/maintenance factors (job context, including salary).
- Equity = perceived fairness of rewards; expectancy = expectancy × instrumentality × valence; goal-setting = specific, challenging, attainable (SMART).
- Job redesign: enrichment (more authority), enlargement (more tasks), rotation (moving between jobs).
- Reinforcement: positive and negative increase behaviour; punishment and extinction decrease it.
- Strategies: goals, feedback, rewards, career development, positive environment, flexible work.
