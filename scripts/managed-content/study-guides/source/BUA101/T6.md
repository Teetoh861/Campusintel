## Topic 6: Entrepreneurship and Small-Business Ownership

**What you must know:** what a small business is and why it matters to the economy; who entrepreneurs are and the entrepreneurial spirit; the start-up phase (three routes to ownership, the business plan); the growth phase and why small businesses fail; financing options (debt and equity, bank loans, microfinance, angel investors, venture capital, crowdfunding, bootstrapping); the franchise alternative (franchisor, franchisee, advantages and disadvantages).

### Lesson 6.1: The big world of small business

**Must know**

- **Small business:** a firm that is **independently owned and operated**, is **not dominant in its field** (market), and is small by number of employees or annual sales. In the United States the usual cut-off is **fewer than 500 employees**.
- **Nigeria (MSMEs):** micro, small and medium enterprises are grouped by size. By employees: **micro** under 10, **small** 10 to 49, **medium** 50 to 199. The government agency that promotes and supports them is **SMEDAN** (Small and Medium Enterprises Development Agency of Nigeria).

**Typical characteristics of a small business:**
1. **Small employee count** (the feature most often examined).
2. Owned and usually **managed by the owner** (owner-manager business).
3. **Limited capital** and resources.
4. **Narrow focus**: few products, often one location or a local market.
5. **Close, personal contact** with customers and staff; quick, flexible decisions.

**Role (importance) of small businesses in the economy:**
1. **Create jobs**: together they employ a very large share of workers.
2. **Drive innovation**: introduce new products, services and ideas.
3. **Give an outlet for entrepreneurs**: a route for people to turn ideas into businesses.
4. **Provide specialist support to larger companies**: as suppliers, distributors and service providers.
5. **Meet specialised needs of consumers** that big firms ignore.
6. **Inject competition**, which keeps prices and quality in check.
7. **Support local economies** and spread income and opportunity.

**Explain more**

- When a question asks why small businesses are important and offers several true reasons plus "all the above", the answer is **all the above**: outlet for entrepreneurs, specialist support to big firms and innovation are all correct.
- **Trap:** small businesses do **not** cut employment, raise tax burdens or curb competition. They **foster jobs and innovation**. "Massive scale", "worldwide reach" and "heavy capital needs" describe large corporations, not small businesses.
- **Lifestyle business vs high-growth venture:** a lifestyle business aims to give its owner a comfortable living and independence (a provision shop, a hair salon, a campus printing stand). A high-growth venture aims to grow quickly into a large firm (many tech start-ups in Yaba, Lagos).
- Most businesses in Nigeria are micro and small enterprises, many of them informal: market traders, tailors, mechanics, food sellers.

### Lesson 6.2: The entrepreneurial spirit

**Must know**

- **Entrepreneur:** a person who **spots a business opportunity** and **assumes the risk** of starting and running a new business, in the hope of profit.
- **Entrepreneurship:** the **process of designing, creating and running a new business**, accepting the risks involved.
- **Entrepreneurial spirit:** the positive, forward-thinking desire to create profitable, innovative business solutions. It involves **creativity, problem-solving and embracing risk**.
- **Intrapreneurship:** showing entrepreneurial behaviour (innovating, taking initiative) **inside an existing organisation** as an employee.

**What entrepreneurship involves:**
1. Spotting a business opportunity.
2. Developing an idea for a business.
3. Satisfying the needs of customers.
4. Taking (calculated) risks and organising resources.

**Common traits of entrepreneurs:**
1. **Risk-taking** (willing to accept calculated risk).
2. **Creativity and innovation**.
3. **Problem-solving** ability.
4. **Self-confidence** and a desire to control their own destiny (independence).
5. **Drive, discipline and hard work**.
6. **Optimism and persistence**; tolerance of failure and uncertainty.
7. **Eagerness to learn** and ability to relate well to people.

**Why people start businesses:** independence (being your own boss), financial rewards, personal satisfaction and achievement, passion for an idea, and lack of (or dissatisfaction with) paid employment.

**Explain more**

- **Who is who:** an **investor** only supplies money; a **manager** runs a business that may belong to someone else; a **stakeholder** is anyone affected by the business. The person who **assumes the risk of starting** the business is the **entrepreneur**.
- **Trap:** "managing large businesses" or "managing a corporation" is **management**, not entrepreneurship. Entrepreneurship is about **creating new** businesses.
- **Trap:** **applying for a job** is **not** associated with entrepreneurship, because it means working for someone else.
- Entrepreneurs are **not** risk-averse and do **not** stick to old ways or ignore market cues. Those are the opposite of the entrepreneurial spirit.
- **Challenges entrepreneurs face:** **navigating risks and unknowns** (uncertainty), limited funds, competition, long hours and heavy personal responsibility. They do not enjoy "unlimited funding" or "zero competition".
- Nigerian examples: Aliko Dangote built the Dangote Group from a trading business; many young founders build fintech firms in Lagos.

### Lesson 6.3: The start-up phase: planning and launching

**Must know**

- **Start-up phase** (also called the **initiation stage**): getting the business off the ground. It includes **idea generation, writing the business plan, raising funds (for example loans and investors) and launching**.
- **3 routes to small-business ownership:**
  1. **Start a new business from scratch** (a start-up).
  2. **Buy an existing business**.
  3. **Buy a franchise**.

| Route | Main advantage | Main disadvantage |
|---|---|---|
| Start from scratch | Full freedom to shape the business | **High failure rate**; no customers or track record |
| Buy an existing business | Customers, suppliers, staff and a track record already exist | Higher purchase cost; you may inherit hidden problems |
| Buy a franchise | Known brand and proven system; support | Fees and royalties; loss of independence |

- **Business plan:** a written document that sets out the business idea, its goals, the strategy for reaching them, and the resources (especially money) needed. It is prepared in the **initiation (start-up) stage**.

**Why a business plan is significant:**
1. **Persuades others** (lenders, investors) **to commit funding**.
2. **Demonstrates the viability** of the venture (that it can succeed).
3. **Guides business activities by defining objectives**.
4. Forces the founder to think through every part of the business and spot problems early.

**Typical contents of a business plan:** executive summary; mission and objectives; company overview; management team; target market and market analysis; competition and competitive advantage; marketing strategy; operations plan; financial plan (start-up costs, projected sales, cash flow, funding needed); critical risks.

**Explain more**

- When "all the above" follows persuading funders, showing viability and guiding activities, choose **all the above**.
- **Trap:** the **high failure rate** is the recognised disadvantage of a start-up compared with buying a business or a franchise. A start-up does not usually need **more** funds than other routes; buying an established business or franchise often costs more.
- **Trap:** global expansion and maintaining growth belong to the **growth** stage; shutting down belongs to closure. A business plan is not written in the expansion, stability or downturn stage.
- Before launch, the founder also chooses a form of ownership and registers it, in Nigeria with the **Corporate Affairs Commission (CAC)**.

### Lesson 6.4: The growth phase and why small businesses fail

**Must know**

- **Growth phase:** **nurturing and sustaining a young business**, that is **building and sustaining progress**. It focuses on **scaling operations, managing cash flow and building a customer base**.
- **Stages of a business (life cycle):** initiation (start-up) → growth (expansion) → maturity (stability) → possible decline (downturn).

**Tasks in the growth phase:**
1. Scale up operations to meet rising demand.
2. Manage cash flow carefully.
3. Build and keep a customer base.
4. Hire staff and **delegate**: the owner can no longer do everything.
5. Seek advice and further finance.

**Why small businesses fail:**
1. **Management incompetence or inexperience**.
2. **Inadequate financing** (undercapitalisation) and **poor cash-flow management**.
3. **Poor planning**, including no proper business plan.
4. **Weak marketing** or not enough customers (poor market research).
5. **Uncontrolled (too rapid) growth**.
6. **Poor location**, poor stock (inventory) control, failure to adapt to change.

**Sources of help and advice:** mentors, advisory boards, networking, business incubators (which give new firms low-cost space and support services) and government agencies such as **SMEDAN** in Nigeria.

**Explain more**

- **Trap:** the growth phase is not about terminating, shrinking or dodging competitors. It is about building on early success.
- A business can fail **while growing**: fast sales growth can use up cash faster than it comes in, so cash-flow management is vital.
- Many owners struggle to move from doing every job themselves to managing others. Learning to delegate is a key growth-phase skill.

### Lesson 6.5: Financing options for small businesses

**Must know**

| | **Debt financing** | **Equity financing** |
|---|---|---|
| Meaning | Borrowing money | Selling part-ownership (shares) in the business |
| Repayment | Must be repaid **with interest** | No repayment |
| Control | Owner keeps ownership | Investors gain ownership and a share of profit, often a say |
| Examples | Bank loans, microfinance loans, supplier credit | Angel investors, venture capital, partners |

**Main financing options:**
1. **Personal savings** and **family and friends**: the most common first source.
2. **Bootstrapping**: funding the business from the owner's own resources and its own revenue, keeping costs low.
3. **Commercial bank loans**: a common source; banks usually want a business plan and collateral.
4. **Microfinance**: small loans to micro and small businesses that banks often turn down. In Nigeria **microfinance banks** are licensed and regulated by the **Central Bank of Nigeria (CBN)**. The **Bank of Industry (BOI)** is a government development bank that also funds small businesses.
5. **Angel investors**: wealthy individuals who invest their **own money** in early-stage businesses in exchange for equity.
6. **Venture capital**: funding from firms (venture capitalists) that invest pooled money in **high-technology, high-risk, perceived high-reward** businesses in exchange for equity, expecting large returns.
7. **Crowdfunding**: raising small amounts from **many people**, usually online.
8. **Seed capital**: the first small sum used to test an idea and set up the business.

**Explain more**

- **Venture capital vs angel investors:** angels are **individuals** using their own money, usually earlier and in smaller sums. Venture capitalists are **firms**, usually investing larger sums at a later stage, and they expect to exit by selling their stake (for example when the company lists on a stock exchange or is bought).
- **Trap:** fixed capital and current (working) capital describe **how** funds are used (long-term assets vs day-to-day running), not a source of funds. Seed capital is the first funding, not the high-risk, high-tech finance; that is **venture capital**.
- **Trap:** tax revenue is government income, staff wages are money owed to workers, and client opinions are feedback. None is a financing option for a small business. **Commercial bank loans** are.
- Informal sources are also common in Nigeria, such as rotating savings groups (esusu or ajo) and trade credit from suppliers.

### Lesson 6.6: The franchise alternative

**Must know**

- **Franchise:** a business arrangement in which one party (the **franchisor**) allows another (the **franchisee**) to **use its business name, brand and system to sell its products or services**, in return for fees. In short, **a licensed brand operation**.
- **Franchisor:** the company that owns the brand and system and grants the right.
- **Franchisee:** the person or firm that buys the right and runs the outlet.
- **Franchise agreement:** the contract setting out rights, duties, fees and rules. The franchisee usually pays an **initial franchise fee** plus continuing **royalties** (a share of sales).
- **2 types:** (1) **product (trade-name) franchise**: the right to sell the franchisor's products, e.g. car dealerships and soft-drink bottlers; (2) **business-format franchise**: the whole way of running the business (name, layout, methods, training), e.g. fast-food chains.

| Advantages to the franchisee | Disadvantages to the franchisee |
|---|---|
| **Pre-existing brand fame** (known name attracts customers) | **High start-up cost** (franchise fee, equipment) |
| Proven business system; **lower risk of failure** | Continuing **royalties** reduce profit |
| **Training and support** from the franchisor | **Loss of independence**: strict rules on products, prices, layout |
| National advertising and group buying power | Franchisor's problems or bad publicity hurt every outlet |
| Easier to obtain finance | Agreement may limit selling the outlet and can be ended |

- **Advantages to the franchisor:** rapid expansion using franchisees' money, steady fee and royalty income, motivated owner-managers at each outlet.

**Explain more**

- **Trap:** high risk, minimal franchisor support and steep initial investment are **not** advantages. The headline advantage is **pre-existing brand fame**.
- **Trap:** a franchise is not a state-owned operation (that is a public enterprise), a merger, a cooperative (owned by its members) or a solo-owner venture (sole proprietorship).
- Franchising is also a way to enter foreign markets (Topic 3). In Nigeria, the Nigerian Bottling Company bottles Coca-Cola products, an example of a product franchise, and international fast-food brands operate here through franchise arrangements.

### One-minute recap

- Small business = independently owned, not dominant in its field, small employee count; Nigeria groups MSMEs by size and supports them through SMEDAN.
- Small businesses create jobs, drive innovation, give entrepreneurs an outlet and support large firms.
- Entrepreneur = assumes the risk of starting a business; entrepreneurship = designing and creating new businesses.
- Entrepreneurial spirit = creativity, problem-solving and embracing risk; applying for a job is not entrepreneurship.
- 3 routes: start from scratch (high failure rate), buy an existing business, buy a franchise.
- Business plan, written at the initiation stage: persuades funders, shows viability, guides by objectives.
- Growth phase = building and sustaining progress: scaling, cash flow, customers.
- Failure causes: poor management, too little finance, poor planning, weak marketing, uncontrolled growth.
- Debt is repaid with interest; equity sells ownership. Venture capital funds high-tech, high-risk, high-reward projects.
- Franchise = licensed brand operation; key advantage is brand fame, key drawbacks are fees, royalties and lost independence.
