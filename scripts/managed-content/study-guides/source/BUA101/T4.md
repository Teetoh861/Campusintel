## Topic 4: Business Ethics and Corporate Social Responsibility

**What you must know:** what ethics and business ethics mean; unethical behaviour and what shapes behaviour; ethical dilemmas versus ethical lapses and how to make ethical decisions; CSR and its four perspectives; CSR towards the natural environment, consumers, employees and the community.

### Lesson 4.1: Ethics and ethical behaviour in business

**Must know**

- **Ethics:** the rules or standards of right and wrong that govern the conduct of a person or a group.
- **Business ethics:** the standards of right and wrong (moral principles such as **honesty** and **fairness**) that guide the decisions and behaviour of a business and its people.
- **Ethical company:** one that holds itself to standards that consider the needs of all its **stakeholders**, not only its owners.
- **Stakeholders:** groups affected by, or able to affect, a business: owners/shareholders, employees, customers, suppliers, the community, government and society at large.

**Three marks of unethical behaviour:**

1. **Competing unfairly and dishonestly:** bribery, fixing prices with rivals, stealing trade secrets, **insider trading** (using confidential company information to buy or sell shares).
2. **Communicating untruthfully:** false or misleading advertising, hiding product defects, falsifying financial records.
3. **Causing harm to others:** selling unsafe products, polluting, exploiting workers, cheating investors.

**Factors that influence ethical behaviour:**

1. **Cultural differences:** what is acceptable (gifts, payments, hiring relatives) varies between countries and communities.
2. **Knowledge:** people sometimes act wrongly because they do not know the facts, the law or the consequences.
3. **Organisational behaviour:** the company's culture, leadership example, rewards and pressures.

**Tools firms use to promote ethics:**

- **Code of ethics:** a written statement of the principles that guide an organisation's decisions.
- **Ethics training** and ethics officers or hotlines.
- **Whistle-blowing:** disclosure by an insider of illegal or unethical conduct by others in the organisation.

**Benefits of ethical practice:** enhanced **brand reputation**, customer trust and loyalty, higher staff morale and retention, investor confidence, fewer fines and lawsuits.

**Explain more**

- **Legal is not the same as ethical.** The law sets the minimum. An act can be legal yet unethical (charging desperate customers outrageous prices during a fuel scarcity), so ethics usually asks more than the law does.
- Top managers **set the tone**. A strong code of ethics means little if leaders reward results "by any means". In companies, the board of directors also sets the tone for ethical behaviour and can remove managers who behave unethically.
- Unethical behaviour produces the opposite of the benefits: eroded consumer confidence, increased litigation costs, lower staff morale, regulatory sanctions and a damaged name.
- **Trap:** "profit pursuit above all", "neglecting worker rights" and "bypassing legal oversight" describe unethical conduct; they are never definitions of business ethics.

### Lesson 4.2: Ethical dilemmas, ethical lapses and making ethical decisions

**Must know**

| | Ethical dilemma | Ethical lapse |
|---|---|---|
| Meaning | A situation where **more than one side** of an issue can be supported with **valid arguments** | A situation where an individual or group makes a decision that is clearly **wrong, illegal or unethical** |
| Is the right answer clear? | No; it is a choice between competing goods or interests | Yes; the person knows (or should know) it is wrong |
| Example | Closing a loss-making plant to save the company, which costs local jobs | Inflating expense claims; selling expired drugs |

**Four questions for making an ethical decision:**

1. **Is it legal?** Does it break any law or company policy?
2. **Is it balanced?** Is it fair to everyone affected in the short and long term?
3. **Can you live with it?** Would you be comfortable if your family or the newspapers knew about it?
4. **Is it feasible?** Can it actually work in the real world?

**Common ways of judging right and wrong:**

- **Utilitarianism:** choose the action that gives the greatest good to the greatest number of people.
- **Individual rights:** respect people's basic rights (life, privacy, free speech, fair treatment).
- **Justice:** treat people fairly and consistently, distributing benefits and burdens fairly.

**Explain more**

- In a **dilemma**, both choices can be defended: a manager may be torn between loyalty to a long-serving supplier and a cheaper new one. The difficulty is not knowing the rule but weighing interests.
- In a **lapse**, the right choice is obvious and the person simply fails to make it, often under pressure to meet targets.
- **Trap:** exams swap the two definitions. "Valid arguments on more than one side" = **dilemma**. "Clearly wrong decision" = **lapse**.
- True professionals avoid committing ethical lapses and carefully weigh all the options when facing ethical dilemmas.

### Lesson 4.3: Corporate social responsibility and its perspectives

**Must know**

- **Corporate social responsibility (CSR):** the idea that a business has obligations to society **beyond** its obligation to its owners or shareholders; a **duty to contribute to the well-being of society**, not only to make profit.
- CSR covers four broad groups: the **natural environment**, **consumers**, **employees** and the **community** (plus investors).

**Four perspectives on CSR:**

| Perspective | Core view |
|---|---|
| **1. Minimalist** | A firm's only social responsibility is to make **profits for its owners within the law** (obey laws, pay taxes). |
| **2. Defensive** | Firms engage in CSR mainly to **protect themselves**: to avoid criticism, boycotts or tougher regulation. |
| **3. Cynical** | CSR is a **public-relations or marketing tool**; firms adopt it only because it pays, often doing little real good. |
| **4. Proactive** | CSR is a **moral obligation**; businesses have a duty to contribute to society because it is right, and see it as part of doing business. |

- **Philanthropy:** donating money, staff time or other resources to social causes **without expecting a direct business benefit**.
- **Strategic CSR:** social contributions **aligned with the company's overall business strategy**, so that both society and the company benefit.

**Carroll's four responsibilities** (bottom to top of the pyramid):

1. **Economic:** be profitable (the base on which everything else rests).
2. **Legal:** obey the law.
3. **Ethical:** do what is right and fair, beyond what the law requires.
4. **Philanthropic:** be a good corporate citizen; give back to the community.

**Explain more**

- The **minimalist** view is the **economic perspective**, linked with the economist Milton Friedman: the business of business is to make profit, staying within the rules. Note that it does **not** say "ignore society" or "exclude stakeholders"; it says the firm serves society by making lawful profit, paying taxes and providing jobs.
- The **proactive** view is the **duty-based (ethical) stance**: CSR is done because it is morally right. A **compliance-focused** lens matches the defensive view; a **profit-driven** lens matches the minimalist or cynical view; a **charitable-giving** angle is philanthropy only.
- **Greenwashing** (exaggerating how environmentally friendly a firm is) is typical of the cynical view.
- **Strategic CSR example:** a telecoms company funding digital-skills training for young people builds future customers and staff while helping society. **Philanthropy example:** a bank donating to flood victims with no link to its business.
- **CSR towards the community** includes backing local nonprofits and charities, funding schools, scholarships, health centres and roads, employing local people and buying from local suppliers. Many large Nigerian firms, such as Dangote, MTN and the banks, run foundations for this purpose.
- Remember the positive role of business in society: offering valuable goods and services, providing employment, paying taxes and contributing to national growth, stability and security. Its potential negative effects: generating pollution and waste, creating health and safety risks, disrupting communities and causing financial instability. CSR aims to increase the first and reduce the second.

### Lesson 4.4: CSR towards the natural environment

**Must know**

- **Pollution:** damage to the air, water or land caused by waste, chemicals, smoke, noise and other harmful releases.
  1. **Air pollution:** factory and generator fumes, vehicle exhaust, **gas flaring**.
  2. **Water pollution:** oil spills, untreated industrial waste discharged into rivers and lagoons.
  3. **Land pollution:** dumping of solid and toxic waste, plastics.
- **Carbon footprint:** the total greenhouse gases (mainly carbon dioxide) produced by an organisation's activities.
- **Sustainability (sustainable development):** meeting the needs of the present **without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own needs**.

**Responsible environmental actions:**

1. **Lowering carbon footprints** (cleaner energy, efficient machines and transport).
2. Proper **waste management**: reduce, reuse, recycle.
3. **Conserving natural resources** (water, forests, energy) rather than depleting them.
4. **Obeying environmental regulations** and cleaning up damage caused.
5. Designing greener products and packaging.

**Explain more**

- In Nigeria, the **National Environmental Standards and Regulations Enforcement Agency (NESREA)** enforces environmental standards, and oil spills and gas flaring in the Niger Delta are the best-known local examples of corporate environmental harm.
- **Trade-offs:** going green can raise costs in the short run, which is why some firms resist. Proactive firms see sustainability as a long-term saving and a source of reputation.
- **Trap:** "boosting emissions", "disregarding regulations" and "depleting natural reserves" are the opposite of environmental CSR. The correct answer is always the one that **reduces harm**, such as lowering carbon footprints.

### Lesson 4.5: CSR towards consumers and employees

**Must know**

**Consumer rights** (first set out by US President John F. Kennedy in 1962):

1. **The right to safety:** to buy products that are safe to use.
2. **The right to be informed:** to honest, clear information (labels, prices, ingredients, risks).
3. **The right to choose:** to choose freely among competing products at fair prices.
4. **The right to be heard:** to have complaints listened to and handled.

Later additions: the right to **redress** (compensation for faulty goods), to **consumer education**, to **privacy** of personal data and to a **healthy environment**.

- **CSR towards consumers** means **ensuring product safety and clarity**: safe products, honest advertising, clear labels, fair pricing, good quality and listening to customer complaints.

**CSR towards employees:**

1. **Equality:** equal opportunity in hiring, pay and promotion regardless of sex, religion, ethnicity or disability; no discrimination or harassment.
2. **Fair (equitable) pay** and benefits.
3. **Occupational safety and health:** a safe, healthy workplace, protective equipment and training.
4. Respect for **workers' rights**, reasonable hours and paid overtime.
5. Training and career development.

**Explain more**

- Nigerian consumer bodies: the **Federal Competition and Consumer Protection Commission (FCCPC)**, set up under the Federal Competition and Consumer Protection Act 2018; **NAFDAC** (food and drugs); the **Standards Organisation of Nigeria (SON)** (product standards).
- Breaching consumer CSR: selling fake or expired drugs, short-measuring goods, hidden charges, ignoring complaints, lowering quality while keeping the price.
- **Equality** at work also means valuing **diversity**: a workforce of different backgrounds, ages, sexes and abilities, with reasonable accommodation for people with disabilities.
- **Safety:** construction, mining, manufacturing and oil firms carry the highest risks. Skimping on safety measures, overlooking worker rights and extending unpaid hours are clear failures of CSR towards employees; **offering equitable pay** is a clear example of meeting it.
- **Trap:** fair treatment of employees is **not** charity; it is part of a firm's basic ethical and legal responsibility.

### One-minute recap

- Business ethics = standards of right and wrong (honesty, fairness) guiding business conduct.
- Unethical behaviour: competing unfairly, communicating untruthfully, causing harm. Influences: culture, knowledge, organisational behaviour.
- Ethical behaviour builds brand reputation, trust and morale; code of ethics and whistle-blowing support it.
- Dilemma = valid arguments on more than one side; lapse = a clearly wrong decision.
- Four questions: legal? balanced? can you live with it? feasible?
- CSR = duty to society's well-being beyond profit. Perspectives: minimalist (profit within the law), defensive, cynical, proactive (moral obligation).
- Philanthropy (giving, no direct benefit) vs strategic CSR (aligned with business strategy).
- Environment: cut pollution and carbon footprint, manage waste, practise sustainability.
- Consumers: safety, information, choice, being heard. Employees: equality, fair pay, safety. Community: support local causes.
